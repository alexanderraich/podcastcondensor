In this episode, Father Andrew and Father Stephen take us on a deep dive into the history and theology of the Samaritans. They start by tracing the origins of this people group all the way back to the division of the united monarchy of Israel. Contrary to what many people think, that united kingdom only lasted about a hundred years, from the reign of Saul to the split after Solomon. The hosts make a fascinating point about Solomon’s downfall, arguing that his main sin was not simply sexual immorality, but making treaty alliances with pagan nations through marriage. These political alliances led to idolatry and ultimately tore the kingdom in two.

After the split, the northern kingdom, often called Ephraim after its most powerful tribe, was ruled by kings like Jeroboam, who set up golden calves at Dan and Bethel. The biblical narrative in the second book of Kings, chapter seventeen, describes how the Assyrians eventually conquered this northern kingdom and scattered its people. They then resettled foreigners into the land of Samaria. These foreigners intermingled with the Israelites who were left behind, and this mixing produced the Samaritans as a distinct people. They developed their own version of the Torah, known as the Samaritan Pentateuch, and built their own temple on Mount Gerizim, near Shechem. This created a bitter rivalry with the Jews over who were the true heirs of Israel.

Father Andrew and Father Stephen point out that the Samaritan Pentateuch is not simply a corrupted text. It shares about two thousand variants with the Greek translation of the Old Testament, the Septuagint, against the standard Hebrew text. This suggests it preserves an independent and ancient Hebrew tradition. The enmity between the two groups only grew, as we see in the book of Ezra, when the Samaritans wrote to the Persian king to stop the rebuilding of Jerusalem. Later, the Hasmonean ruler John Hyrcanus conquered Samaria and destroyed their temple on Mount Gerizim around one hundred and fifty years before Christ, deepening the wound even further.

But the story does not end there. The prophets Jeremiah and Ezekiel spoke of Ephraim’s restoration. In Jeremiah, Rachel weeps for her children, but God promises mercy. In Ezekiel, the sticks of Judah and Ephraim are joined together, symbolizing the reunification of Israel under the Messiah. The hosts explain that this prophecy is fulfilled in the inclusion of the Gentiles into the Church. They connect this to Jacob’s blessing in Genesis, chapter forty-eight, where he prophesies that Ephraim’s offspring would become the fullness of the Gentiles. This is the concept of Ephraim representing the scattered nations who are brought back into the people of God.

In the New Testament, Samaria serves as a crucial pivot point. Jesus’ encounter with the Samaritan woman at the well, who is venerated as Saint Photini in the Orthodox Church, shows the gospel breaking down barriers. And in the first chapter of Acts, Jesus tells his disciples they will be his witnesses in Jerusalem, Judea, Samaria, and to the ends of the earth. This was not a random list. Samaria was a deliberate step in the mission, symbolizing the reunification of Israel. When Philip preaches in Samaria in the eighth chapter of Acts, and Peter and John come to lay hands on the new believers, we see the Samaritans being accepted fully into the Church.

The hosts conclude by reflecting on the pattern of death and resurrection woven throughout this history. Samaria was emptied and refilled, disciplined and restored. This same pattern applies to the Church and to each individual believer. Through death and resurrection, God brings about redemption and transformation. The story of Ephraim and the Samaritans is ultimately a symbol of how the Gentiles are grafted into the people of God, and a reminder that God can bring life out of even the most broken and divided history.
