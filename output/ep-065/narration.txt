In this episode, Father Andrew Stephen Damick and Father Stephen De Young take on a big question: what is the soul, really? They start where the Bible starts, in Genesis, chapter two, verse seven. There, we read that God formed Adam from the dust of the ground and breathed into his nostrils the breath of life, and Adam became a living soul. The key is that Adam didn't receive a soul. He became one. The soul is not a separate thing that gets put into a body. It is the life of the living being itself.

The Hebrew word here is nephesh, and the Greek word is psyche. Both of these words carry a sense of life, but also desire and appetite. So the soul is not some abstract, floating thing. It is the embodied life of a person, with all of its hungers and longings. The fathers make a careful distinction between soul and spirit. The soul is the life of the body, while the spirit is the organizing principle of a being. They are not two separate things stacked inside you. They are aspects of the same living reality.

This is where the episode takes a sharp turn against a very famous Greek philosopher, Plato. Plato taught that the body is a prison for the soul, and that the soul is a self-contained, immortal thing that just uses the body like a vehicle. The fathers say that is simply not the biblical view. The body is not a prison. It is integral to who you are. Your body is part of your identity, and the soul is not the whole self. That is why the bodily resurrection matters so much. It is not a bonus. It is the restoration of the whole person.

The hosts also talk about what eternal life actually means. It is not just living forever. In John, chapter seventeen, verse three, Jesus defines eternal life as knowing God and knowing Jesus Christ. So eternal life is a quality of relationship, not a quantity of time. It is about being connected to God. And the soul, in a real sense, is that connection. Through the soul, a living being receives both physical and spiritual life from God.

They also get into the question of death. There is physical death, and there is spiritual death. Saint John of Damascus makes this distinction, and the fathers explain it using John, chapter eleven, where Jesus says that whoever believes in him will never die. That is not saying believers won't physically die. It is saying that spiritual death, which is separation from God, is overcome. The intermediate state, the time between physical death and the resurrection, is a time when the soul remains either connected to God or separated from him.

The conversation then moves to Christ himself. They discuss the fact that Christ has two natural wills, a human will and a divine will. This is essential for salvation. If Christ did not have a human soul, then he could not save our human souls. This is the heresy of Apollinarianism, and Saint Gregory the Theologian is quoted as saying that what is not assumed is not healed. Saint Maximus the Confessor is the great defender of the two wills of Christ, and they point to Gethsemane, where Jesus prays, not my will but yours be done, as proof that he had a genuine human will.

Finally, they talk about how spirits can organize not just individual people, but whole communities. Angels are called spirits in Psalm one hundred four, and they serve as organizing principles. The saints, through their connection to God in Christ, also become organizing principles for larger groups. John the Forerunner came in the spirit and power of Elijah. Elijah's life became a kind of pattern that organized John's life. And this can go the other way too. Mammon is a spirit of wealth and reputation that can organize your life, and it leads to dehumanization.

So the big takeaway is that your identity is not something you find inside yourself. It is formed through relationships, choices, and experiences. You have to deliberately choose which spirit will organize your life. Will it be the Spirit of God, or will it be something else? The soul is not a thing you have. It is the life you live, and it is found outside yourself, in connection to God and to others.
