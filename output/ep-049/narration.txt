In this episode, Father Andrew and Father Stephen take on the thunder gods. They start with Baal, the great Canaanite storm god, also known as Hadad. They walk through his origins and his role in the ancient Baal Cycle, a text that tells of his rise to power. The hosts are careful to point out that when Baal appears in the Old Testament, especially in the stories of Elijah and the prophets, the point is never that Baal is Yahweh. Instead, the Bible takes the language of the storm god and uses it to declare that Yahweh is superior. Baal is not God, and the prophets make that clear.

From there, they move to Zeus. Zeus has deep Indo-European roots, but his worship absorbed a lot from the Near East. In antiquity, people often identified Zeus and Baal as the same deity. Alexander the Great even reconsecrated Baal shrines to Zeus. The hosts also talk about Zeus in Greek mythology, where he is a shapeshifter and, frankly, a rapist. This is a stark contrast to the God of Israel, who is holy and faithful.

Then they turn to Thor, the Norse god of thunder. A key point here is that almost everything we know about Thor and Norse mythology comes from Christian sources, written centuries after the conversion. Snorri Sturluson, a thirteenth-century Icelandic Christian, wrote the Prose Edda, and that is a major source. So the hosts caution that these stories may not accurately reflect what pagans actually believed. They still explore Thor’s tales, like his fishing expedition where he nearly catches the Midgard Serpent, and the events of Ragnarok, where Thor fights the serpent and both die.

Throughout the episode, the hosts keep coming back to a central argument. The pagan gods are tied to storms, lightning, and fertility, but Yahweh is distinct and superior. The Bible often uses storm imagery, but it does so to assert His supremacy. For example, Psalm twenty-four speaks of the King of Glory entering the gates. This echoes Baal’s enthronement, but in Christian liturgy, it is applied to Christ’s Ascension. Similarly, Daniel chapter seven describes the Son of Man coming on the clouds. This is a corrective rewriting of Baal’s cloud-rider image. And in Job chapter forty-one, Leviathan is drawn up with a hook. This parallels Thor’s fishing trip, but in Scripture, it shows God’s power over chaos, not a god’s struggle against it.

The hosts also bring in the cultural context from Acts chapter fourteen, where Paul and Barnabas are mistaken for Zeus and Hermes in Lystra. That shows just how present these gods were in the everyday world of the New Testament.

Finally, they address a scholarly theory that Yahweh was originally just a storm god in a Canaanite pantheon. Father Andrew and Father Stephen argue that this view has no evidence behind it. They say it is based on flawed presuppositions, not on the text of Scripture itself. They also touch on the idea of hypostasis, where a local manifestation of a deity was worshipped, like Baal of Peor. And they mention theophoric names, names that include a god’s name, such as Jezebel, which includes Baal, or Ben-Hadad, which includes Hadad.

In the end, the episode is a sweeping look at how the ancient world understood thunder and power, and how the Bible takes that language and turns it on its head. The God who thundered on Mount Sinai is not one god among many. He is the Lord of all, and the storm is simply His servant.
