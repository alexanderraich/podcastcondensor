Father Andrew Stephen Damick and Father Stephen De Young close out their ten-part series on the sacraments of the Orthodox Church with a deep dive into blood and water. They start in the Old Testament, tracing what blood actually means. From the very beginning, Abel’s blood cries out from the ground to God, demanding justice. That image sets the tone. Blood is not just a substance; it is the life of a creature. After the flood, God forbids eating blood because it is the life itself. And in the sacrificial system, blood is used to purify and consecrate. It is sprinkled on people, on objects, on the altar. The idea is that life, which is blood, overcomes death and the residue of sin. This is why the Day of Atonement involves blood in such a central way. The shedding of blood brings purification and transformation out of death. It is a strange and powerful paradox, but it runs through the whole Torah.

Then they turn to water. Water in Scripture has two faces. On one hand, it is chaos. It is the deep, the flood, the destructive force that wipes out everything. The ancient world even had gods of the sea and the river, like Yam and Nahar, who represented that primordial chaos. On the other hand, water is also life. It is the spring in the desert, the ritual washings that cleanse, the water that Moses brought from the rock. So water is both a threat and a gift. And this ambivalence is resolved in Christ. When Jesus is baptized in the Jordan, He does not need to be cleansed. Instead, He purifies the waters themselves. He steps into the chaos and transforms it into life. That is why the Orthodox Church blesses water so solemnly at Theophany. The Great Blessing of Waters invokes Christ’s baptism and sanctifies the water, so that it can be used to bless homes, people, and objects. The prayer even mentions deliverance from the deception of Baal, connecting the purification of water with the defeat of idolatry. In the Theophany icon, you can see the old chaos deities subdued under Christ’s feet.

Father Andrew and Father Stephen also connect this to the broader story of creation. The material world was subjected to the curse and to the power of the evil one. But Christ’s incarnation and His baptism begin the purification of all matter. By touching unclean things, He cleanses them. His blood and water, poured out from His side on the cross, become the source of the sacraments. That moment in John’s Gospel, where blood and water flow from Christ’s pierced side, is the Church’s foundation for both Baptism and the Eucharist. Blood and water come together. Life and purification are joined.

The priests also point to a passage from Ezekiel, where God promises to sprinkle clean water on Israel to cleanse them from idolatry and give them a new heart and a new spirit. That promise is fulfilled in the sacraments. And they mention the ritual for cleansing a house from mildew in Leviticus, which uses birds, fresh water, and blood. That might sound strange, but it is a biblical basis for blessing homes. The pattern is consistent. Blood and water are the instruments of purification and renewal.

Finally, they reflect on the Eucharist itself. The sacraments are not mere rituals. They are transformative encounters with God’s grace. They purify, renew, and even exorcise. But they require active participation and repentance. You cannot just go through the motions. The blood and water that flow from Christ are meant to change you. And this is the heart of the Orthodox understanding of the sacraments. They are not symbols only. They are real encounters with the living God, who takes the chaos of our lives and turns it into life.
