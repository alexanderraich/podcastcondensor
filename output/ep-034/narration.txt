This episode of the Lord of Spirits is an open-line Q&A, and Fathers Andrew and Stephen tackle a wonderfully wide range of questions from their listeners. They dive into everything from ancient Egyptian onion worship to the nature of demons, marriage in heaven, and even UFOs and Sasquatch. Throughout it all, they keep coming back to a few key principles: understanding words by how they’re actually used rather than their etymology, recognizing the spiritual reality of demons as cosmic intelligences, and seeing salvation as a transformative process rather than a single transaction.

One of the first questions concerns the ancient Egyptians and their reverence for onions. The Fathers confirm that this was a real practice, citing St. John Chrysostom and Roman writers. The Egyptians saw onions as symbols of eternity, used them in mummification, and may have even offered them as sacrifices. It sounds strange to us, but it points to the deeply spiritual nature of ancient pagan worship.

A caller then asks about the famous passage in John 21, where Jesus asks Peter if he loves him. The Greek words phileo and agape are used, and many people have tried to draw a sharp distinction between them, suggesting one is a higher form of love than the other. Father Andrew and Father Stephen push back on this. They show that St. John uses these words interchangeably throughout his Gospel, so in this conversation, they are simply synonyms. The point isn’t a technical distinction between kinds of love, but the repeated, heartfelt question and Peter’s response.

The conversation then turns to the demonic. A listener asks about the hierarchy of demons. The Fathers explain that demons do not have the ordered ranks that angels have. Angels are organized because they participate in God’s works, but demons have turned away from that. They can cooperate for common goals, but they lack a true military structure. They are more like a chaotic mob than a disciplined army.

Another question concerns marriage in the resurrection. The Fathers reference Matthew 22, where Christ says that in the resurrection, people neither marry nor are given in marriage. They clarify that this means there will be no weddings, no new marriages for the purpose of procreation. But the spiritual intimacy and love that a husband and wife have built over a lifetime does not vanish. It endures, transformed and perfected, in the life of the world to come.

They also address a difficult passage from 1 Peter 3, where Christ is said to have preached to the spirits in prison. Some have wondered if this means the dead get a second chance. The Fathers, drawing on the Book of Enoch, identify these spirits as the fallen angels, the Watchers, from the time of Noah. Christ’s preaching to them was not an offer of salvation, but a proclamation of their doom. St. Augustine himself struggled with this passage, but the Fathers are clear: this is the harrowing of Hades, Christ’s victory over the powers of darkness.

The conversation moves to Pharaoh’s hardened heart. Why would God harden someone’s heart and then punish him for it? The Fathers explain that this was an act of justice against the gods of Egypt. Pharaoh’s own hardness came first, and God’s action was a judgment, not a prevention of repentance. It’s also a reminder that God’s foreknowledge does not negate our free will. God is not bound by time like we are, so His knowledge of what we will do is not the same as Him forcing us to do it.

Finally, they clarify the relationship between Hades, Sheol, and hell. These terms are roughly synonymous, all referring to the underworld, the place of the dead. Individual authors may use them in technical ways, but in general, they point to the same reality. And this connects to the practice of praying for the departed. We do not know the final state of anyone, and our prayers for the dead are a means by which God works salvation. His mercy is not limited by our understanding of time.
