This is the hundredth episode of the Lord of Spirits podcast, and Fathers Andrew Stephen Damick and Stephen De Young are marking the occasion by looking back at the big picture. They’re exploring the theme of continuity and change in the Christian faith, building on their last conversation about how the worship of the true God has remained unbroken from the Stone Age through the Axial Age. Tonight, they’re focusing on a crucial link in that chain: the relationship between Second Temple Judaism and the early Church.

Their central argument is that Christianity is not a new religion that replaced an old one. Instead, it is a direct continuation of Second Temple Judaism. In fact, they make the bold claim that Christianity is essentially a variation of Pharisaism. The shift from the temple to the synagogue, the adaptation of liturgical forms, and even the development of the New Testament canon all happened within an overarching continuity. They point out that Second Temple Judaism was incredibly diverse, with temples operating in places like Leontopolis in Egypt and possibly even on Elephantine Island, alongside the main temple in Jerusalem. So the early Christian movement, with its own gatherings and practices, fits right into that diverse landscape.

This paradigm helps them evaluate other belief systems. They make a clear distinction between a heresy, which is a wrong belief within Christianity, and an entirely different religion. Arianism, for example, which denied the full divinity of Christ and was popular among the Goths, is a heresy because it still operates within the Christian scriptures and worship. But Gnosticism, which rejected the Old Testament and the goodness of creation, is something else entirely. It’s a different religion that borrows Christian elements. The same goes for Neoplatonism, which they describe as a pagan philosophy that borrowed Christian ideas to compete with the Church. St. Irenaeus, who coined the term Gnostic, was fighting this battle in the second century.

The conversation also touches on the nature of scripture itself. They argue that the Protestant idea of biblical infallibility is logically incoherent because a text cannot exercise authority on its own. It always requires interpretation by fallible human beings. They also introduce the idea of primary and secondary scriptures. The Torah and the Gospels hold a primary place, while the Prophets and the Epistles are secondary, serving to interpret the primary texts. St. Paul’s writings, for instance, are secondary scriptures that interpret the Gospels.

They take listener calls on a few topics, including the harrowing of Hades. They discuss how Christ descended into the realm of the dead after His crucifixion to liberate the righteous dead, citing Psalm twenty-four and the first epistle of Peter, chapter three. They also address the Roman Catholic concept of the state of grace, contrasting it with the Orthodox understanding of salvation.

One of the most striking points they make is about the availability of salvation. They insist that it is possible for anyone who has ever lived to find salvation, regardless of time or place, because God’s grace is available to all who respond faithfully to the revelation they have been given. They point to the Ninevites from the book of Jonah. These were demon-worshipping pagans, yet they repented, and Christ Himself honored them as an example. This shows that faithfulness is not limited to those with perfect doctrine.

They even bring up St. Isaac the Syrian, a saint venerated in Orthodoxy who belonged to the Assyrian Church of the East, a heretical body. This shows that while heretical groups may lack the full power of Orthodox Christianity for salvation, God can still save individuals within them. The episode concludes with practical advice for modern Christians. The emphasis is on obedience and community over intellectual correctness. The continuity of the faith is not just an academic idea; it’s a call to live faithfully in the same tradition that stretches back through the ages, trusting in the God who has always been at work.
