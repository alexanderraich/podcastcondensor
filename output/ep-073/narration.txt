Father Andrew Stephen Damick and Father Stephen DeYoung open this episode with a question that sounds almost comical at first: what in tarnation? But they quickly get to the heart of a very serious subject. They are talking about the nature of eternal condemnation, and they want to clear up a lot of confusion. The first thing they do is make a careful distinction between Hades and what we often call hell. Hades is the intermediate state, the place where the dead reside in this age, between their physical death and the general resurrection. It is not the final destination for anyone. The final state of the wicked is something else entirely, and the Church has a clear teaching on it.

The hosts stress that Orthodoxy means right opinion. There cannot be multiple conflicting correct opinions on essential doctrines. So when it comes to the question of whether everyone will be saved, they insist that the Church has spoken. They take aim at universalism, the belief that all rational beings, even demons, will eventually be reconciled to God. This idea, known as apokatastasis, was condemned at the Fifth Ecumenical Council in 553 AD. They mention Origen, whose views were condemned there, and they also bring up more recent proponents like David Bentley Hart. Father Andrew and Father Stephen argue that universalism is not just a harmless hope, but a heresy with serious consequences.

One of the key points they make is about repentance. Repentance is not merely feeling guilty or regretful. It is actively repairing the damage caused by sin. And this is crucial: repentance requires a body. It requires actions in the world. That is why it is impossible for demons and for the dead. This life is the only time for repentance. Once you die, your state is fixed. This is why prayers for the departed are so important. They are not effective because the dead can repent on their own. They are effective because they are acts of repentance offered on their behalf by the living.

The hosts then walk through the biblical images for eternal condemnation. There is the lake of fire, which they explain is not a literal place of fire but a metaphor for the experience of God’s presence by the wicked. There is outer darkness, associated with weeping and gnashing of teeth, which they say symbolizes madness and terror. And there is the second death, which is eternal spiritual death, the separation of the soul from God. They read from Matthew, chapter twenty-five, where Jesus speaks of the eternal fire prepared for the devil and his angels, and then in verse forty-six, where he draws a direct parallel between eternal punishment and eternal life. Both are equally ultimate. They also turn to John, chapter five, where the resurrection of the dead leads either to a resurrection of life or a resurrection of condemnation. And in Revelation, chapter twenty, death and Hades are thrown into the lake of fire, which is the second death.

Father Andrew and Father Stephen address the common objection that a loving God would not send anyone to eternal torment. They argue that God is not obligated to save everyone. Universalism wrongly subjects God to necessity, as if he must save all. They also say that universalism erases the significance of history and personal choices. If everyone is saved no matter what, then repentance becomes ultimately meaningless. They bring in St. John of Damascus, an eighth-century theologian, whose writings summarize earlier patristic teaching on repentance and death. They also mention St. Gregory of Nyssa and St. Isaac the Syrian, who are sometimes claimed by universalists, but the hosts argue that their writings do not actually support the heresy. They even bring up Theodore Abu Qurra, a medieval Arabic Orthodox bishop who wrote against Origenism.

The hosts acknowledge that this doctrine is hard. It is difficult to hear. But they offer pastoral guidance for those who struggle with it. They point to Deuteronomy, chapter thirty, where Moses sets before Israel life and death, blessing and curse, and urges them to choose life. The possibility of eternal condemnation is real, and it is precisely that possibility that motivates repentance and faithfulness. It is not meant to be a source of despair, but a call to action. The teaching of the Church is clear, and it is a teaching that takes human freedom and God’s justice seriously.
