In this episode of the Lord of Spirits, Fathers Andrew Stephen Damick and Stephen De Young explore the deep biblical and theological roots of Great Lent. They want to correct a common misunderstanding: Lent is not a medieval invention. It is firmly grounded in Scripture, where the number forty shows up again and again as a time of preparation, anticipation, and repentance.

Think about the flood. It rained for forty days and forty nights, a kind of uncreation of the world that prepared the way for a new creation. Jacob’s body was prepared for burial over forty days. Moses spent forty days on Mount Sinai, twice, fasting and interceding for Israel. The spies spent forty days exploring the land of Canaan. Israel wandered for forty years in the wilderness, a long period of purification before entering the Promised Land. Elijah journeyed forty days to Mount Horeb to encounter God. Jonah gave Nineveh forty days to repent. And of course, Jesus himself fasted for forty days in the wilderness, recapitulating Israel’s testing and Moses’ fasting. Even after the Resurrection, Jesus appeared to his disciples for forty days, preparing them for what was to come.

So the number forty is not arbitrary. It signals that something significant is about to happen. It is a time of waiting, of purification, of getting ready.

The fathers then connect this directly to baptism. In the early Church, the forty-day fast of Lent was originally a period of preparation for those who were going to be baptized at Pascha. And baptism itself is not the end of the journey. It is the beginning. It is a death to the old man, to Adam, and a resurrection to new life in Christ. Saint Paul makes this clear in Romans chapter six, where he says that we are buried with Christ in baptism so that we might walk in newness of life. Baptism prepares us to receive the Holy Spirit. That is why, in the Orthodox tradition, chrismation, the anointing with holy oil, comes immediately after baptism. It is the reception of the Spirit.

This leads to a bold claim. The true climax of the liturgical year is not Easter, or Pascha, as the fathers call it. It is Pentecost. Pascha is the great feast of the Resurrection, of course. But Pentecost is the feast of the giving of the Holy Spirit. It is the renewal of the covenant, the fulfillment of what was promised. Jesus told his disciples that it was to their advantage that he go away, so that the Helper could come. So Lent is ultimately a preparation for Pentecost, for living in the Spirit.

The fathers also address the practice of asceticism. This is not about harming the body or treating matter as evil. The body is not the enemy. Asceticism is about disciplining the flesh so that we can gain self-mastery. The goal is to be mastered by the Holy Spirit, not by our sinful desires. Fasting, vigils, and almsgiving are tools for controlling the passions. They help us see our weaknesses clearly.

And here is a very practical point. Lent is almost set up for us to fail. We try to fast, we try to pray, and we stumble. But that is not a disaster. It is a revelation. It shows us where we are weak. It gives us self-knowledge. And self-mastery is the hardest fruit of the Spirit. Saint Paul contrasts the works of the flesh with the fruit of the Spirit in Galatians chapter five. The works of the flesh are obvious, but the fruit of the Spirit is love, joy, peace, patience, kindness, goodness, faithfulness, gentleness, and self-control. That last one, self-control, is the hardest. Lent is the training ground for it.

So as you enter Great Lent, remember what it is for. It is not just a season of giving things up. It is a season of preparation. It is a time to die to the old man and to be raised to new life. It is a time to be emptied so that you can be filled with the Holy Spirit. And when you fail, do not despair. Learn from it. Get back up. Keep walking in the Spirit. That is the whole point.
