Father Andrew and Father Stephen open this episode with a deep dive into a fascinating and little-known text: the Apocalypse of Abraham. This is a Jewish apocalyptic writing from the late first century, probably composed around the year eighty, not long after the destruction of the Second Temple. It was written by a non-Christian Jew, and it has a strange and winding history. The text was preserved not by the Jewish community, but by a medieval dualist sect in the Balkans called the Bogomils. The Bogomils held Gnostic-like beliefs, thinking the material world was created by the devil, and they kept this ancient Jewish text alive in Slavonic translation. It’s important to note that while they preserved it, they did not write it.

The content of the Apocalypse of Abraham is striking. It begins with Abraham rejecting the idolatry of his father, Terah. Then he is taken up into heaven and guided by an angel named Yehoel. This angel bears the very name of God, and he acts as a priest and a guide for Abraham. This fits into a broader Jewish theological idea of a second power in heaven—a visible divine being, distinct from God, yet carrying His name. Christians later came to see this figure as the pre-incarnate Christ.

The heart of the text is a cosmic Day of Atonement ritual. Abraham is shown a vision of history, and he participates in a kind of heavenly liturgy that mirrors the ritual described in Leviticus sixteen. In that chapter, two goats are used—one for the Lord and one for Azazel, the scapegoat. In the Apocalypse, Azazel appears as a raven and a dragon, a demonic being who is ultimately cast out. The text also reinterprets the story of Cain and Abel from Genesis four, suggesting that Cain was possessed by Azazel. And it draws on Genesis fifteen, where birds of prey descend on Abraham’s covenant sacrifice, seeing that as a sign of Azazel’s attack.

The vision culminates in the destruction of the Second Temple and the coming of a messianic figure. This is where the episode takes a fascinating turn. The hosts explain that this text is the earliest testimony we have of a non-Christian Jewish author applying the Messiah ben Joseph tradition to Jesus of Nazareth. Messiah ben Joseph is a Jewish messianic figure associated with the northern tribes, who gathers Gentiles to worship God and suffers persecution. The fact that a non-Christian Jew would apply this to Jesus is remarkable.

From here, Father Andrew and Father Stephen pivot to the theology of atonement. They argue that Second Temple Jews expected a cosmic Day of Atonement—a final, all-encompassing purification of all creation, a defeat of evil once and for all. This is the context for understanding what Christ did. When John the Baptist calls Jesus the Lamb of God who takes away the sin of the world, he is combining Passover and Day of Atonement imagery. And Paul’s teaching in Romans five, that sin entered the world through one man, points to the need for a cosmic solution, not just a personal one.

This is where the hosts draw a sharp contrast with penal substitutionary atonement, the doctrine most associated with John Calvin. That view holds that Christ took the punishment for the sins of the elect, and that it required His spiritual death and separation from God. Father Andrew and Father Stephen are clear: this is not and has never been taught by the Orthodox Church. Orthodoxy rejects the idea that the Father turned away from the Son, or that Christ’s death was a legal transaction. Instead, the Orthodox understanding is cosmic atonement—Christ defeats death, Hades, and the devil, and restores all of creation.

The episode closes with a reminder of the hope and spiritual warfare inherent in this message. The Christian faith is not about appeasing an angry God, but about the victory of Christ over the powers of evil. The Apocalypse of Abraham, though non-canonical, gives us a window into the world of Second Temple Judaism and the expectations that shaped the apostolic proclamation. It’s a powerful reminder that the gospel is bigger than we often imagine—it’s about the renewal of the whole cosmos.
