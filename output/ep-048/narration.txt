This episode brings the three-part series on the Fall of Man to a close, and Fathers Andrew and Stephen turn their attention to the Tower of Babel. They want us to understand that this story from Genesis chapter eleven is not a myth or a fable. It is a biblical retelling of the actual founding of the city of Babylon, a historical event that the ancient audience would have recognized immediately.

The name Babel is simply the Hebrew way of saying Babylon. And the hosts dig into the meaning of that name. In the Akkadian language, the city was called Bab-ilu, which means Gate of the Gods. But the biblical author makes a deliberate pun. He connects the name to the Hebrew word balal, which means to confuse. This is not a mistake in etymology. It is a theological commentary. The city that claimed to be the gate to heaven is renamed as the place of confusion. It is a mockery of Babylon’s imperial pride.

Historically, Babylon was founded around twenty-three hundred BC. It rose to become the first true world empire under King Hammurabi. He united Mesopotamia and created a vast trade network that connected the known world. The Tower of Babel itself was likely a ziggurat, one of those stepped temple towers common in ancient Mesopotamia. The hosts argue that the tower represents humanity’s attempt to bring God down to serve their own purposes. It was an act of collective pride, an effort to unite all of humanity under one rule, without any reference to the true God.

God’s response was to scatter the people and confuse their language. But there is a deeper layer here. When God divided humanity into nations, He also appointed angelic beings to govern them. This is rooted in Deuteronomy chapter thirty-two, verse eight, which says the Most High divided the nations according to the number of the sons of God. In the Greek translation of the Old Testament, that phrase is angels of God. So the nations were placed under the stewardship of these spiritual beings.

Sadly, many of these angels fell. They became demons and led the nations into idolatry and corruption. Deuteronomy thirty-two, verse seventeen says the nations sacrificed to demons, not to God. And Psalm eighty-two describes God judging these gods, these angelic rulers, for their failure to govern justly. The hosts connect this to the figure of Nimrod, the mighty hunter who is described as the first ruler of Babylon. They link him to the giants of Genesis six and to the Mesopotamian god Ninurta, suggesting he represents a resurgence of that antediluvian wickedness.

This understanding of angelic governance shapes the whole biblical story. Babylon becomes the archetype of every empire that tries to unite humanity under a single, godless rule. The pattern repeats in Rome and in every subsequent empire. The book of Revelation picks up this imagery, depicting Babylon as a harlot, a corrupt world system that opposes God. Revelation sixteen and seventeen show that God remembers Babylon for judgment.

But the story does not end in despair. Christ came to defeat these demonic powers that had enslaved the nations. He fulfilled the promise found in Deuteronomy thirty-two, verse forty-three, which calls for vengeance on the enemies of God. Through His death and resurrection, Christ liberated humanity from the influence of these fallen angels.

The hosts also address the question of land promises. They are clear that all the land promises to Abraham’s descendants were fulfilled in the conquest of Canaan. Joshua twenty-one, verses forty-three through forty-five, states this plainly. There are no outstanding land promises to any ethnic nation today.

Finally, they turn to the ultimate destiny of humanity. This is where theosis, or deification, comes in. The goal of salvation is not just to escape the world. It is to become like God, to participate in His divine nature. The saints are destined to take up the roles that the fallen angels abandoned. They will reign with Christ, as described in Revelation twenty, verse four, and they will sit on thrones, as promised in Matthew nineteen, verse twenty-eight. Humanity was created to govern creation alongside God. The fall disrupted that plan, but Christ restored it. The scattering at Babel was a judgment, but it was also the beginning of a new path, one that would ultimately lead to the calling of Abraham and the blessing of all nations.
