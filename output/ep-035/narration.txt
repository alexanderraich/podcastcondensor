Father Andrew and Father Stephen open this episode with a bold claim: priesthood is not a job, and it is not a clerical office. It is something far more basic. It is built into what it means to be human. They argue that priesthood is a way of relating to God and to creation, and that this calling goes all the way back to the beginning.

They start in the Garden of Eden. The garden, they say, was a temple. And Adam was placed in it as the image of God, mirroring the way ancient temples were built in the ancient Near East. Adam was created as a priest. His task was to represent God to creation, and to offer creation back to God. Through this priestly work, he was meant to expand Eden, to spread the holy space outward. This priestly function is tied to fatherhood and to masculinity, but it is not exclusive to men. Women participate in it fully, though in a different way.

The conversation then moves through the Old Testament, tracing how priesthood developed and, at times, fractured. They point to a crucial moment with Moses. After Moses failed to circumcise his son, the priesthood was taken from him and given to his brother Aaron. From that point on, priesthood and kingship were separated. They were no longer held by the same person. This was a fracture, not the original design. The Aaronic priesthood was established, and the Levites were given their role. Holiness was arranged in concentric circles. At the center was the high priest. Around him were the priests. Then the Levites. Then the people of Israel. And finally, the nations. Each circle served as priests to the next one outward. Israel was called to be a kingdom of priests among the nations, as we hear in Exodus nineteen, verses five and six.

Father Andrew and Father Stephen also address the rebellion of Korah. They make the point that Korah’s rebellion was not about seizing power for its own sake. It was about faithfulness. And God confirmed Aaron’s priesthood in a dramatic way, by opening the earth to swallow Korah and his followers. The priesthood was not a human invention. It was divinely appointed.

The discussion then turns to the corruption of the priesthood in the Second Temple period. The Sadducees controlled the temple, and they corrupted the priesthood for financial gain. This was a dark chapter. But it sets the stage for the coming of Christ.

Christ, they explain, is the Great High Priest. He does not abolish the Old Testament priesthood. He fulfills it. He fills it to overflowing. And in doing so, He reunites what was fractured. He brings priesthood and kingship back together. This is foreshadowed in the person of Melchizedek, the priest-king of Salem, who is a type of Christ. In Christ, the original priestly nature of humanity is restored.

In the New Covenant, this priestly calling is extended to the whole Church. The Greek word presbyter, which means elder, is the root of our English word priest. So the elder in the New Testament is reunited with the priestly function. And the Church as a whole is called a royal priesthood. This echoes Exodus nineteen, and it is applied directly to Christians in First Peter, chapter two, verse nine. The same idea appears in Revelation, chapter one, verse six, where we are told that Christ has made us a kingdom, priests to His God and Father.

The episode closes with practical applications. How do we live out this priestly vocation today? Father Andrew and Father Stephen emphasize that it is not about domination. It is about transparency, sacrifice, and integration. It applies in the family, in the community, and in society. Every Christian is called to this priestly work, to represent God to the world and to offer the world back to God. This is not a special calling for a few. It is the calling of every human being, restored and fulfilled in Christ.
