Father Andrew Stephen Damick and Father Stephen DeYoung open this episode with a question that sounds simple but gets complicated fast: is progress even a thing? They’re not asking whether things get better. They’re asking whether history itself is moving toward some goal, and whether that idea comes from the Bible or from somewhere else entirely.

The modern belief that history is a straight line heading upward toward a better world is, they argue, not traditional Christianity. It’s a secularized version of Christian hope, borrowed and reshaped. The clearest example is the German philosopher Hegel, whose dialectical view of history made progress the engine of everything. The hosts even joke about going “full Hegel,” meaning you’ve adopted the idea that every conflict and contradiction eventually synthesizes into something higher. That view of history as inevitable improvement is everywhere in modern culture, but it’s not what the Church has taught.

Traditional Christianity, they insist, sees Christ’s return not as the culmination of human achievement but as an interruption. It will come like the days of Noah, as the Gospel of Matthew says. People will be living ordinary lives, eating and drinking, and then suddenly everything changes. That’s not progress. That’s a breaking in from outside. The writer of Ecclesiastes agrees: there is nothing new under the sun. The hosts point out that this ancient wisdom directly contradicts the modern assumption that we are always moving beyond the past.

So where does real change come from? Not from social engineering or political force. The abolition of slavery is their key example. They argue that the Church didn’t change its teaching on slavery. Instead, Christians living out the commandments gradually transformed individuals, and over time that transformation reshaped society. Gregory of Nyssa, a fourth-century Church Father, spoke against slavery not because he was a modern activist but because he took the Gospel seriously. St. Paul’s instructions about slaves and masters were never about restructuring institutions. They were about transforming hearts. When hearts change, structures eventually follow.

This is where the hosts introduce the idea of functional atheism. That’s when you believe in God but act as if He isn’t actually involved in the world. Both progressivism and presentism, they argue, fall into this trap. Progressivism assumes that human effort alone can push history forward. Presentism assumes that people in the past thought exactly like we do, which is a form of modern arrogance. Both views leave God out of the picture, even when the people holding them claim to be religious.

They also discuss language and theology. New words don’t change who God is. They help us make distinctions and understand more clearly, but the reality of God remains the same. The development of doctrine is not about inventing new truths. It’s about articulating what was always there. Language shapes our experience, but it doesn’t alter the underlying reality.

The real engine of change, the hosts say, is theosis. That’s the process of becoming like God through participation in His energies. It’s not about achieving moral perfection by willpower. It’s about being transformed by the Holy Spirit. Romans chapter twelve tells us to be transformed by the renewing of our minds. That transformation is what leads to genuine societal change. It’s slow, it’s personal, and it’s not glamorous. But it’s real.

They also bring up Francis Fukuyama, who wrote about the end of history, arguing that liberal democracy was the final stage of human ideological evolution. The hosts use his book as an example of a secular teleology, a story about where history is going that leaves God out. Aristotle’s idea of the final cause, the telos, is useful here. Everything has a purpose, an end toward which it moves. But Christianity doesn’t say that history itself is moving toward a goal in the modern sense. It says that creation has a beginning and an end, and that end is God’s action, not human achievement.

The episode closes with a warning. If you think progress is automatic, or if you think you can engineer the Kingdom of God through politics, you’re setting yourself up for disappointment. The Kingdom comes through transformation, not legislation. It comes through the slow, often invisible work of the Holy Spirit in individual lives. That’s the only progress that matters, and it’s not the kind you can measure on a chart.
