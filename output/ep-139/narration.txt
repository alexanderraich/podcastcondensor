In this episode, Fathers Andrew Stephen Damick and Stephen De Young take us on a journey through the non-canonical Acts of the Apostles. They focus on four major texts: the Acts of Paul, the Acts of Peter, the Acts of John, and the Acts of Thomas. These are the stories of what the apostles did after the events recorded in the Book of Acts. The fathers want us to understand that these texts are not the source of the Church’s traditions about the apostles. Instead, they are witnesses to traditions that already existed. This is a crucial point. The traditions came first, and the texts simply wrote them down.

The fathers begin with the Acts of Paul. This is a second-century text that contains several distinct parts, including the story of Paul and Thecla, a letter to the Corinthians, and the account of Paul’s martyrdom. Even secular scholars admit that parts of this text are historically reliable. In fact, some early churches considered it canonical. The story of Thecla is particularly important. She was a disciple of Paul who chose a life of voluntary virginity. The fathers make a bold claim here. They say that this Christian embrace of voluntary virginity is the very origin of the concept of sexual consent in Western culture. The idea that a woman could choose not to marry, and that her choice should be respected, was revolutionary.

Next, they turn to the Acts of Peter. This is a late second-century text that collects traditions about Peter’s time in Rome. It includes his conflict with Simon Magus, the sorcerer from the canonical Book of Acts who tried to buy the Holy Spirit. In this text, Simon Magus becomes a representative of Gnosticism. He performs magical feats in Rome, but Peter defeats him. The text is clearly anti-Gnostic. It also contains the famous “Quo Vadis” episode, where Peter meets Christ on the road and asks, “Lord, where are you going?” This text is a strong witness against the Gnostic heresy.

The Acts of John is more complicated. It is a mid-second-century text with traditions about John’s ministry, including raising the dead and his peaceful death. However, the fathers explain that chapters eighty-seven through one hundred and five are a later Gnostic insertion. You can identify this insertion by its different style, vocabulary, and theology. The Manichaeans, a major Gnostic religion, used this version of the text. This particular section was even debated at the Seventh Ecumenical Council, because the iconoclasts used it in their arguments against icons.

Finally, they discuss the Acts of Thomas. This text tells the story of Thomas’s mission to India. He is sold as a slave, builds a palace in heaven, and performs exorcisms. It also contains the Hymn of the Pearl. The fathers say this text is ambiguous regarding Gnosticism. It can be read in a Gnostic way or in an Orthodox way. Because of this ambiguity, it is suitable for private reading, but you must read it with discernment.

The fathers emphasize that the Book of Acts itself is the continuation of Christ’s work through the Church. Just as the Gospel of Luke covers what Christ began to do and teach, Acts covers what he continued to do through his apostles. And just as John’s Gospel tells us that not everything Jesus did is recorded, the same is true for the apostles. This justifies the existence of traditions that are not in the New Testament.

The fathers also address the question of canon and authority. A text’s authority is not determined by its content alone. It is determined by the Church’s use of it in public worship. Canonicity is an objective fact of church practice. The apocrypha are not read publicly in church, but they are considered beneficial for private reading. Saint Nikephoros of Constantinople, a ninth-century patriarch, compiled a list of which apocryphal texts are acceptable for this purpose.

The episode also touches on the use of Old Testament imagery in these texts. For example, in the Acts of Thomas, a talking donkey claims descent from Balaam’s ass from the Book of Numbers. And the fathers connect the Tree of Life from Genesis to the Cross and the Eucharist. The Cross is seen as the restoration of the Tree of Life, and its leaves heal the nations. The fathers close by reminding us that these texts are part of the broader Orthodox tradition. They are not scripture, but they are valuable witnesses to the faith and practice of the early Church.
