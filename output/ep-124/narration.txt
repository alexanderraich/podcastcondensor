In this episode, Father Andrew and Father Stephen take us on a deep dive into the history and religion of ancient Babylon. They start by pointing out that Babylon was not always a great city. In its early days, it was a minor settlement, not unlike Byzantium before it became Constantinople. It only rose to real prominence with the arrival of the Amorites, a Semitic people from Syria, who founded the Old Babylonian Empire. That empire, which included the famous dynasty of Hammurabi, lasted for centuries before being eclipsed. The real story picks up again with the fall of Assyria. The Assyrians had been the dominant power in the region, known for their brutality and for their habit of collecting and translating the cultural texts of the peoples they conquered. They also tried to create a single, uniform culture by deporting and assimilating entire populations. But their empire eventually crumbled, and it was Nabopolassar who led a successful revolt and founded the Neo-Babylonian Empire. His son, Nebuchadnezzar the Second, would become the greatest king of that empire.

Nebuchadnezzar came to power around the time of the Battle of Carchemish, where he defeated the Egyptians and solidified Babylonian control over the region. He is also the king who conquered Judah and destroyed Jerusalem, leading to the Babylonian exile. The hosts note that in the book of Jeremiah, God actually calls Nebuchadnezzar his servant. In Jeremiah twenty-seven, God declares that he has given all lands into the king’s hand, making him an instrument of divine judgment against Judah and the other nations. This is a striking claim, that a pagan king could serve God’s purposes so directly. Nebuchadnezzar was also a great builder. He constructed the famous Ishtar Gate and is traditionally associated with the Hanging Gardens, one of the wonders of the ancient world. His building projects were not just for show; they were acts of religious and political devotion.

The second half of the episode turns to Babylonian religion, especially the god Marduk. Marduk started as a local agrarian deity, but he rose to become the head of the Babylonian pantheon. His story is told in the Enuma Elish, the Babylonian creation epic. In that myth, Marduk defeats the primordial chaos dragon, Tiamat, and creates the heavens and the earth from her body. The Enuma Elish was recited during the Akitu festival, the Babylonian New Year celebration, which reenacted Marduk’s victory over chaos. The hosts describe Marduk as being characterized in the epic as savage yet relenting. He is prone to wrath and violence, but he can occasionally show mercy. This is a sharp contrast to the biblical God, who is defined by love and faithfulness. The episode also touches on the Tower of Babel story from Genesis eleven. The hosts interpret it as a spiritual account of the Bronze Age collapse, a time when God dispersed humanity and confused their languages to prevent the reemergence of the wickedness that had existed before the flood. The unfinished ziggurat in Babylon, the Etemenanki, serves as a historical backdrop for that story.

Finally, the episode examines Nebuchadnezzar’s appearances in the Bible, particularly in the book of Daniel. Daniel interprets the king’s dream of a great statue, which symbolizes a succession of world empires, culminating in the messianic kingdom of God. Then there is the story of Shadrach, Meshach, and Abednego, who refuse to worship the king’s idol and are thrown into a fiery furnace, where they are protected by a divine figure. And then there is the dream of the great tree, which warns of Nebuchadnezzar’s temporary madness. The king loses his reason and lives like an animal for a time, but he is eventually restored. His restoration leads to a confession of faith in the Most High. The hosts emphasize that this shows God working for the repentance and salvation of even pagan kings. They also bring up the death of King Josiah, who was killed at the Battle of Megiddo against Pharaoh Neco, despite God’s warning through Neco. That story illustrates the consequences of not trusting God’s word. The episode concludes with a reflection on how God works in the lives of all people, even those outside the covenant, and the importance of trusting in God’s lordship over all of history.
