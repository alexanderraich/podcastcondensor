In this Halloween special of the Lord of Spirits podcast, Fathers Andrew Stephen Damick and Stephen De Young take on a truly monstrous topic: madness. They explore how being out of your mind has been understood from ancient times all the way to the modern day, and the picture they paint is surprising.

In the ancient world, madness wasn’t just a medical problem. It was often seen as a sign of contact with the divine. Think of Gilgamesh, the legendary king of Uruk, who was said to be two-thirds divine and stood above the social order. Or his companion Enkidu, the wild man who lived outside society and was seen as touched by the gods. Even the Greek philosopher Socrates claimed a divine spirit guided him, which let him stand apart from Athens and critique it. In these cultures, being set outside the normal rules of society could give you special wisdom or insight. The category of madness, then, is not a fixed thing. It is fluid, and it is defined by whatever a society considers outside its norms.

That understanding shifted over time. By the medieval period, madness became a sociopolitical tool. It was used to label political dissidents, women, and anyone who didn’t conform. The French philosopher Michel Foucault traced this in his work on madness and civilization, showing how the concept changed in Western history. And eventually, madness became medicalized as mental illness. But the hosts point out that this modern category is also broad, used for anyone who cannot or will not function within society.

The episode then turns to the Bible, and the story of Nebuchadnezzar in Daniel chapter four. The Babylonian king was driven mad by God. He was forced to live like a wild animal until he learned humility and the sovereignty of the Most High. His madness was a divine judgment, meant to teach him that his power was delegated and temporary. Daniel, the Jewish prophet, served as his wise counselor, interpreting the king’s dream of the great tree that was cut down.

So how do we tell the difference between divine madness, demonic possession, and mental illness? The hosts offer pastoral guidance. A fool for Christ, like the Russian holy fool Basil who confronted Ivan the Terrible, is set apart by God to speak truth and call people to repentance. Their behavior may look odd, but their testimony is prophetic. Demonic possession is different. It is a state where a person is controlled by an anti-human spiritual force, leading to destructive behavior, but with no prophetic testimony at all. And people do not accidentally become demon-possessed. It results from a long period of living in a demonic way. But even the most possessed can be healed by Christ, as we see in Mark chapter five with the Gadarene demoniac, who was filled with a legion of demons and then sent to testify to his community after Jesus healed him.

The hosts also challenge the idea of the world. In Scripture, the world often refers to oppressive social structures that are animated by demonic spirits and pressure everyone to conform. This stands in opposition to the Kingdom of God. Think of Cain in Genesis chapter four, who murdered Abel because the righteous man was a threat to his unrighteous social order. Christians are called aliens and strangers in this world, as Peter writes in his first letter.

So what is the takeaway? Christian communities are called to be counter-cultural. They can offer an alternative to the world’s social structures by becoming actual families in Christ, providing a place for all people and freeing them from conformity. Even if that means being seen as insane by the world, the hosts conclude, we are called to embody the sanity of God’s kingdom.
