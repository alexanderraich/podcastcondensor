In this episode, Father Andrew and Father Stephen take us deep into the prophetic tradition, and they center everything on the towering figure of Elijah. They make the case that Elijah is the paradigmatic prophet. What does that mean? It means he is the standard, the model for what a prophet actually is. Unlike many other prophets who are mostly known for their writings, Elijah gets a full, detailed biography in the books of Kings. His life is a story, and that story sets the pattern for everything that comes after.

The hosts spend a lot of time on Elijah’s role in spiritual warfare. His great confrontation with the prophets of Baal is not just a miracle story. It is a battle in the heavenly realm. Baal was a storm god, a usurper in the divine council, and Elijah’s ministry was a direct assault on that false worship. This is spiritual warfare made visible. And it is important to understand that the divine council is not just an abstract idea. It is the heavenly assembly where God presides with his angels and with exalted humans. Prophets like Elijah stand in that council. They receive their messages there, and they are guided by it.

That brings us to John the Forerunner. The hosts connect Elijah directly to John. The angel Gabriel tells Zechariah that John will go before the Lord in the spirit and power of Elijah. This fulfills the prophecy from Malachi, where God promises to send Elijah before the great and terrible day of the Lord. So John is not just a random preacher in the wilderness. He is Elijah’s successor, guided by Elijah himself in the divine council. John lives what the hosts call the angelic life. That is a life lived in constant awareness of the angels, a life of worship and spiritual warfare. John lived that life in the wilderness, and it is the same life that monastics later pursue.

The episode also covers the concept of the remnant. In Elijah’s time, he thought he was alone, but God told him there were seven thousand in Israel who had not bowed to Baal. This is a pattern throughout history. God’s work is often carried by a faithful minority. But the hosts are careful to warn against identifying yourself as the remnant. The call is to be faithful, not to claim a special status. And they tie this to community. God does not save individuals in isolation. Salvation is communal. You have to be part of a community to be saved. That is a core teaching of the episode.

There is also a fascinating discussion about the Zachariah mentioned by Jesus in Matthew chapter twenty-three. Jesus says that Zachariah, the son of Berechiah, was murdered between the temple and the altar. The hosts argue, following church tradition, that this is John the Forerunner’s father. According to tradition, Zachariah was killed in the temple because he refused to reveal where John was hiding. Elizabeth had taken the child into the wilderness to escape Herod’s massacre. So this Zachariah is not some obscure figure from the Old Testament. He is the father of the Forerunner, and his death is part of the same story of spiritual warfare.

Finally, the hosts talk about patron saints. The relationship between Elijah and John is the model for this. A patron saint is not just a distant intercessor. A patron saint leads and mentors the person under their care. Elijah guided John, and in the same way, the saints guide us. This is all part of the larger picture. The prophets, the divine council, the angelic life, the remnant, and the saints are all woven together into one story. And that story is about God preparing the way for Christ, and about us being drawn into that preparation through community, through the saints, and through faithful witness in the midst of spiritual warfare.
