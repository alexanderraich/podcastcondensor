In this episode, Father Andrew and Father Stephen take on the enormous subject of astrology. They trace its story from the ancient world all the way to its rejection by the Church, and they do it by looking at where it came from and what it actually means.

The story begins in Babylon. There, astrology started as a system of omens. Unusual events in the sky were read as messages from the gods. If something strange happened with the planet Jupiter, that was a sign from the god Marduk. If Venus behaved oddly, that was a message from Ishtar. Over time, these scattered omens were organized into a system. The movements of the planets, which the Greeks called the wanderers, or planetes, were tracked against the fixed stars. The path of the sun, called the ecliptic, was divided into twelve sections, and the zodiac was born.

Now, this way of thinking did not stay in Babylon. It spread into the Jewish world, especially during the Second Temple period. The hosts point out that there was a real Jewish astrology at this time. The twelve tribes of Israel were connected to the twelve signs of the zodiac. In the book of Genesis, when Jacob blesses his sons, the animal imagery used for each tribe lines up with the zodiac constellations. The arrangement of the tribes in the wilderness camp, described in the book of Numbers, mirrors the layout of the Babylonian zodiac, with four main tribes at the cardinal points and two on each side. Even the prophet Ezekiel’s vision of the four living creatures is tied to the four fixed signs of the zodiac. So the symbols were there, and they were used.

But the key point is how these things were understood. The philosopher Philo of Alexandria, a Jewish thinker, made a careful distinction. He said the heavenly bodies are not independent gods. They are vice-regents of God, messengers. They have a role, but they are not the source of power. The stars and planets are associated with angelic beings, but those beings are not free agents. They serve the one God.

This is where the Christian rejection comes in. The Church Fathers, especially St. Augustine and St. John Chrysostom, were very clear about this. Augustine, in his Confessions and in the City of God, argued that astrology requires fatalism. If the stars determine your life, then you have no free will. And if you have no free will, then there is no point to moral responsibility, and no point to salvation. Chrysostom preached against astrology directly. He said the stars are angels, but if you give them any independent power, you take away from the sovereignty of God. The practice of divination, trying to read the future from the stars, was condemned because it denies human freedom.

There is a striking example of this in the story of Aquila of Sinope. He was a second-century translator of the Hebrew Bible into Greek. He was excommunicated from Christianity because he practiced astrology, and he later converted to Judaism. The Church did not take this lightly.

The hosts also look at the scriptures that seem to speak positively about the heavens. Psalm nineteen says the heavens declare the glory of God. They read this as a reference to the sun’s circuit along the ecliptic. The sun is like a bridegroom coming out of his chamber, running its course. This is not a problem. It is a declaration of God’s handiwork. The Apostle Paul even quotes this psalm in his letter to the Romans, saying that the gospel has gone out to all the earth through the heavenly bodies. So there is a positive use of this language, but it is always about the Creator, not the creation.

Moses warned the people in Deuteronomy not to worship the sun, moon, and stars, which are allotted to all peoples. Philo used this very verse to make his point. You can look at the stars and see the glory of God, but you must not worship them.

In the end, the hosts argue that Christianity is an ethos of freedom. It is freedom from fatalism, freedom from the bondage of demonic powers, and freedom from the expectations of others. The stars are not your masters. They are messengers. The goal of the Christian life is not to figure out what the stars have decreed, but to be transformed, to grow into the likeness of God, which is theosis. The call in Hebrews to lay aside every weight and the sin that clings closely is a call to be free from these bondages. Salvation is not about escaping a predetermined fate. It is about being set free from the powers that would enslave you, so that you can truly choose the good.
