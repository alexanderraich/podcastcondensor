This episode picks up where the last one left off, diving deeper into modernism and its strange, often toxic relationship with Christianity. Father Andrew and Father Stephen start by laying out the core pillars of modernism. It’s a cultural movement that puts the individual front and center, abstracted from any family, community, or history. This leads straight into egalitarianism, the idea that everyone is fundamentally the same and equal, which then fuels democracy and the notion that every opinion carries the same weight. And then there’s the theory of development—the belief that history moves through predictable stages and that we can understand and even steer that progress. The hosts argue that this whole worldview arose as a rejection of Christianity, an attempt to build a civilization on something other than the Christian religion.

When Christianity gets reshaped by these modernist ideas, it stops being Christianity altogether. It becomes something else wearing a familiar mask. The hosts compare this to ancient heresies like Arianism, which used the same scriptures and the same church structures but believed in a different Christ. Modernist Christianity does the same thing—it keeps the language but changes the meaning. Worship becomes about the individual’s experience, scripture gets filtered through contemporary politics, and ethics get redefined around equality and personal autonomy.

Then the conversation turns to postmodernism. The hosts make a crucial point here: what we call postmodernism is really post-structuralism. It’s a critique of modernism’s claims to objectivity and universal truth. Thinkers like Jacques Derrida and Michel Foucault argued that language can’t convey meaning objectively. All signs are arbitrary, and so-called universal truths are really just power plays. Friedrich Nietzsche laid the groundwork for this with his perspectivism, and Karl Marx, ironically, was a peak modernist with his grand theory of historical development. The hosts bring in Terry Eagleton’s work to show how postmodernism, for all its critique, still operates within the same individualistic framework.

So what happens when postmodernism reshapes Christianity? You get a faith that’s all about subjective experience and personal identity. There’s no real authority structure left. Instead, you get charisma and personality cults. People follow whoever speaks to their lived experience, and doctrine becomes whatever feels authentic to the individual. The hosts warn that the reactionary response to this is just as modern as modernism itself. Traditionalism—the dead faith of the living—is an individual’s reconstruction of the past, based on personal preference. It assumes the very things it claims to hate. Even Edmund Burke, the father of traditional conservatism, was critiquing the French Revolution from within a modernist framework.

So where does that leave us? The hosts point to the Orthodox Church as the way forward. It bridges the universal and the particular. It is the one holy catholic and apostolic church, but it’s also constituted by particular local churches. It’s not an abstract ideal or a personal project. It’s a living tradition, formed in community, and it offers a correctable, communal experience of Christ. The tradition is the living faith of the dead, not a museum piece. And it’s grounded in the reality that even angels and demons can die spiritually, as we hear in Psalm eighty-two, being separated from God. The hosts also touch on the idea of consequent necessity, from First Corinthians fifteen, where Christ’s death was necessary given the way the world actually is, but wouldn’t have been if the world were different. The Church, rooted in this living tradition, is the place where we can actually encounter Christ, not as a projection of our own desires, but as the risen Lord who meets us in community.
