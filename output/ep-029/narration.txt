In this Monster Month episode of the Lord of Spirits, Fathers Andrew Stephen Damick and Stephen De Young take us on a journey through the biblical and historical roots of some of our most enduring monsters. They begin with Lilith, a figure who first appears not in the Bible, but in ancient Sumerian poetry. In the poem about Gilgamesh and Enkidu in the Netherworld, she is a demon living in a tree, and her name is tied to the Southwest wind. From there, she moves through Akkadian and Hebrew traditions, eventually appearing in the Book of Isaiah, in chapter thirty-four, verse fourteen. There, she haunts the ruins of Edom, and many translations render her as a night bird or a screech owl. The hosts connect her to other ancient demonesses like Lamashtu, who was known for harming infants and pregnant women, and to the Greek Lamia, a serpent-woman who devours children. They make clear that these are not just myths, but spiritual realities that the ancient world took very seriously.

Next, they tackle the unicorn. In the King James Version of the Bible, the word unicorn appears several times, but it is a translation of the Hebrew word re’em. That word most likely refers to a wild ox or an aurochs, not a gentle horse with a horn. The hosts argue that in the biblical context, the unicorn is actually a monster, a creature representing wild, untamed, and even demonic forces. You hear about it in Numbers chapter twenty-three, verse twenty-two, in Balaam’s oracle, and again in Job chapter thirty-nine, verse nine, where God points to His power over creation. Even in Psalm twenty-two, verse twenty-one, the psalmist cries out for deliverance from the horn of the wild ox. So the unicorn of scripture is not a symbol of purity, but a beast of chaos.

The conversation then turns to werewolves. The hosts trace these legends back to ancient Greece, specifically to the story of King Lycaon, who sacrificed a child to Zeus and was transformed into a wolf as punishment. They connect this to the rituals at Mount Lycaeon, where human sacrifice and cannibalism were said to take place. The werewolf, they explain, is not just a man who turns into an animal. It is a person who has entered into demonic communion, often through cannibalistic acts. They compare this to the Native American Wendigo stories, where a person becomes a cannibalistic spirit through greed and the eating of human flesh. In every case, the transformation is tied to a real spiritual corruption.

From werewolves, they move to vampires. Here, the hosts are very clear: a vampire is not a living human being. It is a demon-possessed corpse that returns from the dead to harm the living. In Greek tradition, this creature is called the vrykolakas. The hosts reference the Nomocanon, a collection of ecclesiastical law, which gives Orthodox priests instructions for dealing with such cases. This is not folklore dismissed by the Church, but a real pastoral concern. The Church provides liturgical remedies for these situations, because the power behind the vampire is demonic, not natural.

Finally, they discuss demon possession and the power of holy people to command demons. They bring up King Solomon and his ring, as described in the Testament of Solomon, a non-canonical text. In that story, Solomon uses a magical ring to command demons to build the Temple. The hosts point out that this motif, of holy people commanding demons through their holiness, appears throughout the lives of the saints. But they also warn that this power is lost through sin. They connect this to Genesis chapter four, verse seven, where sin is described as crouching at the door, waiting to master us. The point is not to seek magical control over the spiritual world, but to trust in Christ. The monsters are real, but so is the power of God, and it is only through Him that we are safe.
