Father Andrew Stephen Damick and Father Stephen De Young open this episode by turning to a figure who appears only briefly in the pages of the Old Testament, yet stands at the very center of the New Testament’s understanding of Christ. That figure is Melchizedek, the king of Salem and priest of God Most High, who meets Abram after the patriarch rescues his nephew Lot from a coalition of warring kings.

The hosts begin by setting the scene from Genesis chapter fourteen. After the battle, Melchizedek comes out to meet Abram, bringing bread and wine, and he blesses him. The text is careful to call him a priest of God Most High, and the phrase is repeated several times. Father Andrew and Father Stephen stress that this repetition is deliberate. Melchizedek’s name sounds like it refers to a pagan deity, and his city, Salem, is what would later become Jerusalem. But the biblical author is making a clear point: this man is not a pagan priest-king. He is a faithful remnant of true worship, a survivor of the confusion of the Tower of Babel, who still serves the one true God.

The conversation then turns to Abram’s refusal of the spoils offered by the king of Sodom. The hosts explain that this is not merely a matter of pride or political maneuvering. The spoils of Sodom were tainted by the culture of the giant clans, and Abram wanted nothing to do with their goods or their gods. His refusal is a act of separation, a rejection of the corrupt world around him, and a declaration that his blessing comes from God Most High alone.

From there, the episode traces the messianic thread that runs through the rest of the Old Testament. Father Andrew and Father Stephen walk through Jacob’s blessing of Judah in Genesis forty-nine, where a ruler is promised from Judah’s line. They then move to the Davidic covenant in Second Samuel, where David is called a prince rather than a full king, pointing forward to a future king who will come from his dynasty. They also address a common misunderstanding of First Samuel chapter eight, where the people demand a king. The problem, they clarify, was not kingship itself. God had actually commanded that a king be appointed, and Deuteronomy seventeen even gives instructions for the king to copy and study the Torah. The problem was that the people wanted a king like the pagan nations, a king who would replace God’s direct rule rather than serve under it.

The heart of the episode is Psalm one hundred ten. Father Andrew and Father Stephen call it the most quoted Old Testament text in the New Testament. The psalm describes the Messiah sitting at God’s right hand, ruling in the midst of his enemies, and being a priest forever after the order of Melchizedek. They explain that this psalm describes the current messianic age, the period between Christ’s resurrection and his second coming. During this time, Christ reigns as both king and high priest, and the central activity of this age is the liturgy, the worship of the Church, until all his enemies are finally defeated.

The hosts then turn to the Epistle to the Hebrews, which draws out the deeper implications of Melchizedek’s priesthood. Unlike the Levitical priesthood, which was based on lineage and was temporary, Melchizedek’s priesthood is eternal and heavenly. It prefigures Christ’s unique high priesthood, a priesthood that does not pass from one generation to another but belongs to Christ alone.

Finally, Father Andrew and Father Stephen introduce a fascinating piece of evidence from the Dead Sea Scrolls, a text known as eleven Q thirteen, written around one hundred years before Christ. This text already interprets Melchizedek as a divine messianic figure who will judge the demonic powers and liberate captives, connecting him to the Jubilee year described in Leviticus twenty-five. This shows that the New Testament’s use of Melchizedek was not a novel invention but was rooted in pre-existing Jewish exegesis.

The episode closes with a reflection on the unity of Scripture. The Bible, Father Andrew and Father Stephen argue, is not a random collection of writings. It is a cohesive whole, a single story that points to Christ. And that coherence, they suggest, is itself a powerful argument for the truth of the Christian faith.
