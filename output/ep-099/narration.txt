Father Andrew Stephen Damick and Father Stephen De Young open this episode with a bold question: what is religion, really? They argue that the word itself is a modern invention, and that when we use it to describe ancient people, we often get things badly wrong. Religion, they say, is not primarily a set of beliefs you hold in your head. It is a way of being in the world, a way that touches every level of reality and shows itself in what people actually do. That working definition shapes everything that follows.

From there, they take us back to a period called the Axial Age, a term coined by the philosopher Karl Jaspers. This was the time from roughly eight hundred to two hundred years before Christ, when major religious and philosophical movements appeared all across the globe. In Greece you had the rise of philosophy. In Israel, the development of what we now call Judaism. In India, Buddhism. In Persia, Zoroastrianism. The fathers point out that these movements shared some striking themes. Spirits became less personal, more abstract. And ethics, the question of how to live rightly, began to take center stage. They also note that this shift was driven by real-world changes, like the rise of literacy and the stabilization of societies.

But to understand the Axial Age, you have to go back further, to the Neolithic Revolution. That is the name for the great transition that happened around ten thousand to eight thousand years before Christ, when human beings stopped wandering as hunter-gatherers and settled down to farm. This was the domestication of plants and animals, and it changed everything, including religion. The fathers explain that hunter-gatherers tended to focus on a single sky deity, a high god above all things. But once people settled and agriculture became central, that changed. Now you get earth goddesses, local spirits, and a whole multiplicity of gods. Rain mattered more than ever, so you get storm gods like Zeus, who was a later development from those earlier sky deities.

They bring in real places to make this concrete. Göbekli Tepe in modern Turkey is one of the earliest known religious sites, dating to around nine thousand years before Christ. It has massive stone pillars carved with animals, and it was built before permanent settlement. That is fascinating, because it suggests that worship came first, and the town followed. Then there is Çatalhöyük, a later Neolithic settlement known for its domestic shrines and evidence of bull worship. And Jericho, one of the oldest continuously inhabited cities on earth, though the fathers note that early excavations damaged the site, so we have to be careful about what we claim to know.

They also bring in scripture. They discuss Deuteronomy thirty-one, where the Torah is commanded to be read publicly every seven years. The fathers use this to suggest that the Torah was not widely known in early Israel, which fits with the broader picture of religious development. And they turn to Acts seventeen, where Paul speaks to the Athenians about the unknown god. That, they say, is a good analogy for how missionaries approached indigenous peoples who still had a memory of a high god, even after centuries of local spirits had crowded in.

The episode closes with a theological reflection. Orthodox Christianity, they insist, is the right worship of the true God. And throughout history, anyone who worshiped God rightly was practicing the same religion, even if their practices and understanding evolved over time. They end with the vision from Revelation, where the angels worship the Lamb, to emphasize that this worship is continuous, stretching from the beginning of the world to its end.
