This episode of the Lord of Spirits was recorded live, and it tackles something that many parishes are experiencing right now: what Fathers Andrew and Stephen call the "Convert Surge." This is the significant influx of people coming into Orthodox Christianity in America, especially since the pandemic. And while it’s a cause for joy, it also brings real challenges and opportunities for local communities.

Father Andrew and Father Stephen begin by framing conversion not as a simple intellectual decision, but as a real, three-dimensional movement. They point to the baptismal service, specifically the prayers of renunciation and alignment. In the service, the person being baptized literally renounces the devil—this is the apotaxis—and then turns to Christ, which is the syntaxis. This isn’t just symbolic. It’s a genuine transfer of allegiance, a movement from one master to another. They even connect the breathing in the baptismal exorcism to the moment in John’s Gospel when Christ breathes on the disciples, showing that this is a real spiritual encounter.

The surge itself, they insist, is not the result of any clever program or strategy. It’s God’s action. The Church’s job, then, is to receive these newcomers with gratitude and wisdom. The big question is how to catechize them properly. Father Andrew and Father Stephen are clear that catechesis should not be like a college survey course—what they call "Orthodoxy 101." It’s not about passing a theology test. Instead, it should train people to be faithful parishioners: worshippers, pray-ers, almsgivers, and volunteers. It’s about forming habits of faithful living, not just filling heads with information.

This leads to a very practical discussion about the clergy shortage. Because there are so many converts and only so many priests, pastoral care is effectively rationed. A priest simply cannot give everyone the individual attention they might want. This is where lay ministry becomes essential. Father Andrew and Father Stephen emphasize that laypeople must step up to minister to one another. This includes practical acts of love, mentoring newcomers, and helping them integrate into the life of the parish. They reference a priest in Raleigh, North Carolina, Father David, whose parish has a huge number of converts and confessions, and he relies heavily on his deacons and lay leaders to make that work.

The conversation also touches on the demographics of the surge. While the first wave may have been dominated by single men, the second peak is increasingly made up of young families. This shifts the needs of the parish, from apologetics to childcare, from late-night discussions to Sunday school.

During the audience Q&A, they address a number of specific concerns. One question asks about attracting women converts. The fathers suggest that the Church’s emphasis on community, on the Theotokos, and on a holistic way of life often resonates deeply, but they caution against trying to engineer outcomes. Another question deals with the role of the internet in conversion. They acknowledge that online content can be a gateway, but they offer a strong warning: discern online Orthodox content by its fruits, as Saint Paul writes in Galatians, chapter five. If a particular influencer or page produces strife, anger, or division, avoid it. If it produces love, joy, and peace, it’s probably safe.

They also address the delicate issue of integrating newcomers without destabilizing existing communities. Here, they turn to Saint Paul’s letter to the Romans, specifically chapter eleven. Paul was writing to a church in Rome that had both Jewish and Gentile believers, and he had to navigate the tension between the original members and the newcomers. The fathers apply this directly: the "cradle" Orthodox and the converts need to learn to be one body. The old members shouldn’t feel displaced, and the newcomers shouldn’t try to take over. It’s a mutual learning process.

Finally, there’s a fascinating question about the meaning of hell in Orthodox iconography. The fathers explain the image of the "hellmouth"—the serpent or dragon swallowing the damned in Last Judgment icons. This image is rooted in Genesis, chapter three, where the serpent is cursed to eat dust. The dust is what we are made of, and what we return to in death. So the serpent eating dust is a symbol of death and the devil’s power. The point of this imagery, they stress, is not primarily to threaten unbelievers. It’s presented in scripture and in icons to believers, to motivate us to repentance and to wake us up to the reality of our choices. As they remind us, we are judged by our actions, not just our opinions, as Christ makes clear in Matthew, chapter twenty-five.

In the end, the episode is a call to see the convert surge as a gift from God, a moment for the Church to be the Church—welcoming, teaching, and loving, not through programs, but through the faithful witness of every single member.
