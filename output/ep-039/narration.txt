In this episode, Fathers Andrew Stephen Damick and Stephen De Young take on a big question: how should we read the Bible? They start by contrasting the way people in the past understood history with the way we do today. For pre-modern people, history was not an objective, scientific record. It was a purposeful narrative, woven together from Scripture, other ancient writings, and the classics. Its goal was to shape a people’s identity, to tell them who they were and whose they were. It wasn’t trying to be neutral or detached.

Then everything changed in the nineteenth century, especially in German universities. History became a science, what they called *Wissenschaft*. The Bible was treated as evidence to be weighed and judged like any other ancient document. This modern shift gave rise to the fight between modernists and fundamentalists. But here’s the thing: both sides actually share the same flawed assumption. They both believe the Bible is making objective truth claims that have to be verified or debunked. They’re playing the same game, just on opposite teams.

The fathers want to get us out of that trap. They propose reading forward. That means starting with the Old Testament, then moving into the New Testament, and then into the Church Fathers. Scripture is not a flat book of propositions. It’s a growing organism, and they borrow an analogy from the philosopher Hegel to make that point. Hegel isn’t usually a friend to Orthodox Christians, but here they give him credit for seeing that ideas develop organically through history. Scripture grows like that, culminating in Christ.

So what does that mean for meaning? The fathers say meaning is not a single essence hidden behind the text, like a secret code you have to crack. Instead, meaning is encountered in application. This is where the theology of God’s essence and energies comes in. We can’t grasp God’s essence, but we can experience His energies. The same is true for Scripture. The text is not a static thing. God acts through it, and we meet Him in that action, especially in the liturgical life of the Church.

The Church Fathers didn’t try to find one single interpretation of a passage. They applied Scripture to their own context. They participated in God’s work through the text. Take the Donatists, for example. They were a fourth-century sect that said salvation was restricted to their own group. The Fathers took First John, chapter two, verse two, which says Christ died for the whole world, and they applied it directly to refute that narrowness. They weren’t doing abstract exegesis. They were using Scripture to heal the Church.

The hosts also bring up a test case: Genesis chapter six, verses one through four, the story of the Nephilim. They ask how the first readers would have understood that passage. And they talk about Isaiah chapter seven, verse fourteen. In its original context, that verse was about the Syro-Ephraimite war, not a prediction of a virgin birth. But Matthew, in chapter one, verse twenty-three, applies it to Christ’s birth. He’s not saying it was a prediction that came true. He’s saying it’s a fulfillment, an echo of an earlier deliverance, now seen in a new light.

There’s also a warning here. Many Christians are what the fathers call functional Marcionites. Marcion was an early heretic who rejected the Old Testament entirely. The hosts say that if we don’t read the Old Testament seriously, we’re falling into the same trap.

So what’s the practical takeaway? First, apply Scripture to yourself before you apply it to anyone else. Then apply it to those under your spiritual care. And finally, remember that you encounter Christ not just by studying, but by obedience to His commandments and by acts of mercy. The Bible is not a modern history book. It’s a story of a people, and you are invited to belong to that people. The goal is not to master the text. The goal is to be transformed by it.
