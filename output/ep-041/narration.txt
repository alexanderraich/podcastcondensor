Father Andrew and Father Stephen open this episode by tackling a question that sounds simple but goes very deep: what does it actually mean to be blessed, or cursed? They start with Deuteronomy, chapters twenty-eight through thirty, where blessings and curses are laid out as the consequences of obeying or disobeying God’s commandments. But they are quick to warn against reading this as a simple transaction, as if God is a vending machine. Instead, they argue, blessing is about living in harmony with the way God created the world to work. It is a state of right relationship within the whole web of creation. A curse, on the other hand, is not just a punishment. It is a state of alienation and corruption, a breaking of those relationships that leads to decay and death.

This is where they take aim at the prosperity gospel. The idea that blessing means material wealth and success is, for them, a distortion. True blessedness is not about accumulating stuff. It is relational and communal. It is about participating in divine life. They dig into the words themselves. The Hebrew word for blessing, barak, literally means to kneel. It suggests a change in posture, a reorientation. The Greek word, eulogia, means a good word, an endorsement. Both point to the same thing: being brought into right relationship. And the Greek word makarios, used in the Beatitudes, refers to the blissful life of the gods. Father Andrew and Father Stephen appreciate David Bentley Hart’s translation of that word as blissful, because it captures that divine connotation. The Beatitudes are not about material prosperity. They are about sharing in God’s own life, a life that begins now, not just in the future.

They also explore how blessing and cursing involve human cooperation with spiritual powers. Blessing aligns with divine energies, while cursing aligns with demonic ones. They bring up ancient Ugaritic death curses as an example of how cursing in the ancient world involved invoking spiritual powers and cooperating with demons. This is not a neutral act. It affects the state of the person and the world. They also distinguish between moral impurity, which comes from sinful actions and involves cooperation with demonic powers, and ceremonial impurity, which comes from exposure to death and decay. Both are related to curse, but in different ways.

The Book of Job is referenced as a challenge to the simplistic view that blessings and curses are directly tied to behavior. Job suffers despite being righteous. And Cain is presented as the archetypal cursed person. His alienation from creation and community leads him to found a city, which becomes a symbol of the community of the alienated. Abraham, by contrast, is the one in whom all nations will bless themselves, as we hear in Genesis chapter twelve, verse three. This connects blessing to justification and to being in right relationship with God.

The heart of the episode is the cross. They quote Galatians chapter three, verse thirteen, where Paul says Christ became a curse for us. This echoes Deuteronomy twenty-one, verse twenty-three, about hanging on a tree. Christ takes upon Himself the curse of sin and impurity. This is connected to the imagery of the Day of Atonement, where the scapegoat is sent into the wilderness to Azazel, a demonic being, carrying away the sins of the people. And it connects to the bronze serpent in Numbers, which Christ references in John chapter twelve, verse thirty-two, when He says He will be lifted up and draw all men to Himself. Through His death, He reconciles us to God, as Paul writes in Romans chapter five, verse ten. That reconciliation is the essence of blessing.

They close with Psalm one, walking through it as a summary of the blessed life. The righteous person is like a tree planted by streams of water, yielding fruit in season. This is not about wealth or success. It is about being rooted in right relationship with God, drawing life from Him continually. Father Andrew and Father Stephen encourage listeners to seek reconciliation and to participate in the liturgical life of the Church. That is how we enter into blessedness. Eternal life is not just a future reward. It begins now, through our present relationships and actions, as we live in harmony with God’s created order.
