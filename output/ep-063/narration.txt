In this episode, Father Andrew and Father Stephen take up the Holy Mystery of Monasticism, and they trace its roots all the way back to the Old Testament. They begin with the book of Numbers, chapter six, where the Lord speaks to Moses and institutes the Nazarite vow. This is a special kind of separation to God, and it comes with three main restrictions. The person taking the vow must abstain from wine and anything made from grapes, they must not cut their hair, and they must avoid contact with corpses. The hosts are careful to point out that this vow was not a human invention. The text is clear that the Lord himself spoke to Moses about it, which shows that God directly established this path of dedication.

The conversation then turns to the most famous Nazarite in the scriptures, and that is Samson. Now, Samson was a Nazarite from birth, as we hear in the book of Judges. But Father Andrew and Father Stephen make a strong case that Samson is really a bad monk. He breaks every single vow he takes. He touches dead things, he drinks at feasts, and his hair, the sign of his dedication, is ultimately cut. They argue that Samson is not a saint. In fact, they point out that many Church Fathers viewed him very negatively. Saint Ephraim the Syrian listed Samson among the dead in hell, and Saint Hippolytus went so far as to call him a forerunner of the Antichrist, linking him to the tribe of Dan. This connects to Jacob’s prophecy in Genesis, where Dan is called a serpent, and it also explains why the tribe of Dan is left out of the list of the twelve tribes in the book of Revelation. Even when Samson is mentioned in the eleventh chapter of Hebrews among the heroes of faith, the hosts note that Saint John Chrysostom defended Saint Paul’s inclusion of him, clarifying that it does not mean God endorses his life.

To contrast with Samson, the hosts turn to Saint John the Forerunner, the Baptist. His birth is announced in the Gospel of Luke, and the parallels to Samson’s birth are striking. But where Samson fails, John succeeds. He keeps his vows. He lives in the wilderness, he abstains from wine, and he embodies the ascetic ideal perfectly. He is the model Nazarite, and he becomes the model for Christian monasticism.

From there, the episode moves into the early church and the development of monasticism in Egypt and Syria. They discuss Saint Anthony the Great, who is considered a founder of the monastic movement, and Saint John Cassian, who brought the wisdom of the Eastern desert fathers to the West and influenced Saint Benedict. The hosts also bring up a fascinating point about hair and sexuality in the ancient world. In Greek and Roman culture, long hair was often associated with sexual availability. This helps explain Saint Paul’s instructions about hair in his first letter to the Corinthians, because he was addressing former pagans who needed to leave those old associations behind.

The discussion also covers a passage from the book of Acts, chapter twenty-one, where Saint Paul participates in Jewish temple rituals, including Nazarite vows and sacrifices, after the resurrection of Christ. This challenges the idea that all temple sacrifices were immediately replaced by the Eucharist. It shows a period of continuity for Jewish Christians.

Finally, the hosts reflect on the nature of asceticism itself. They are clear that asceticism is not about rejecting creation as evil. It is about giving up good things for better things. Fasting, vigils, and almsgiving are ways to discipline the passions so that good things do not become snares. They also discuss the monastic tonsure, the cutting of the hair during profession, which is the material act of the sacrament of monasticism, symbolizing a new beginning and a self-offering to God.

The episode closes with a powerful reminder that Christianity is not just a set of beliefs. It is a way of life, a way of being. Monasticism embodies this in a profound way, but all Christians are called to embody their faith in their daily lives. The practices of the monks are not irrelevant to the rest of us. They show us what it means to give everything to God.
