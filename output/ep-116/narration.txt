Father Andrew and Father Stephen wrap up their three-part series on the history of Israel in this episode, picking up with the return of the Jewish exiles under Cyrus the Great. Cyrus, the founder of the Persian Empire, is actually called “Messiah” in the Hebrew Scriptures because he allowed the people to go back to Judea and rebuild the Temple. That sets the stage for what we call Second Temple Judaism, a period that runs from the rebuilding of the Temple around 515 BC all the way to its destruction in AD 70. During this time, the Jewish people developed synagogues and a way of life known as Ioudaismos, which simply means the Judean way of life, encompassing everything according to the Torah. This was not just a religion but a whole identity shaped by the diaspora, the dispersion of the people outside Judea.

Then came Alexander the Great, who conquered everything and spread Hellenistic culture. After his death, his empire split into the Seleucid and Ptolemaic kingdoms, and Judea got caught in the middle. The real trouble started with Antiochus IV Epiphanes, a Seleucid king who persecuted the Jews terribly. He desecrated the Temple by sacrificing a pig on the altar to Zeus, which is the abomination of desolation mentioned in Daniel and the Gospels. That sparked the Maccabean Revolt, led by Judas Maccabeus, also known as Judah the Hammer. He recaptured and rededicated the Temple, which is what Hanukkah commemorates.

The revolt eventually led to the Hasmonean dynasty, a line of priest-kings who ruled Judea. But here is the key point the fathers make: the Hasmoneans were illegitimate. They combined the roles of king and high priest, which the Torah forbids, and they were not from the line of Zadok. This caused real problems, especially with the Pharisees. Now, the fathers are very clear that the Pharisees are not the bad guys in the New Testament. Jesus never says they are wrong about anything, only that they are hypocrites. The Pharisees were actually the good guys, faithful to the Torah and waiting for God to intervene. The Sadducees, on the other hand, were the priestly aristocracy descended from the Hasmonean line. They rejected the oral traditions, did not believe in resurrection, and were often in conflict with the Pharisees.

The Hasmoneans expanded the kingdom by conquering Samaria and Idumea, and they destroyed the Samaritan temple on Mount Gerizim. That is why the Samaritans, who had mixed ancestry and their own version of the Torah, were such rivals to the Judeans. Eventually, Rome took over, and Herod the Great, an Idumean, became the client king. He was not a Judean but an Edomite, and he is famous for his massive building projects, including the expansion of the Second Temple.

But the fathers want us to see the bigger theological picture. The restoration of Israel was never just about Judah. The prophets Ezekiel and Jeremiah spoke of the whole house of Israel, including the northern kingdom. Ezekiel’s vision of the dry bones in chapter thirty-seven is about the restoration of all twelve tribes. Jeremiah thirty-one talks about the watchmen calling Ephraim to go up to Zion. And in Genesis forty-eight, Jacob blesses Ephraim, saying his descendants will become the fullness of the nations. St. Paul picks up on all of this in Romans.

In Romans nine, Paul uses the potter metaphor, but the fathers stress that this is not teaching double predestination. Paul is referencing Jeremiah eighteen, where God’s plans for nations are conditional on their repentance. Then in Romans eleven, Paul gives us the olive tree metaphor. There is only one olive tree, meaning there is only one Israel. The natural branches are the Jews, and the wild branches are the Gentiles who are grafted in. The wild branches do not become natural branches; they remain distinct but draw life from the same root. This means the church is not a replacement for Israel but a grafting into the same tree. The fathers also critique modern ideologies like dispensationalism and supersessionism, because both get this wrong. There is only one Israel, and the fullness of the nations is part of God’s plan to restore all of it in Christ.
