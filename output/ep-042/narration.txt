Father Andrew and Father Stephen open this episode by tackling a question that weighs on many minds: what actually happens when we die? They start by clearing away a common modern assumption—that we immediately go to heaven or hell forever. That idea, they say, is not what the Orthodox Church teaches. Instead, there is an intermediate state, a waiting period between death and the final resurrection.

To understand this, the fathers take us back to the ancient world. The Hebrews spoke of Sheol, and the Greeks spoke of Hades. In Christian tradition, these words are essentially synonyms. Both refer to the underworld, a shadowy, gloomy place where everyone went after death—not a place of conscious torment, but a dim and undesirable existence. The distinction we often draw between hell and Hades is a modern invention. The Old Testament paints this picture vividly, especially in the Psalms of the Sons of Korah. Psalm eighty-eight describes the despair of the dead in Sheol, cut off from God’s presence. Yet these same psalms also carry a hope for deliverance. Psalm sixteen, verse ten, says God will not abandon His Holy One to Hades. The apostles later read this as a prophecy of Christ’s resurrection.

That hope points to something bigger. The fathers explain that the coming of the Messiah was the first stage of God’s plan, inaugurating the Messianic Age. But there is a second stage yet to come—the end of days, when the final judgment and the new creation will occur. This is what they call a two-stage eschatology. Christ’s resurrection and His descent into Hades affected all humanity. He liberated the righteous and led them to paradise, fulfilling the hopes of the Old Testament. This is the Harrowing of Hades, and it began the general resurrection, which will one day include both the righteous and the wicked.

Now, what about the soul in the meantime? The fathers push back against Platonic and Gnostic ideas that crept into Christian thinking—especially the notion that the soul is immortal by nature and that the body is a prison. They insist that the soul is not a physical object but the principle of life. All living things have souls. Genesis chapter one describes the creation of living creatures as the creation of souls, so animals have souls too. Humans have a noetic soul, the highest faculty of which can know God. And larger entities, like communities or nations, can have what the fathers call macro-souls—a collective life that is more than the sum of its parts, and which can be holy or sinful.

This leads them to critique the beatific vision, a Western theological concept influenced by Platonism, which claims the soul can behold the essence of God immediately after death. The fathers argue this is incompatible with Orthodox theology. The hope is not a disembodied vision but the bodily resurrection and the transformation of the whole cosmos. The parable of the rich man and Lazarus in Luke sixteen reflects the intermediate state, with the righteous in paradise and the wicked in Hades, but it is a parable, not a literal map of the afterlife.

They also discuss the first resurrection mentioned in Revelation twenty. This is not a physical event but the spiritual resurrection of the soul to life in Christ, which happens at death for the righteous. The bodily resurrection comes later, at the end of time. The fathers even bring up their friend Bart Ehrman, a New Testament scholar, to note that the Bible does not make scientific or historical claims in the modern sense. It conveys truth through narrative and theological proclamation. Trying to prove the resurrection through modern historical methods is misguided.

Finally, they turn to practical life. Eternal condemnation is a real possibility for every person, but it is not a certainty for anyone. That possibility should spur us to repentance, not to condemn others. The fathers close by reminding us that the Christian life is a struggle, and the hope of resurrection gives that struggle meaning.
