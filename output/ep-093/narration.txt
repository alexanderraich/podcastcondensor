In this episode, Fathers Andrew and Stephen begin with a helpful distinction. An ascension is a bodily ascent into heaven while a person is still alive, like Enoch or Elijah. An assumption is when a person’s body is taken up after death, as with Moses and the Theotokos. They point out that the tradition of Moses’ assumption is clearly referenced in the New Testament, especially in the Epistle of Jude, where the Archangel Michael disputes with the devil over Moses’ body. This dispute is rooted in the devil’s claim to human bodies because of the curse in Genesis, where he is told he will eat dust. The devil, as the eater of the dead, argues that he has a right to corrupt human flesh. But the righteous are delivered from that corruption through resurrection.

The fathers also discuss the Orthodox understanding of the Dormition of the Theotokos. The Orthodox Church teaches that she died, and her body was assumed into heaven. This is contrasted with the Roman Catholic dogma, which allows for ambiguity but leans toward the idea that she did not die at all. The fathers emphasize that her death and assumption are consistent with the hope of bodily resurrection for all the righteous.

They then turn to the Ascension of Isaiah, a composite text with both Jewish and Christian sections. It contains an apocalyptic vision of the prophet Isaiah, who is martyred by being sawn in two, a detail referenced in Hebrews, where it mentions prophets who were sawn in two. The text includes early Christian Trinitarian thought, with worship directed to the Father, Son, and Holy Spirit. This shows that Trinitarian belief existed in the first century, not as a later invention but as an early confession. The fathers also explore the meaning of garments in this text. In the Ascension of Isaiah, garments symbolize the transformed, immortal resurrection body. This is the same language Paul uses in First Corinthians, where he compares the resurrection body to a seed that becomes a plant. It is not about imputed righteousness but about the actual transformation of the person.

The Assumption of Moses is a testament attributed to Moses, likely written in the early first century. It uses Moses as a literary vehicle to critique the corrupt priesthood of that time. This kind of pseudepigraphy is not fraudulent. It is an interpretive device, using a revered figure to speak into a later situation. The text expands on themes from Psalm eighty-two, where God judges the gods and exalts the righteous. The fathers reflect on the exaltation of humanity, noting that the Christian hope is not merely going to heaven when you die. It is bodily resurrection and exaltation to be like the angels.

They also discuss the phenomenon of incorrupt relics, where the bodies of saints do not decay. This is a sign of the bodily resurrection and a foretaste of what is to come. The devil’s claim to human bodies is broken by Christ’s resurrection, and the righteous are delivered from corruption.

Finally, the fathers reflect on intellectual humility. They stress the need to trust others and institutions, especially the Church, rather than relying solely on one’s own understanding. This is not blind obedience but a recognition that we are not meant to figure everything out alone. The tradition of the Church, passed down through the ages, is a gift that helps us see clearly. The episode closes with the reminder that the destiny of humanity is not disembodied bliss but the resurrection of the body and life in the age to come.
