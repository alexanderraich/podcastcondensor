This episode is about a man most Christians have heard of and almost none have actually reckoned with. Ezra. The scribe, the priest, the reformer who came back from Babylon and, in a real sense, built the world that the New Testament would be born into. And the question the hosts are working through is this: why is Ezra so central to the story of Israel and the Second Temple, and yet so strangely absent from the way most modern Christians read their Old Testament? The answer takes them into the canon itself, into the question of what was actually considered Scripture and by whom, and into a whole library of books that bear Ezra's name.

They open with a reading from the forty-eighth chapter of 1 Enoch, where the Son of Man is named before the Lord of Spirits, before the creation of the stars, and the hosts set the tone for the show: that the world we see is not the whole of it, that there is an unseen reality running underneath and through everything, and that the Scriptures are a window into that enchanted world.

Then they settle in. It is the sixth anniversary of the podcast, and they are recording live, with callers joining in the second half. They joke about their age, about Emilio Estevez films, and then they do something they have done before on anniversaries: they read their negative reviews. One listener complains that the show is just an Orthodox recruiting pitch. Another, more memorably, calls one of the hosts the false prophet. They take it in good humor, and then they get to work.

Who is Ezra? That is where the substance begins. Ezra is one of the pivotal figures of the post-exilic period, the era after the Babylonian exile, when the people of Judah came back to the land and had to figure out what it meant to be the people of God in a world where the temple was rebuilt but the glory seemed to have departed. And here is the odd thing. In the Protestant canon, this entire period is a kind of doughnut hole. You have the exile, you have the return, and then you have four hundred silent years before the New Testament opens. But those years were not silent. They were some of the most formative years in the history of God's people. And the Hebrew canon itself, the hosts point out, seems to have deliberately excluded the Hasmonean material, the books that would have covered the Maccabean revolt and its aftermath. There are multiple versions of the late Old Testament texts. The picture is not as tidy as most people assume.

So who was Ezra? His name means "Yahweh is my helper." He was a scribe, a priest, and likely a high priest. In the Greek tradition he is Esdras, in the Latin likewise, and the tradition credits him with an astonishing range of work. The Talmud says he wrote the book of Ezra, edited Nehemiah, wrote Chronicles, and possibly even Malachi. One of the hosts is skeptical about the Malachi attribution, but the point stands: Ezra was remembered as a foundational author and editor of Scripture itself.

His historical role is concrete. He was sent by the Persian king Artaxerxes in 457 BC. Nehemiah was sent later, in 444 BC, as governor. And what Ezra did when he arrived reshaped the identity of God's people for centuries. He formed the Great Assembly, the proto-Sanhedrin. He began the synagogue system. And most importantly, he reoriented Judean identity around Torah observance rather than around the temple and the land. This is the birth of Judaism as we know it. The word "Jew" means "Judean," but after Ezra, a Judean could be a Judean anywhere in the world, because the identity was no longer tied to living in Judea. It was tied to Torah. The temple was relativized within this new ethos. You could be faithful to God in Alexandria, in Egypt, in Mesopotamia, because the Torah went with you.

This is why so many books claim Ezra as their author. Ten or twelve books bear his name in one tradition or another. His importance in rabbinic Judaism and in historic Christianity was enormous, even if Protestant culture has largely forgotten him. The pseudepigraphic attribution makes sense when you realize how central he was.

After a break, they return to more reviews and then to the callers. The first caller, Paul, asks about how the Pharisees and Sadducees attained their authority. This is a question that connects back to earlier episodes on the Hasmonean period and on ordination and apostolic succession. The Sadducees, the hosts explain, were essentially the high-priestly families of the Zedokite line who had hijacked the priesthood and become collaborators with Rome. The Pharisees claimed a different kind of authority: Mosaic succession through chains of ordination, teacher to student, going back to Moses himself. Those chains were broken in the first century. And Christians believe that the Messiah restored them through the apostles. This is the mechanism of authority that stands behind the whole question of who gets to speak for God.

The next caller, Kota, asks about the divine council and Cyrus. And here the hosts open up something important. The return from exile was incomplete. Why? Because Persia still ruled. The people came back to the land, but they were still under the dominion of a pagan empire, and in the divine council framework that the hosts have developed across many episodes, the nations are under evil spiritual powers. The exile was not just geographical. It was spiritual. And Cyrus, though he is called God's anointed in Isaiah, is like Saul: chosen by God for a purpose, but not thereby justified or made a saint. The exile continued until Christ defeated the powers. This is the framework within which the whole post-exilic period makes sense.

Then they turn to the textual question, and this is where things get really interesting. The books of Ezra are more complicated than most people realize. In the Hebrew canon, Ezra and Nehemiah are one scroll. But there is another version, 1 Esdras, which is a single continuous text that reorders the material and includes the third-person Nehemiah chapters to make one flowing narrative. Both versions begin with a verbatim quote of the end of 2 Chronicles. And here is the claim that the hosts make: 1 Esdras, not Ezra-Nehemiah, was the canonical Ezra for Josephus and for every extant church father who comments on the subject. The switch to Ezra-Nehemiah happened in the late second century AD. This means that the Protestant Old Testament canon is demonstrably not the canon of any Jewish group before the second century. Protestants often quote Josephus to support their canon, but Josephus actually names 1 Esdras.

The numbering is confusing, so they lay it out. 1 Esdras is 1 Ezra. English Ezra is 2 Ezra. Nehemiah is 3 Ezra. And 4 Ezra is 2 Esdras in the Latin tradition. Jerome translated 1 Esdras as the canonical Ezra and placed 4 Ezra in the deuterocanonical section, alongside Tobit, Judith, and Maccabees. The Orthodox Study Bible includes 1, 2, and 3 Ezra plus Ezra-Nehemiah, which the hosts criticize as not matching the historical testimony.

And then they make a provocation. Is Nehemiah even canonical? From a Protestant sola scriptura standpoint, it is hard to defend. Nehemiah is never read publicly in Orthodox liturgy. No church father testifies to its canonicity; they refer to 1 Esdras as "Ezra." The Latin Vulgate and the Slavonic Bible do not have Ezra-Nehemiah as canonical; they have 1 and 2 Esdras. The only sources that testify to the canonicity of the non-1 Esdras parts of Nehemiah are rabbinic Jewish sources after the second century. Canonical arguments are post-hoc. No one starts from a blank slate. And sola scriptura requires a clear canon, which is historically unattainable.

This leads to a broader point. The Orthodox Church does not construct doctrine from the Bible, so canonical differences are tolerable. The hosts critique both Protestant and Roman Catholic attempts to use canon as a weapon. Rome had no single Old Testament canon until Trent; Florence declared one, but it was debated for a century. Protestants hold their canon because it is the tradition they received, not because of historical argument. The Orthodox Church tolerates canonical diversity because it does not base doctrine on a fixed list.

Then they return to the content of Ezra. The public reading of the Torah by Ezra is the foundation of synagogue worship and of Jewish identity. And there is a language dimension here that matters. The people spoke Aramaic, not Hebrew, so the Torah had to be interpreted when it was read. Aramaic had become the imperial language of the Persian Empire, not through violence but through the displacement of Aramean merchants. This is why Jesus and the apostles spoke Aramaic. It is why the first-century Judea of the Gospels is an Aramaic-speaking world.

And then there is the intermarriage crisis, which is the concrete crisis in 1 Esdras. The returning Judean men had married local women without conversion, and this threatened to dissolve Judean identity entirely. The Northern Kingdom had ceased to exist because of intermarriage after deportation. If the same thing happened to the returning exiles, there would be no people of God left. So Ezra required conversion. Identity had to be propagated generationally, even outside Judea. This is the concrete example of Ezra forming Jewish identity as a concept.

After more anniversary banter and review readings, the callers return. Ryan asks whether Christ was depictable before the Incarnation. The hosts answer that Moses and others saw a form but did not make depictions, likely because of the people's propensity to idolatry. After the Incarnation, we have depictions of Christ from the apostolic era. This connects back to earlier episodes on theophanies.

Ryan also asks about the patristic definition of canon. And here the hosts give a detailed answer. The Church fathers did not produce a single definitive canon list. They spoke of books that were publicly read, books that were privately read, and books that were not to be read. St. Athanasius's 367 Paschal letter is the earliest list of twenty-seven New Testament books, but it was written to exclude Gnostic texts, not to define a closed canon. The Nag Hammadi library was buried when Athanasius drew those lines. St. Nicephorus in the ninth century gives a more complex list. Some churches in the ninth century publicly read the Apocalypse of Peter instead of Revelation. The Ethiopian and Coptic canons were pre-Chalcedonian and never an issue of division. The Church fathers sometimes quoted the Gospel of Thomas as authentic sayings of Christ. The Church never divided over canon.

Michael asks about Ezra and Malachi on divorce. Ezra demanded that the men divorce their foreign wives. Malachi condemns divorce. Is this a contradiction? The hosts explain that these are two prophets applying the same principles to different situations. Scripture contains apparent contradictions because texts must be interpreted and applied. The prophet's role is to apply God's word bindingly to specific situations. Malachi's condemnation of divorce may be a dispute with Ezra's policy. But the goal of both prophets is the salvation of the people involved. Malachi's "thus says the Lord" is not verbatim quotation but speaking with God-given authority.

Then they turn to 4 Ezra, also known as 2 Esdras. This is a semi-canonical text, in the Slavonic appendix and the Ethiopic Bible. It was originally written in Hebrew and Aramaic around the end of the first century AD by a non-Christian Jewish author, and later Christianized in the Latin version. It is cited authoritatively by church fathers like Ambrose, and famously by Christopher Columbus, who quoted 2 Esdras 6:42 to argue that the earth is seven-eighths land and one-eighth water, and used it to estimate the distance to Japan.

The Christian additions to the Latin version are divided into 5th Ezra, the first two chapters, and 6th Ezra, the last two chapters. These are not in the Slavonic or Ethiopic versions. They are obvious Christian additions.

The Archangel Uriel is a major figure in 4 Ezra and other Second Temple literature. He is one of the seven archangels, along with Michael, Gabriel, and Raphael. Tradition holds that he rescued John the Forerunner and Elizabeth from Herod's men. He is the angel in Leonardo's Virgin of the Rocks with John and Elizabeth. He accompanied the Holy Family on the flight into Egypt, attested in Pseudomathia and Coptic liturgics. He appears in the Apocalypse of Peter as the angel of judgment. Dionysius and Gregory the Theologian list Uriel among the seven archangels.

The core of 4 Ezra, chapters 3 through 14, contains seven visions. The first three are Ezra questioning God's justice. Why does the exile continue under Persia? Why was Judah exiled in the first place? Why are pagan nations ruled by evil powers? God's answer is twofold: divine ways are beyond human understanding, and judgment is coming to set things right. This is Job-like questioning, not abstract theology.

The fourth vision is the bridegroom. A bridegroom dies at the threshold of the bridal chamber. His mother mourns and is transformed into the city of Zion. This imagery is reflected in Orthodox hymnography for Holy Week and Pascha, especially regarding the Theotokos, who is both mother and city.

The fifth vision is the eagle. A three-headed eagle with twelve and eight wings, identified with the fourth beast of Daniel's prophecy: Rome, specifically the Flavian dynasty under Domitian. The lion of Judah, the Messiah, kills and burns the eagle. The sixth vision shows the Messiah burning a mob and finding new disciples among the Gentiles, prefiguring the kingdom being taken from Israel and given to the Gentiles.

The seventh vision describes Ezra restoring the lost Torah and all the scriptures, twenty-four books, word for word after the Babylonian destruction. This tradition is also found in the Epistle of Barnabas. St. Jerome refers to it in his preface to the Pentateuch, but his comment was later misused by nineteenth-century German scholars to claim that Jerome doubted Mosaic authorship. The hosts debunk this and warn against repeating unverified claims, even from Orthodox sources. The seventy secret books mentioned in 4 Ezra likely refers to the Septuagint, the apocryphal books of the seventy.

There is also the Greek Apocalypse of Ezra, a Christian text that mentions Paul and John. It lists nine archangels, including unusual names like Gabuthelon, Akr, Ourphoukatonos, Beboros, and Zebulion. It is a tour of heaven and hell where Ezra questions God about evil and free will. God answers that humans have free will and that He took flesh to forgive sins.

The Vision of Ezra and the Questions of Ezra are medieval texts that continue the same theme of Ezra questioning God about the problem of evil. And the Revelation of Ezra is a ninth-century Christian text, not an apocalypse but a calendar logion, a genre that predicts weather, crops, and fertility based on the day of the week on which New Year and feasts fall. This genre exists in Jewish and Christian literature, for example the Treatise of Shem from the first century BC and the Dead Sea Scroll known as 4Q Cryptic, which predicts physiognomy based on astrological sign at birth.

And then they land where the episode has been heading all along. Orthodox tradition is not a static encyclopedia or a code of law. It is a living, experienced tradition. The variety of Ezra texts illustrates this. Liturgical texts like the Kontakion and the Canon are not absolute dogma. We must let go of the need to categorize everything, especially God. Humility is necessary to accept that we cannot understand everything. The point is not to master the data. The point is to enter into the life that the data bears witness to. And that life is inexhaustible.
