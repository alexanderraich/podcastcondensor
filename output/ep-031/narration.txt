In this episode, Fathers Andrew Stephen Damick and Stephen De Young continue their series on Old Testament Christology, focusing on a phrase that appears again and again in the Scriptures: the word of the Lord. And their first, bold claim is this: that phrase never refers to the Bible itself. When the prophets speak of the word of the Lord, they are not talking about a book. They are talking about a divine person. That person is the pre-incarnate Christ, the Son of God, appearing and acting in the Old Testament before His birth in Bethlehem.

The hosts are careful to correct a common misunderstanding. Many modern readers, especially in the Protestant world, tend to think of the word of God as the written text. But in the Bible, the word of the Lord is a living, personal being. He appears to prophets, He creates, and He reveals the Father. He is distinct from the Father, yet He is fully God.

To make this point, they walk through several key passages. In Genesis chapter fifteen, the word of the Lord appears to Abram in a vision. Abram speaks to this figure and calls Him Lord Yahweh. And the figure does not correct him. He accepts the worship and the title, showing that this word is indeed Yahweh Himself. In the first book of Samuel, chapter three, we read about the boy Samuel. The word of the Lord appears to him bodily, standing by his bed. Samuel comes to know Yahweh through seeing this word. It is a personal, physical encounter. And in the first chapter of Jeremiah, the word of the Lord comes to the prophet, speaks in the first person as Yahweh, and touches his mouth. Again, this is not a message. This is a person.

Now, this brings us to the Gospel of John. The hosts argue that when John writes about the Logos, the Word, he is not borrowing from Greek philosophy. This is a common scholarly assumption, but they insist it is wrong. John is rooted in the Old Testament and in the traditions of Second Temple Judaism. The Dead Sea Scrolls confirm this way of thinking. Even Philo of Alexandria, the Hellenistic Jewish philosopher, reflects this Jewish understanding of the divine word as an intermediary, not a Greek abstraction.

So when John opens his Gospel with, In the beginning was the Word, he is identifying Jesus as the same divine person who appeared to Abram, to Samuel, and to Jeremiah. This Word was with God, and this Word was God. He created all things. And then, the most astonishing claim of all, this Word became flesh and dwelt among us. The pre-incarnate Christ, who spoke to the prophets, who touched Jeremiah’s mouth, who stood by Samuel’s bed, took on human nature and was born of the Virgin Mary.

The hosts also draw a beautiful connection between the light of creation and the life of Christ. In the first chapter of Genesis, God says, Let there be light. That light, they argue, is the life of Christ. It is the same light that shines in the darkness in John’s Gospel. And this light was sent for a purpose. It was sent to make humanity children of God. From the very beginning, the goal of creation was theosis, the deification of human beings. We were not created just to exist. We were created to become partakers of the divine nature, to be adopted as sons and daughters of God through the Word made flesh.

John the Baptist appears in this Gospel as the paradigm prophet. He is the last and greatest of the Old Testament prophets, the one who testifies to the light. He points to Jesus and says, Behold the Lamb of God. And in doing so, he fulfills the role of every prophet who ever received the word of the Lord.

The episode closes with a call to see Christ as the rational order behind all creation and all knowledge. He is not just a figure in a book. He is the Word through whom everything was made, and He is the one who reveals the Father to us. When we read the Old Testament, we should look for Him. When we read the New Testament, we should see Him. And when we come to know Him, we come to know the God who has always been speaking, from the very beginning.
