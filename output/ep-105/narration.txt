In this episode, Father Andrew and Father Stephen take up a question that goes to the very heart of the Christian faith: what does it mean that God became man? They start by looking back at some of the earliest arguments about Jesus, especially a heresy called Docetism. The name comes from a Greek word, *dokeo*, which means to seem or to appear. The Docetists taught that Jesus only seemed to be human, that his body was not real. The hosts make a strong point here: the existence of these Docetist communities in the first century actually challenges a popular modern idea. Some scholars, like Bart Ehrman, argue that Jesus was first seen as a mere man and only later, over time, was he thought of as divine. But Father Andrew and Father Stephen say that’s backwards. If anything, the earliest debates were not about whether Jesus was God, but about whether he was truly human. And they are careful to note that Docetism should not be confused with Gnosticism. The Docetists were likely Jewish Christians who thought of Jesus as a kind of theophany, not people who believed matter was evil.

That leads them to the Old Testament, where God appears in physical ways again and again. He walks in the garden in Genesis, He eats with Abraham at Mamre, He stands before Samuel, and He reaches out His hand to touch Jeremiah’s mouth. In Exodus, Moses and the elders see God, and there is a pavement under His feet. They even eat and drink in His presence. So the question becomes: who is this God who appears so physically? The hosts connect this to a concept from Second Temple Judaism sometimes called the Second Yahweh, a divine being distinct from the Father, often identified with the Son. They also bring in the Aramaic term *Memra*, the Word, which was used in the ancient translations to describe God’s visible manifestation. This is the same Word, the *Logos*, that John tells us became flesh.

And here is the heart of their argument. The incarnation is not a change in God. It is not as if the Son added something new to Himself at a certain point in history. Rather, Christ’s humanity is eternal. It exists outside of time and space. That is how His feet could walk in Eden, and that is how He can be called the Lamb slain from the foundation of the world, as we read in Revelation. His sacrifice is not just a historical event; it is rooted in the eternal purpose of creation. The hosts even point to the icon of the Creation of Adam, where Christ and Adam share the same face. Humanity is made according to Christ, who is the image of God. The incarnation is not an afterthought or a rescue plan. It is the center and the purpose of everything.

They also touch on some related teachings. They mention the early heretic Cerinthus, who taught that Jesus was a man upon whom the divine Logos descended at his baptism, a form of adoptionism. And they bring up Ignatius of Antioch, a disciple of the Apostle John, who wrote letters insisting on the reality of Christ’s flesh. The hosts also note that most Protestants believe in the virgin conception but not the perpetual virginity of Mary, which they see as a departure from historic Christian teaching. Mary is the Theotokos, the God-bearer, and they connect her to the Ark of the Covenant.

Finally, they address a common question: when we say God died on the cross, what do we mean? They clarify that the person Jesus Christ died, in the sense that His human soul was separated from His body. The divine nature did not die. And they point to the many scriptures that emphasize the flesh of Christ, from Romans and Hebrews to First John, where confessing Jesus come in the flesh is the very test of the Spirit of God. The episode ends with a reflection on Ephesians, where we are told we have already been raised up and seated with Christ in heavenly places. The incarnation is not just something that happened long ago. It is the eternal reality that shapes all of creation, and we are invited into it.
