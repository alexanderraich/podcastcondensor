In this episode, Father Andrew and Father Stephen tackle a big question: are the pagan gods real? They start with a verse that might surprise you. Psalm ninety-six, verse five, in the Greek translation of the Old Testament, says plainly that all the gods of the nations are demons. That’s not a metaphor. The hosts argue that the Bible takes these gods seriously as real, dangerous spiritual beings. They also point to the Apostle Paul, who wrote in First Corinthians that what the pagans sacrifice, they sacrifice to demons. And in Deuteronomy, Moses says they sacrificed to demons, not to God.

The hosts then dig into the Hebrew word for these false gods: *elil*. It means worthless, or vain. It’s a dismissive word, but it doesn’t mean they don’t exist. It means they’re worthless compared to the true God. They also mention a Babylonian loanword, *shedu*, which refers to a territorial spirit. That word shows up in Deuteronomy thirty-two, again for demons.

Now, here’s where things get interesting. The hosts argue that our modern word “monotheism” doesn’t really fit the biblical view. That term was only invented in the seventeenth century. The Bible doesn’t say there’s only one spiritual being. It says Yahweh is in a completely different category from all other spiritual beings. He’s the Creator. They’re creatures. So the biblical picture is more like this: there are many spiritual powers, but only one God.

They lay out three categories of demons. First, there are the Watchers. These are the angels who fell by teaching forbidden knowledge to humans. They’re now imprisoned in the Abyss, which the Greek New Testament calls Tartarus. That’s the same prison the Greeks used for the Titans. Second, there are unclean spirits. These are the disembodied spirits of the Nephilim, the giants born from the Watchers and human women. Third, there are the gods of the nations. These are fallen angels who were placed over the peoples after the Tower of Babel. They accepted worship and became demons. The hosts compare this to Greek mythology, like Prometheus, who gave fire to humans and was punished by Zeus. That story echoes the Watchers.

So how does idolatry actually work? The hosts explain that an idol is like a body for a spirit. Through rituals, like the “opening of the nostrils,” the spirit is invited to dwell in the image. That’s why the Ark of the Covenant had no idol. It wasn’t a technique to control God. It was a place of meeting, built out of love and obedience. Idolatry, by contrast, is a technique. It’s a way to manipulate spiritual forces for your own gain. You use the idol as an access point to control the god.

And here’s the kicker: idolatry changes you. Psalm one hundred fifteen says that idols have mouths but cannot speak, eyes but cannot see, ears but cannot hear. And those who make them become like them. The worshipper becomes powerless and lifeless, just like the idol. Isaiah forty-four makes fun of this. A man plants a tree, cuts it down, uses half for firewood to bake bread, and the other half he carves into a god and bows down to it. The craftsman is clearly more powerful than the thing he made.

The hosts also clarify something about the golden calf. That wasn’t worshiping a different god. It was an attempt to worship Yahweh through an idol. That’s the real sin. They wanted to control God, to make him manageable. And that’s still a danger today. Modern idolatry isn’t always carved statues. It can be treating God as a means to an end, as a tool to get what you want. It’s trying to control outcomes instead of trusting God’s future.

Christian worship is the opposite. It doesn’t try to control God. It uses images, yes, but in a different way. Icons connect us to the true God through love and humility. They don’t manipulate. They open us up to transformation. And that transformation is the whole point. Idolatry makes you like the idol—dead and powerless. But worshiping Christ makes you like him. You become more alive, more loving, more human. The hosts close by urging us to examine our own hearts. Are we trying to use God, or are we letting him shape us? That’s the real question.
