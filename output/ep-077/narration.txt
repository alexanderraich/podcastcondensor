This episode of the Lord of Spirits podcast is a special one, recorded for those who couldn’t make it to the conference. Father Andrew Stephen Damick takes the opportunity to explore a theme that feels incredibly modern, but is actually as old as civilization itself: the fear of missing out, or FOMO. And he does it by taking us all the way back to the second millennium before Christ, to ancient Mesopotamia.

The whole episode is framed around a simple, humorous question: what would it be like to miss a party in ancient Mesopotamia? Father Andrew presents three ancient texts that give us a glimpse into exactly that. The first is an Acadian tablet, written in cuneiform, which tells the story of a cleaner who is excluded from a celebration because his clothing is not proper for the occasion. It’s a reminder that the social anxiety of showing up underdressed is not a new invention. The second text is even more absurd. It describes a slave woman who adopted a monkey child, and apparently, that was a reason for her to be left out of the festivities. It sounds like a joke, but it’s a real historical record, and it shows that the reasons for social exclusion can be strange and arbitrary.

But the real highlight of the episode is the third text. Father Andrew shares a translation of what is essentially a stand-up comedy routine from ancient Mesopotamia, dating to the early first millennium BC. It’s a jester’s routine, and it gives us a rare look at the style and content of ancient humor. The jokes are self-deprecating and often involve absurd comparisons. And, as Father Andrew points out, they could be quite offensive and cross lines, much like modern comedy. The point is that people in ancient Mesopotamia experienced the same social pressures we do. They worried about being left out, they worried about what others thought of them, and they used humor to cope with those anxieties.

The episode opens with a quote from the book of First Enoch, specifically chapter forty-eight, verses four and five, which praises the Lord of Spirits. This sets a theological tone for the whole discussion. Even when we’re laughing at ancient jokes about monkey children and bad clothing, we’re reminded that the human condition is consistent across the ages. We are all still trying to find our place in the community, and we all still fear being left out of the party.

Father Andrew’s presentation is lighthearted, but it’s rooted in serious historical and theological reflection. He’s not just sharing funny stories from the past. He’s showing us that the continuity of human experience is a profound thing. The same fears, the same social rules, the same desire to belong—it’s all there, written in wedge-shaped marks on clay tablets. The episode is a reminder that we are not so different from our ancestors. We might not have adopted monkey children, but we still worry about our clothes, we still tell bad jokes, and we still feel that pang of fear when we think we might be missing out on something important. And that, in itself, is a kind of comfort. It connects us to the long story of humanity, and it reminds us that our struggles are shared, even across thousands of years.
