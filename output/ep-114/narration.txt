In this episode, Father Andrew and Father Stephen kick off a three-part series on the history of Israel. Their goal is to give you a framework for understanding the whole Bible. They start where the story starts, with the promises God made to Abraham. Now, we often think those promises were about land and descendants, but the hosts make a bold claim. The ultimate fulfillment isn't the dirt or the family tree. It’s theosis. It’s becoming like the stars of heaven. That’s the real goal.

They introduce a way of reading prophecy called a sequence of signs. The idea is that an immediate event, like the birth of Isaac, is a real fulfillment. But it’s also a sign pointing to something much bigger down the road. The birth of Isaac confirms the promise, but it points to Christ. They use the same logic for the virgin birth prophecy in Isaiah. It had a near-term sign, but the ultimate fulfillment is Jesus.

Then they get into a fascinating category that most people miss. There are non-Israelite Abrahamites. Think of the Edomites, the Moabites, and the Ammonites. These are descendants of Abraham, but they are not Israelites. And they are not Gentiles either. They are their own thing. And here’s the kicker. They initially worshiped Yahweh. God gave them land, and He told Israel not to harass them. In Deuteronomy, God says He has given Mount Seir to Esau, and He has given land to Lot’s descendants. So these nations had a real relationship with God, even though they weren’t part of the covenant people of Israel.

This leads to a heavy critique of dispensationalism. Father Andrew and Father Stephen are clear that the modern state of Israel is not the biblical Israel. There is no direct connection between them. They even argue that dispensationalism is inherently anti-Semitic, because it treats Jews as tools for end-times prophecy rather than as people who need salvation. They also stress that the Torah was never a way to earn salvation. It was never a merit system. It was given to Israel as a way of life, a nomos, that defined their society. It was unique to them. It wasn’t given to the other Abrahamite nations, and it wasn’t given to the Gentiles.

So how did Israel actually become a nation? It wasn’t by blood alone. It was through the Passover and the Exodus. The blood of the lamb on the doorposts marked the boundaries of the people. That shared experience, that deliverance, constituted them as a nation. And then the giving of the Torah at Sinai defined how they would live. The tribe of Dan is a great example of this messy reality. They might have originated from the Sea Peoples, the Danai, not primarily from biological descendants of Jacob. They were poorly assimilated and later fell into idolatry. So Israel was never a pure ethnic line. It was a people formed by God’s action in history.

The hosts wrap up by embracing the messiness of it all. History and theology are not neat and tidy. They are messy. And when you try to force them into a clean system, you get bad theology. You get things like dispensationalism. The promises to Abraham are fulfilled in Jesus Christ. He is the ultimate descendant, and through Him all nations are blessed. The righteous will shine like the stars in the resurrection, just as Daniel says. That’s the promise. That’s the goal. And that’s where the whole story is heading.
