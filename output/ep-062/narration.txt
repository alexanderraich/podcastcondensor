In this episode, Father Andrew and Father Stephen take on the big topic of kingship. They start with the very beginning, in Genesis, where God rests on the seventh day. That rest isn't just a nap. It means God is seated on His throne, presiding over all of creation. So the very pattern of kingship comes from God Himself. The Torah, the first five books of the Bible, is even structured like an ancient treaty between a great king and a lesser king. God is the Great King, and Israel is His vassal. The king of Israel, then, is a vassal of God. Deuteronomy seventeen lays out the rules for that king. He isn't supposed to acquire many horses, or many wives, or excessive wealth. He is to be an icon of God's rule, representing God to the people. But he is not an idol. He is not to be worshipped like the pagan kings who claimed to be gods.

This sets up the discussion of Constantine the Great. The hosts spend a good deal of time clearing away myths about him. They insist that Constantine genuinely converted to Christianity. His actions prove it. He stopped offering pagan sacrifices. He ended gladiatorial combat. He issued laws that reflected Christian values. They also tackle the many false claims about the Council of Nicaea. That council did not decide which books belong in the Bible. It did not create the Roman Catholic Church. It did not cover up any secret family of Jesus. It did not change the identity of the Jewish people. It did not burn books or people. It did not force Constantine's personal theology on anyone. In fact, Constantine might have been semi-Arian himself. The council did not proclaim Jesus to be divine for the first time. That was already believed. The debate was about the nature of His divinity. It did not move worship to Sunday, because Christians had gathered on the Lord's Day since the New Testament era. It did not start the practice of baptizing babies. And it could not have ended slavery, because that was not within its power, and the whole economy depended on it.

The hosts connect Constantine's conversion to the plan of St. Paul. Paul's goal was to preach the gospel to the emperor. Constantine's conversion was the fulfillment of that plan. The Byzantine emperor was not a mere layperson. He received communion at the altar with the clergy. This signified that his office was a ministry from God, similar to ordination. The coronation itself was a sacrament. The emperor was anointed with chrism and crowned on the ambo, the elevated platform where the gospel is read. The people would chant "Axios," meaning "worthy," just as they did at ordinations. This was the Byzantine ideal of symphonia. Church and state were not separate. They were partners. The emperor and the patriarch had different ministries, but they worked in harmony for a Christian society.

The hosts also bring in the story of King Saul, who failed by acting as a priest illegitimately. King David, on the other hand, is the model of kingship, a type of the Messiah. They mention the coronation of King Charles the Third, whose anointing oil was blessed by the Orthodox Patriarch of Jerusalem. This shows the ancient tradition still lives on. They also discuss the divine right of kings. This does not mean a king is above accountability. It means he has greater responsibility. Jesus told Pontius Pilate that his authority came from above. St. Paul wrote in Romans thirteen that governing authorities are instituted by God and are His servants. Psalm eighty-two is the standard by which all rulers are judged. It is about the gods, the fallen angels, who failed to do justice. The same standard applies to earthly kings.

The hosts conclude by applying these ancient principles to modern political life. The modern idea of separation of church and state did not exist in the early church. The Byzantine model saw the emperor and patriarch as partners. They remind listeners that Christ is the true King. Revelation shows the heavenly worship of the Lamb, who is worthy of all honor. Our hope is not in any earthly ruler. It is in Him. And in the meantime, we are called to live in community, honoring those in authority, while keeping our eyes fixed on the true King.
