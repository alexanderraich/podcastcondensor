In this episode, Fathers Andrew Stephen Damick and Stephen De Young take on the huge topic of time, and they start by poking at a popular modern myth. That myth is the idea of Western civilization as a simple blend of Christianity and paganism. They point to the Olympics as an example, where you can see paganism reasserting itself in our own day. But the heart of the conversation is about God and time. The core teaching is that God is not bound by time or space at all. Temporal categories simply do not apply to Him. If they did, He would be a creature, not God. His actions do show up in time and space for our benefit, but He Himself is completely beyond those limits.

From there, they dismantle a few theological systems that get this wrong. Calvinism, for example, is logically incoherent because it imagines God making decrees before creation, which implies God is stuck in time. Open theism is also false, because it makes God finite and subject to time, reducing Him to a creature. They also bring up the Greek word *proorizo*, which is often translated as predestined. But it really means to put in order in advance. When Saint Paul writes in Romans chapter eight, verse twenty-nine, he is not talking about God decreeing individuals to salvation or damnation. He is talking about God providing the way of salvation in advance. It is about order, not fatalism.

They also get into the biblical picture of time. The days of creation in Genesis, chapter one, are not literal twenty-four hour periods. They are manifestations of God’s work. And the seventh day, the Sabbath, represents God’s enthronement and His kingdom. The phrase from Revelation, chapter twenty-two, verse five, unto the ages of ages, refers to the age to come, which has no end. It is not a finite period. The liturgical calendar, with its cycles of feasts, is a way of structuring our experience of time. It is a means of theosis, uniting our human life with divine life.

Then they turn to the human experience of time. Time is not an objective thing out there in the world. It is phenomenological. It exists only in the realm of experience, like color or sound. Different creatures experience it differently. They draw on Saint Augustine, whose Confessions contain a brilliant analysis of time. He describes time as a tension between memory of the past and anticipation of the future, with the present being a slippery point of attention. They also bring in Saint John of Damascus, who discusses the limitations of angels, showing that even spiritual beings are not outside of time.

They also touch on apophatic theology, which is the approach of describing God by negation. This is a way of staying humble, recognizing that our language and concepts cannot fully capture God. We make negative statements about God to avoid idolatry. And they mention that God is impassible, meaning He is not capable of being acted upon or experiencing emotions the way creatures do. That is a quality of creatures, not of God.

Finally, they offer practical advice. The present moment is the only place where we can actually act. It is the only place where we can repent, heal the past, and build the future. Prayer should focus on the present. We are called to live in the now, with gratitude and hope, trusting that God is not bound by our time but is present with us in every moment.
