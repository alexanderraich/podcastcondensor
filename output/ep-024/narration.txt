In this episode, Fathers Andrew and Stephen pick up where they left off, diving deep into the theological foundations for why Orthodox Christians venerate relics. They start by clarifying what a body actually is, pushing back against the idea that it’s just a physical suit we wear. In the ancient understanding, a body is a nexus of powers and potentialities. That’s a fancy way of saying a body is defined by what it can do, not just by its material parts. They bring in Greek philosophical terms like nature, will, and energy to sharpen this. Nature is the set of powers and potentialities, will is the movement toward perfection, and energy is the actualization of that will in the world. This isn’t just abstract speculation—it’s how we understand Christ and how we understand our own salvation.

From there, they make a striking point. A human being is an animated body, and the material body is truly the person. You are not a soul trapped in flesh. You are your body, and your body is you. That’s why the identity of a person doesn’t depend on the exact matter involved—after all, your body replaces all its matter every seven years, yet you remain the same person. So what makes you you is something deeper, something noetic and spiritual, but it’s always expressed through the body.

Now here’s where it gets really interesting. Because humans are both noetic and material, we can serve as bodies for other spiritual beings. This can go one of two ways. There’s theosis, where a person does God’s works and becomes the body of Christ. And then there’s what the hosts call demonosis, where a person does the works of demons and becomes like them. They quote Saint John Chrysostom to back this up—he taught that souls of those who live in sin become demons, not those who die violently. And they connect this to First John, chapter three, verse eight, which says that those who practice sin are of the devil, and that Christ appeared to destroy the works of the devil. There’s no neutral ground. Your works are either God’s works or the devil’s works.

This is where the church comes in. The church corporately is the Body of Christ, animated by the Holy Spirit. They read from Ephesians, chapter one, verses twenty-two and twenty-three, which describes the church as the fullness of him who fills all in all. This isn’t a metaphor. It’s a reality. The church is the presence of Christ in the world, and holy tradition is the internal life of the Holy Spirit in the church. So when we talk about the Body of Christ, we’re talking about something real, something that includes both the individual believer and the whole community.

Now, all of this leads to relics. If the body is truly the person, and if the saints are truly the Body of Christ, then their bodies remain the saints themselves even after death. That’s why veneration is appropriate. They bring up Second Kings, chapter thirteen, verse twenty-one, where a dead man is thrown into Elisha’s tomb and comes back to life when his body touches the prophet’s bones. That’s not magic. That’s the power of God working through matter that has been sanctified. They also mention Saint John of Damascus, whose defense of icons centered on the relationship of matter to God. Matter is not evil. Matter is used by God for salvation. And they share a beautiful example: the incorrupt hand of Saint Mary Magdalene, kept at Semenopetra Monastery on Mount Athos, which is said to be warm and flexible. That’s a sign that the saint is still present, still alive in Christ.

They also address some related practices. The forty-day blessing after death, the service of altar service, and the question of cremation. On cremation, they’re clear. Cremation dissolves the body, which is still the person, and it acts against the hope of resurrection. Christians traditionally do not cremate. The body is not a shell to be discarded. It’s the person, and it’s destined for glory.

Finally, they step back and reflect on the material world itself. Matter is the medium of salvation. God created it, God took it on in the Incarnation, and God uses it to save us. The whole earth was corrupted through the works taught by Azazel, as we read in First Enoch, chapter ten, verse eight. But Christ came to undo that corruption. And so the material world, including our bodies, is not something to escape. It’s something to be transfigured. That’s the core teaching of this episode. Bodies matter. Matter matters. And the saints, in their relics, show us that God’s grace can permeate everything.
