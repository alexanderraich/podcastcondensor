Father Andrew Stephen Damick and Father Stephen De Young open this episode by looking at the nature of Mosaic authority. They argue that Moses’ authority was not simply about performing miracles or settling court cases. It was about making binding rulings on how God’s commandments apply to daily life. Moses was given the authority to interpret the Law, to decide what it meant in specific situations, and those decisions were final for Israel.

The fathers point to the book of Exodus to show this in action. When God commands Moses to consecrate the firstborn, Moses does not just repeat the command. He elaborates on it, explaining how it should be applied. This is the exercise of Mosaic authority. It is the act of taking God’s word and making it concrete for the people. This is not a human invention. It is an authority established by God Himself.

The episode also looks at how this authority was shared and delegated. In Exodus chapter eighteen, Jethro, Moses’ father-in-law, advises him to appoint capable men to help judge the people. These men are called elders, or presbyters in the Greek. They share in the work of judgment, but Moses retains the final authority. This delegation is not a loss of authority. It is a way of extending it.

The transmission of this authority is a central theme. In Numbers chapter twenty-seven, God commands Moses to lay his hands on Joshua and invest him with authority. This laying on of hands is not a symbolic gesture. It is the actual means by which authority is passed from one person to another. This establishes a pattern of prophetic succession. Moses passes his authority to Joshua, and Joshua passes it to the elders who outlive him, and so on through the prophets.

The fathers connect this to the Hebrew term semikha, which refers to ordination by the laying on of hands. The root of the word means sturdy, or able to support weight. The idea is that the one receiving the authority is being given the strength to bear it. The Greek word for authority, exousia, comes from a verb meaning it is permitted. This authority is the freedom and ability to make rulings.

The episode then turns to the problem of false prophets. In Jeremiah chapter twenty-three, the prophet condemns those who speak visions of their own minds. These false prophets give false assurances. They tell the people that all is well when it is not. They lead the people away from repentance. The true prophet, by contrast, is one who has stood in the divine council, the heavenly assembly of God and His angels. The true prophet has received God’s word directly and speaks it faithfully.

The fathers also discuss the story of Phineas, the priest who zealously defended the holiness of Israel at Baal Peor. He executed an Israelite man and a Midianite woman who were committing idolatry and sexual immorality. This act stopped a plague and earned him a covenant of peace. This is an example of the kind of authoritative action that is required to protect the holiness of the people.

The episode critiques a common modern approach to the Old Testament. Many Christians divide the Law into moral, civil, and ceremonial categories. The fathers argue that this division is not found in the text itself. The Old Testament remains relevant and authoritative. It is not a collection of outdated rules. It is the living word of God.

The discussion also touches on the dangers of rejecting authority altogether. The fathers argue that classical liberalism, which is rooted in the rejection of authority, is satanic and leads to destruction. It encourages individuals to follow their own judgment rather than the authority that God has established. This is a rejection of the very structure that God built for His people.

The episode sets the stage for a follow-up discussion on how this authority continues in the New Testament and in the Church. The pattern of prophetic succession, the laying on of hands, and the authority to make binding rulings all carry forward. The authority that Moses exercised, and that Joshua received, is not a thing of the past. It is a living reality that continues to shape the life of God’s people.
