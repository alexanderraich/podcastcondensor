In this episode of the Lord of Spirits, Fathers Andrew and Stephen continue their series on the end times by looking at what they call the bad boys of the apocalypse. They take up the Four Horsemen, Gog and Magog, and the locust armies, and they start by clearing away some common misunderstandings about the riders in the sixth chapter of Revelation.

The first horseman, often called Pestilence or Conquest, is identified as Reshef, the ancient Canaanite god of plague and war. He rides a white horse and carries a bow. The second horseman is War, associated with the sword as a spiritual agent of divine judgment. The third horseman is Famine, linked to a figure named Raav, who is part of the retinue of Môt, the god of death. The scales this rider carries symbolize economic injustice, and the mention of a denarius for a day’s wage shows extreme food prices. The fourth horseman is Death himself, and he is identified with Môt, the Canaanite god of death. In the text, Hades follows along behind him, not as a ruler, but as a beast.

The fathers stress that these demonic forces are not independent agents. They are under God’s control, unleashed as instruments of judgment and as calls to repentance. This is what they call the left hand of God. God uses even evil to accomplish His will, allowing these powers to act within strict limits. They point to Deuteronomy thirty-two, where God says He will send Reshef and Keteb, and to Habakkuk three, where Deber and Reshef are described as attendants of Yahweh. Even the plagues of Egypt are attributed to Reshef in Psalm seventy-eight. So these Canaanite deities, filtered through the Hebrew prophetic tradition, become tools in the hand of the true God.

The hosts also note that in Revelation six, only the fourth horseman is actually named in the text. The other three are identified by tradition, not by the text itself. And they connect these riders to the four chariots in Zechariah six, which are sent to patrol the earth and bring judgment.

Then they turn to Gog and Magog. These figures come from Ezekiel thirty-eight and thirty-nine, where Gog is a person and Magog is a place. The name Gog may be based on Gyges, a king of Lydia in the seventh century before Christ, known from Plato’s Republic and the story of the Ring of Gyges. Magog appears in Genesis ten as a son of Japheth, and it may come from an Assyrian term meaning the land of Gyges. Over time, Gog became a type of the final enemy of God, the ultimate Antichrist figure who gathers the nations against Christ.

The fathers also discuss the later legends about Alexander the Great building a gate to imprison Gog and Magog. This story developed in the Syriac Alexander Legend and in the seventh-century text of Pseudo-Methodius, which placed the gate between two bronze mountains. They warn against modern misuse of these texts, especially in dispensationalism. They mention Hal Lindsey, whose book The Late Great Planet Earth popularized the idea that Gog and Magog referred to the Soviet Union.

They also clear up a common mistake about Armageddon. It does not refer to the Valley of Megiddo. Instead, it is a Greek transliteration of Har Mo’ed, the mountain of assembly, which is attacked by Amalek in the Old Testament.

The episode closes with a pastoral reflection. These apocalyptic images are not meant to inspire fear or speculation. They are meant to inspire repentance, trust in God’s sovereignty, and a life of active love and holiness. Suffering, even demonic affliction, is an opportunity for growth and repentance, not necessarily a sign of personal sin. By choosing to receive all circumstances as chances to draw closer to God, a Christian becomes invulnerable to harm, because nothing can separate us from the love of God. The fathers remind us that the purpose of divine judgment is always to call people back to Him, not to destroy them.
