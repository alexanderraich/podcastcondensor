This is the second part of their series on the Torah, and the hosts, Father Andrew and Father Stephen, dive into three big themes: clean and unclean, worship, and the death penalty. They start by tackling the idea that the laws about clean and unclean are not just arbitrary rules from an ancient time. Instead, these laws are meant to teach us discernment. They help us tell the difference between what is holy and what is not, between moral and ceremonial purity. This is a practical way to train our minds, and it’s still relevant for Christians today. They point to Leviticus, where God tells the people to distinguish between the clean and the unclean, and they argue this is about learning to see the world rightly.

The conversation then moves to the concept of death by holiness. This is the idea that approaching God’s presence without proper purification is dangerous. It’s not that God is mean, but that His holiness is so intense that it can consume what is not prepared for it. This is why the purity laws and the sacrificial system existed. And this is ultimately resolved in Christ’s atonement. They bring up the Day of Atonement from Leviticus sixteen, where two goats are used. One is sacrificed, and the other, the scapegoat, is sent into the wilderness to a figure called Azazel. This ritual purified the sanctuary from the taint of sin. The hosts explain that this points directly to Christ, who acts as both the sacrificial goat and the scapegoat. He purifies the whole world, not just individual sins, and He defeats the devil, who is bound and punished, just as Azazel is in later Jewish literature. They connect this to the first epistle of John, which says Christ is the atonement for the whole world.

Next, they discuss worship in the Torah. Worship was centered on the tabernacle and later the temple, with specific practices, especially the offering of incense with prayers in the morning and evening. This was a commandment, and the hosts stress that this pattern is never abrogated in the New Testament. It continues directly into Orthodox Christian worship, particularly in the services of Matins and Vespers. The incense and the prayers go together, just as they did in the temple.

They also talk about the Eucharist as a sacrifice. This is a key point. They reference Saint Paul’s first letter to the Corinthians, where he explicitly compares the Eucharist to both Jewish and pagan sacrifices. He asks what the pagans are doing when they offer to idols, and he says that when we partake of the cup and the bread, we are participating in the sacrifice of Christ. So the Eucharist is not just a memorial; it is a true sacrificial offering that fulfills and sums up all the Old Testament sacrifices.

Finally, they take on the Torah’s death penalty. They argue that this is not a literal call for execution in every case. Instead, the phrase “cut off from among the people” is a spiritual threat. It means being excluded from the community, which in the ancient world was equivalent to death. They bring up the Greek philosopher Socrates, who chose to drink hemlock rather than accept exile, because exile from his community was worse than death. In the New Testament, this is fulfilled in excommunication. Saint Paul talks about handing someone over to Satan for the destruction of the flesh, so that the spirit may be saved. So excommunication is meant to be a severe but loving act, intended to promote repentance and bring the sinner back into the community.

They also take listener calls on topics like Jewish Christianity, ritual sacrifice, and fasting. They touch on Saint James, the brother of Jesus, who led the early Jewish Christian community, and whose martyrdom was seen by some as a cause for the destruction of Jerusalem. And they clarify that while nothing is inherently unclean, as Saint Paul says in Romans, our actions can make things unclean, shifting the focus from things to our behavior. A believing spouse sanctifies the family, just as circumcision included the whole household in Israel. The core teaching is that the Torah is not obsolete. It is a living guide that finds its fulfillment in Christ and in the life of the Church.
