In this episode of the Lord of Spirits, Fathers Andrew Stephen Damick and Stephen De Young take up the sacrament of confession, and they start by tackling a very common assumption head-on. Many of us grow up believing that God must punish every single sin, that His justice demands a payment for every wrong. The fathers challenge this idea directly, pointing out that if God is required to punish, then He is subject to some higher principle above Himself, which undermines the very nature of His mercy. They argue that God can and does forgive sins directly, without requiring punishment, and that this is the heart of the Gospel.

From there, they turn to the purpose of the Torah, the Law of Moses. They are careful to explain that the Torah was never given as a way to earn salvation. Instead, it was a sin management system. It provided commandments for righteous living, and it also provided rituals for dealing with sin when it inevitably occurred. King David is a perfect example of this. He kept the Torah perfectly, not by being sinless, but by repenting fully when he sinned, as we see in Psalm fifty-one. Yet even David still needed a Savior. The fathers also look at the Old Testament sacrificial system, showing that forgiveness was tied to purification and restitution. Confession was always implied in the process, and the goal was cleansing from the corrupting effects of sin, not just a legal pardon.

When they move to the New Testament, the communal nature of confession becomes the central theme. They examine Matthew chapter eighteen, where Jesus gives the apostles authority to bind and loose, indicating that the church’s actions on earth correspond to heaven. They also look at James chapter five, which commands us to confess our sins to one another and pray for one another so that we may be healed. This is not merely private prayer between the individual and God. Confession is a communal act, done within the church community, and it is through that community that God works to forgive and heal. The example of Zacchaeus is brought up, the tax collector who repented and made restitution, showing that confession is connected to making things right with others.

The conversation then turns to the practice of confession in the Orthodox Church. The fathers introduce the Greek term epitimia, which is often translated as penance. They stress that this is not punishment. It is a treatment plan, a form of guidance for spiritual growth, aimed at healing and forming good habits. It is about restoration, not retribution. They also discuss church discipline, including excommunication. They reference Saint Paul’s letter to the Corinthians, where he speaks of handing someone over to Satan for the destruction of the flesh so that the spirit may be saved. This is an act of love, intended to bring about repentance and protect the community, not a final condemnation. Even the anathema, the formal declaration of excommunication, is meant to call people to repentance.

The fathers also touch on the Reformers, noting that Martin Luther had a sacrament of absolution and a version of public confession, and that John Calvin considered church discipline a mark of the true church. This shows that the communal and disciplinary aspects of confession are not just Orthodox inventions, but are rooted in the earliest Christian practice. Throughout the episode, they emphasize that repentance, or metanoia, is a change of mind and heart. It is a turning away from sin and a turning toward God, and it is a lifelong process.

The episode concludes with a call to embrace the communal and healing nature of confession. The fathers encourage us to see it not as a courtroom, but as a hospital. It is a place where we are purified, healed, and restored to fellowship. And they remind us that we are called to offer the world the freedom and forgiveness that we have found in Christ, a forgiveness that is real, direct, and available to all who ask.
