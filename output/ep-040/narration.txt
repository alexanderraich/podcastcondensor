Father Andrew opens this episode by reading the full text of the Harrowing of Hell from the Gospel of Nicodemus, which is also known as the Acts of Pilate. This is an apocryphal text from the fourth or fifth century, and Father Andrew is careful to explain its place in the Christian tradition. It is not Scripture, but it belongs to a category of books that are meant to be read at home. The ancient church made a clear distinction between three kinds of books: those read in church, which are Scripture; those read at home for edification; and those that should not be read at all because they are heretical. The Gospel of Nicodemus falls into that middle category, and it is honored in Orthodox liturgical tradition even though it is not part of the Bible.

The story itself is a dramatic telling of what happened when Christ descended into Hades after his crucifixion. He breaks the gates of brass and cuts the bars of iron asunder. Hell is personified as a character who argues with Satan, but in the end, both are defeated. Christ, the King of Glory, tramples death, binds Satan, and leads the righteous dead out of hell. Among those rescued are Adam, the patriarchs, and the prophets. The account is given through the testimony of two men, Karinus and Leucius, who are the sons of Simeon. They were raised from the dead at the time of the crucifixion, and they write down what they witnessed.

The narrative includes several powerful moments. When Christ arrives in hell, the prophets recognize him. Isaiah quotes his own prophecy, saying that the people who walked in darkness have seen a great light. David speaks the words of Psalm twenty-four, calling for the gates to lift up their heads so the King of Glory may come in. He also quotes Psalm one hundred seven, declaring that God has broken the gates of brass and cut the bars of iron asunder. Isaiah adds the words of Hosea, asking where death’s sting is and where hell’s victory is. Adam cries out in the words of Psalm thirty, thanking the Lord for bringing his soul out of hell. David then calls for a new song, and Micah proclaims that God removes sins and casts them into the depths of the sea.

There is also a moment involving Seth, the son of Adam. He recounts how the archangel Michael told him that after five thousand five hundred years, the Son of God would come to anoint believers with the oil of mercy through baptism. This oil is a symbol of God’s mercy and the Holy Spirit. When Christ appears, he makes the sign of the cross over Adam and all the saints. The penitent thief, who was crucified with Jesus, also uses the sign of the cross to enter Paradise. He reminds Christ of the promise made to him on the cross, that he would be with Jesus in Paradise that very day.

John the Baptist is also present in hell, and he announces the arrival of Christ to the waiting righteous. The story concludes with the saints entering Paradise, where they meet Enoch and Elijah, the two men who were taken up without dying. The penitent thief is there as well. After the events are recorded, Karinus and Leucius return to Jerusalem. They give their writings to the high priests Annas and Caiaphas, and also to Nicodemus and Joseph of Arimathea. Joseph himself testifies to the resurrection of the dead, and Pontius Pilate records all of these events in his public books.

Father Andrew’s reading of this text is not just a recitation of an old story. It is a reminder of the central Christian teaching that Christ truly descended into hell and conquered death. The righteous dead were not abandoned. They were led out of darkness and into the light of Paradise. The Gospel of Nicodemus may not be Scripture, but it preserves a tradition that the church has long honored, and it gives voice to the hope that death does not have the final word.
