In this episode, Father Andrew and Father Stephen take on the huge topic of magic in the ancient world, and they’re careful to distinguish it from the stage magic we think of today. In the ancient world, magic wasn’t about illusion or entertainment. It was a set of techniques for interacting with spiritual beings. Anyone could learn these procedures, and they often involved rituals and material objects. This made magic different from the role of a priest. A priest had a specific social position, but a magician was defined by knowing the right techniques.

The hosts survey many different kinds of magic. There was wonder-working, which is performing miracles. There was demonology, which is learning how to command spirits. There was divination, which is gaining knowledge through spiritual means. This included augury, reading the flight of birds, and extispicy, reading the entrails of sacrificed animals. There was also astrology, which in the ancient world was based on the belief that celestial bodies were divine or angelic beings influencing earthly events. And there was alchemy, which wasn’t primitive chemistry but the production of objects or substances meant to manipulate spiritual forces. Amulets were the most common magical objects, worn to ward off evil spirits. People wore pendants and rings, often featuring images like the face of Medusa, based on the idea that the image of misfortune repels misfortune. There were also execration bowls, clay bowls inscribed with curses and the name of a target, then smashed to symbolize the curse.

The Bible takes all of this very seriously. In Exodus twenty-two, we read, “You shall not permit a sorceress to live.” And in Deuteronomy eighteen, God lists forbidden practices like divination, sorcery, and necromancy. The hosts emphasize that God forbids magic not because it’s fake, but because it works, and it leads to communion with demons. The Bible assumes the reality of these practices. Right after the prohibition in Deuteronomy, God promises a prophet like Moses. Prophecy is a direct relationship with God, while divination is manipulative. The story of King Saul consulting the Witch of Endor in First Samuel twenty-eight is a clear example of necromancy. And in Second Chronicles thirty-three, King Manasseh practiced sorcery and necromancy before repenting.

The New Testament continues this theme. Jesus’ opponents didn’t deny his miracles. Instead, they accused him of casting out demons by Beelzebul. This shows that miracles alone don’t prove divine origin. In Acts eight, we meet Simon Magus, a Samaritan magician who offered money to the apostles to buy the power of the Holy Spirit. This reveals a magical mindset that treats spiritual power as a purchasable technique. The hosts also mention the seven sons of Sceva in Acts nineteen, who tried to exorcise using Jesus’ name and were overpowered. Many new believers then burned their magical books. Paul lists sorcery among the works of the flesh in Galatians five, and in Second Thessalonians, we learn that the man of lawlessness will work signs and wonders through the energies of Satan.

The hosts point out that magical practices persisted among Christians throughout history. They tell the story of Bishop Sofronius of Tella in the fifth century, who used a cheese sandwich oracle and a divining cup to identify a thief, as recorded in council acts. This illustrates how even church leaders could fall into magical thinking. They also discuss figures like Apollonius of Tyana, a contemporary of Jesus reputed to work miracles, and Carl Jung, who studied Hermeticism and alchemy and took these traditions in a psychological direction.

The episode concludes with a strong critique of modern magical thinking. The hosts label prosperity gospel preachers like Kenneth Copeland and Benny Hinn as not Christian, accusing them of preaching magic rather than repentance. They also critique Oprah Winfrey’s association with “The Secret” and “A Course in Miracles” as magical thinking with a veneer of Christianity. The true Christian alternative to magic is repentance, which means surrendering control and submitting to God’s will. Father Stephen concludes that contentment, being at peace with unmet desires, is the antidote to magical thinking. It’s a superpower that frees us from the futile quest to control outcomes.
