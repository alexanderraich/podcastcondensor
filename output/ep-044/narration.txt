In this episode, Fathers Andrew and Stephen begin with a big question: where does the authority of the Bible actually come from? They start by looking at the ancient world, at texts like the Babylonian Enuma Elish and the Egyptian Book of the Dead. These weren’t just stories people believed in. They were ritual documents, enacted in ceremonies to keep the cosmos in order. That changes how we should think about the Torah. The first five books of the Bible weren’t written as a modern history book. They emerged from oral tradition and served as a priestly manual, a guide for kings and elders. The authority was never in the text itself. It was in God, mediated through the text, pointing to an experience of Him.

The fathers use several examples to make this concrete. In Genesis fourteen, the place names are updated for later readers, showing an editing process over time. In Exodus twelve, the Passover instructions are given before the event even happens, which only makes sense if the text is a ritual manual. Leviticus sixteen gives the Day of Atonement ritual in response to a specific crisis, showing the Torah as a way to manage sin. And Deuteronomy seventeen tells the king to make his own copy of the Torah and study it, so he can govern justly. The Torah is the law of the house, the household law of God’s family, not a legal code in the modern sense.

Then they turn to the canon. How did we get the list of books in the Bible? The key claim is that the canon is descriptive, not prescriptive. No council and no emperor ever decided it. It formed organically through the reception of communities. In Second Temple Judaism, there was real diversity. The Qumran community, for example, had a canon that included the Book of Enoch and the Book of Jubilees, and they followed a solar calendar. Josephus made claims about the canon, but the fathers caution that he was hyperbolic and partisan. The Old Testament books were written and compiled over centuries, by many hands, and that doesn’t weaken their authority at all.

The New Testament canon came together the same way, through use and reception. The Book of Revelation is a special case. It had a difficult history because it was associated with chiliasm, the belief in a literal thousand-year reign of Christ on earth, which Orthodoxy considers heretical. Revelation was only accepted after commentaries, especially one by St. Andrew of Caesarea in the sixth century, rehabilitated it with an orthodox interpretation. Even then, it is not read in the liturgical cycle in the East.

There’s also a middle category of texts. The Shepherd of Hermas was widely read in the early church but never made the canon. The Church Fathers and some apocrypha are like this. They are authoritative and edifying, but they are read privately, not publicly in church as scripture. The word apocrypha literally means hidden or private books.

Finally, they reflect on Holy Tradition. Tradition is not just passing down data. It is the ongoing life of the Holy Spirit in the church. Reception history is the work of the Spirit. The authority of texts and councils rests on how the church receives them over time. Jeremiah thirty-one speaks of a new covenant where the law is written on hearts, and that points to a shift from mediated to direct experience of God. Jude even quotes the Book of Enoch, which shows how these texts circulated and influenced each other.

The fathers close with a call to humility. We need to ground theology in reality, not in abstract ideas. The text points beyond itself to God. And we should hold our own opinions loosely, trusting the Spirit’s work in the life of the church.
