In this episode, Fathers Andrew and Stephen continue their deep dive into apostolic succession, picking up where they left off with the authority of Moses. They start by looking at a challenging passage in the Gospel of Matthew, where Jesus tells the crowds to obey the Pharisees because they sit on Moses’ seat. The hosts make it clear that Jesus was affirming the legitimacy of their authority, even while he strongly condemned their hypocrisy. The people were to follow their teachings, but not their example. This sets the stage for a crucial point: authority itself is not the problem, but how it is used.

From there, the conversation moves to the transfer of that authority. Jesus, after critiquing the Pharisees, gives the same power to bind and loose to his apostles. We hear about the moment in Matthew chapter sixteen, where Peter is given the keys of the kingdom, and then again in Matthew chapter eighteen, where the whole group of disciples receives that authority in a conciliar setting. The hosts emphasize that this was not just a symbolic gesture. It was a real, tangible passing of authority, rooted in the Old Testament pattern of Moses laying hands on Joshua. This physical act, the laying on of hands, is the thread that connects the ancient priesthood to the early church.

Father Andrew and Father Stephen explain that the apostles held a unique combination of roles. They were prophets, representing God to the people, and priests, representing the people to God. This dual office was then passed on to their successors, the bishops. The hosts point to Saint Paul, who was directly commissioned by Christ and then appointed men like Timothy and Titus, reminding Timothy of the gift he received through the laying on of hands. This established a new chain of succession, a new line of authority that continues to this day.

The episode also tackles a fascinating historical detail. The hosts explain that the ancient Jewish practice of ordination, called semikhah, which traced its authority all the way back to Moses, actually broke down between the years three hundred and sixty and four hundred and twenty-five after Christ. Modern rabbinical ordination is not the same as that ancient chain. This historical break highlights the significance of the Christian succession, which did not break.

The discussion then turns to how this authority is structured in the Church. The hosts describe the development of the threefold ministry of bishop, presbyter, and deacon. They also explore the Orthodox understanding of primacy and conciliarity. It is not a matter of one bishop ruling over everyone else, but rather a balance. We see this in the Council of Jerusalem in the book of Acts, where the apostles gathered together to make decisions. Peter had a primacy of honor, but he did not act alone. The authority is held collectively by the bishops in synod, while individual bishops have real authority within their own dioceses.

Finally, the hosts address the accountability of church leaders. They make a strong point that bishops are primarily accountable to God for their rulings, and secondarily to the synod of bishops. They are not accountable to the laity in the same way a democratic leader is. This leads to a discussion on the importance of obedience within the Church, even when it is difficult. They contrast this with the claims of Joseph Smith, who said he received authority from angelic visitations. The hosts argue that this is a completely different model, one that breaks from the historical, tangible chain of authority that Christ established and that has been passed down through the laying on of hands from generation to generation.
