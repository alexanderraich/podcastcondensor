In this episode, Fathers Andrew and Stephen open the mailbag for a wide-ranging question and answer session. They tackle listener questions on everything from Old Testament sacrifices to modern questions about gender and the end of the world.

The conversation begins with a question about a difficult passage in Leviticus. The fathers explain that the mention of Molek is not a general reference to some ancient ritual, but rather points to a specific demon. This demon was associated with child sacrifice, a practice that stood in direct opposition to God’s command to be fruitful and multiply. It was a perversion of life itself.

From there, they turn to the Gospel of John. Why are there no exorcisms in that Gospel? The fathers suggest that John’s Gospel was written later than the others, and it assumes the reader already knows them. It is a theological supplement, focusing on different themes, like the deep teachings of Christ, rather than repeating the same miracles and events.

This leads to a discussion about the Holy Spirit and prophecy. They clarify the difference between the Spirit coming upon someone temporarily, as it did with King Saul, and being filled with the Spirit as a permanent state. For someone who is impure, the Spirit’s presence can be a painful experience. God can even use sinners for His purposes, but that does not mean they are holy.

The conversation then takes a deep turn into the nature of sin itself. Sin is not just a bad action or a legal infraction. It is a demonic spiritual being, a force that works through human cooperation. When we yield to our passions, we give control to these demonic forces. There is a continuum between sinning and being fully possessed. The fathers reference the story of Cain, where sin is described as a beast crouching at the door, waiting to master him.

They also explore the story of Adam and Eve. Their nakedness before the fall was a sign of innocence, a childlike state. The garments of skin that God made for them are not literal animal hides. The Church Fathers saw this as a symbol of a profound change in human nature itself, a coarsening and a mortality that entered the world after the fall.

A listener asks about the relationship between blessing, justice, and wisdom. The fathers explain that these are distinct but connected. Justice is the proper order of creation. Blessing is the experience of living in that right order. And wisdom is the ability to perceive and understand that order.

They then address a question about curses and the evil eye, a concern raised by a priest from the Indian Orthodox Church. The fathers are clear: Christians should not live in fear of these things. We are called to trust in Christ’s protection. They caution against folk magic and superstition, encouraging prayer and reliance on God instead.

Another listener, a Bible college student, asks about the role of women in the Church. The fathers affirm that women have a vital and irreplaceable ministry. They point to the Theotokos, the Mother of God, as the highest example of human holiness, and to saints like Kassiani, the great hymnographer. While women are not ordained as priests, their ministry is in no way inferior. It is essential to the life of the Church.

The discussion moves to the atonement. The fathers explain that the Hebrew word for atonement means to cover. Its deep meaning comes from the Day of Atonement in the Old Testament. They contrast the Eastern approach with the complex legal theories that developed in the West, often influenced by figures like Saint Augustine and later medieval theologians. Salvation is not a legal transaction. It is about freedom from slavery to sin and death. When the scriptures speak of debt, they are using a metaphor, not describing a commercial exchange between God the Father and God the Son.

This leads to a profound discussion of theosis and its opposite, demonosis. Our actions draw us closer to God or to demons. Sanctity and demonization are not absolute states but matters of degree. There are no shortcuts on either path. Our own cooperation is essential.

They also explore the union of the spiritual and material. The fathers suggest that the real distinction is not between a material world and a spiritual one, but between the seen and the unseen. They use the analogy of the common sense, the ancient idea that our five senses are integrated into a single perception of reality. Spiritual perception works in a similar way.

On the Eucharist, they explain that Christ’s command to eat His flesh and drink His blood is the exact inverse of a predatory sacrifice. In pagan rituals, the worshipper consumes the victim. But here, Christ offers His own life to us. The blood represents life, and He gives it freely to us.

They also touch on marriage, describing it as a transition to a new mode of life. Spouses serve Christ by serving each other and their children. The wedding service is beautiful, but it only has meaning when it is lived out in daily faithfulness.

A question about how change happens in the Church leads to a discussion of pastoral authority. The core faith does not change, but practices can and do evolve. This usually happens through the pastoral decisions of bishops, which are then received by the wider community.

On the topic of gender, the fathers distinguish between biological sex and the social construct of gender. They affirm the importance of the body and its potentialities. They suggest that much of the confusion around transgender identity stems from a breakdown of community and a loss of a stable sense of self.

Finally, they address the Second Coming. It is a future event in material time, though our understanding of time in the intermediate state is limited. Christ is already present with us, and we await His final coming in glory. The episode closes with a quotation from the Book of Revelation, declaring the worthiness of the Lamb who was slain.
