In this episode, Fathers Andrew and Stephen continue their deep dive into the world of images, picking up from their last conversation. They want to clear up a big misunderstanding right away. Many people assume that iconography and idolatry are basically the same thing, but the hosts argue they are two completely separate phenomena that have existed side by side in many cultures. They also make a firm distinction between veneration and worship. Veneration is the honor you give to an icon or a saint, while worship is reserved for God alone. The honor you show to an image passes to the person it depicts, which they call the prototype.

To get at this, they talk about myth and ritual. A myth isn’t just a false story. It’s a story that has ongoing meaning for a community, and it’s kept alive through ritual. Iconography works as a kind of ritual reenactment. When you look at an icon, you’re not just seeing a picture. You’re being drawn into the story it tells and the spiritual reality behind it.

They look back at ancient pagan sites to show how this played out. At places like Göbekli Tepe and Çatalhöyük, you find plenty of images carved into stone or mounted on walls, like the bull horns at Çatalhöyük. These were part of the religious landscape, but they weren’t necessarily idols. An idol is something different. An idol is an object that is worshipped as a deity, often believed to house a spirit, and it becomes the focus of sacrifice. So you can have iconography without idolatry, and you can have idolatry without iconography.

Then they turn to Israel. God himself commanded the creation of images. Think of the tabernacle, which was built as an icon of the heavenly sanctuary. The Ark of the Covenant had cherubim on its cover, representing the throne of God, not a container for him. And then there’s the bronze serpent from the book of Numbers. Moses made it at God’s command, and it brought healing to the people. That’s a clear example of an icon being used properly. But later, in the second book of Kings, we hear that King Hezekiah destroyed that same bronze serpent because the people had started burning incense to it. It had become an idol. So the object itself wasn’t the problem. The misuse was.

The hosts also point out that humanity itself is an icon. In Genesis, we hear that we are made in the image of God. The word for image there is the same word for icon. And in the first letter of John, we’re told that if we cannot love our brother whom we have seen, we cannot love God whom we have not seen. That only makes sense if humans are icons of God.

When it comes to Christian iconography, the hosts show that it didn’t appear out of nowhere. Early Christians continued the Jewish tradition of using images. At Dura-Europos, an ancient city in Syria, archaeologists found both a synagogue and a house church, and both were covered in extensive iconography. So the earliest Christians were already making and using sacred images.

They also address modern objections to icons, especially from the Protestant Reformation. John Calvin argued against icons, but the hosts say his arguments were based on incorrect historical assumptions. Modern iconoclasm, they argue, is ultimately grounded in a kind of Platonic dualism that rejects the material world as a way of encountering God. But the incarnation changes everything. God became flesh, so the material can carry the divine.

Finally, they touch on wonderworking icons. These are icons through which God works miracles, like healing or the flow of myrrh. They aren’t magical objects. They are means through which God acts, just like the bronze serpent was. And they mention Saint Phanourios, a saint known for helping people find lost things. People bake a cake called a Fanouropita in his honor, have it blessed, and share it as a thank-offering.

In the end, the hosts want us to see that iconography is not a concession to paganism. It’s a way of participating in spiritual reality, grounded in the incarnation and rooted in the very beginning of Scripture.
