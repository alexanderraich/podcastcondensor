This episode is about St. Irenaeus of Lyon, and about a book of his that most people have never read. The book is called On the Apostolic Preaching, and it was lost for something like fifteen hundred years. What it contains is not a defense of the faith against heretics, and not a systematic theology textbook. It is a demonstration of how the apostles read the Old Testament through Christ — which is to say, it shows us the apostolic way of reading Scripture itself. And because it was written around the year 180, two generations from the Apostle John, it shows us what the Church already believed before Nicaea, before Constantine, before any of the things people imagine were invented later. So the question this episode answers is: what did the second-century Church actually teach, and how did it read the Bible?

We begin with the man himself. Irenaeus was Greek, born in Smyrna, in Asia Minor. He was a disciple of St. Polycarp, and Polycarp was a disciple of St. John the Apostle. So we are two links from the Apostle John. He moved west to Lugdunum, which is modern Lyon, in Gaul, and he became the second bishop of that city around the year 177, after the first bishop, St. Pothinus, was martyred under Marcus Aurelius. Marcus Aurelius is remembered as the philosopher-emperor, the Stoic, the one Ridley Scott put on screen in Gladiator — and it is worth remembering that the film originally shot scenes of Christian persecution under his reign that were cut from the final movie. He was a persecutor of Christians. The Church remembers Irenaeus as a hieromartyr, though the early evidence for his martyrdom is late.

His relics had a hard history. The ancient church that held them was destroyed by the Huguenots during the Reformation. Two skull fragments and a heel bone are claimed as his relics today. Carbon dating puts the skull fragments in the medieval period and the heel bone in the second century. This is a pattern with Reformation iconoclasm: much was destroyed, but some relics had already been divided and carried to the continent, and some have since returned to the United Kingdom. There are relics of St. Martin of Tours in Louisiana, for instance. The point is not to settle the archaeology but to note that the physical memory of the saints was deliberately attacked and has partially survived anyway.

Now, to read Irenaeus well, you have to understand what kind of book you are holding. Against Heresies is a polemical work. It is not a positive theological treatise. In Greek, theological treatises were titled with the word peri plus the topic — On First Principles, On the Incarnation, that kind of thing. Aristotle did it, Origen did it, Athanasius did it. A polemical work is written to fight a specific opponent, and it argues in the terms of that fight. A positive treatise lays out the teaching as it stands. This distinction matters enormously, because modern readers ignore genre and rhetoric. They read a Church Father's polemical writing as if it were his settled positive theology, and they get him wrong. The classic example is the Calvinist use of Augustine's anti-Pelagian writings. Augustine wrote those to fight Pelagius, and he pushed certain things hard for the sake of that fight. Read them as his whole theology and you will misread him. Modern culture ignores rhetoric entirely; it cares only about what can be done with words, not what kind of speech is being made. But if you want to read the fathers, genre is not optional.

On the Apostolic Preaching is the other kind of book. It is a positive presentation of the apostolic teaching. It was known by its title from ancient lists, but no copy survived — until 1904, when an Armenian translation was found in an ancient cathedral. Armenia, of course, was the first officially Christian nation. The Armenian text is a woodenly literal translation of the Greek, which means you can reconstruct the original behind it. Father John Behr's translation for St. Vladimir's Seminary Press, published in 1997, includes a useful introduction and a Greek apparatus, and it is the one to get.

And what does the book do? It is not a summary of New Testament quotations. It is a demonstration of how the apostles read the Old Testament through Christ. It does what the New Testament itself does. It alludes to the Johannine literature more than to Paul. And it presents the rule of faith — which is the canon, used interchangeably with apostolic tradition — as a hermeneutic for Scripture. This is the key move, and it reframes the whole Protestant debate about Scripture and tradition. Holy tradition is not a set of secret oral teachings running alongside the Bible. It is a way of reading Scripture. That is how the early Church answered the problem of competing interpretations: not by opposing Scripture to tradition, but by reading Scripture within the apostolic tradition. Orthodox apologists should not simply recycle Roman Catholic arguments here. The Orthodox answer is different and older.

Now to a specific controversy. There is a claim that floats around that St. Irenaeus taught Jesus was fifty years old. This comes from Against Heresies, Book 2, section 22. What is actually happening there? Gnostic groups taught that Jesus' ministry began at thirty and lasted one year, based on numerology. Irenaeus argues against this that Christ passed through all the ages of human life, including mature adulthood. And he cites St. John's Gospel, where the Jews say to Jesus, "You are not yet fifty years old." Irenaeus never says Jesus was fifty. He says Jesus was not yet fifty. The people making this claim are using an extra-biblical tradition — the three-year ministry — to discredit Irenaeus. And the three-year ministry is itself not in the Bible. It is a tradition. So the argument is self-refuting: it uses tradition to disprove tradition in order to prove Sola Scriptura. Irenaeus also says Christ was born in 3 BC, which is worth noting.

Now, a caller named Ilya asks about preparation for Holy Communion — the midnight fast, and receiving communion while unprepared. The hosts' answer begins with the most important thing: preparation is a matter for your father confessor. The Eucharistic fast is about reverence, about preparing to receive the King of kings. The caller describes receiving communion while hungover and chewing gum. No gum immediately before or after communion. Preparation includes dress, attitude, sleep, humility. And the rules are not legalistic: diabetics, pregnant women, those on medication may eat a little and still commune. The fast is purification and repentance; it trains inordinate desires, under a father confessor. The caller's real question was whether God's grace covered his past ignorance, and the answer is yes — but the point stands that preparation is pastoral, not a rulebook.

Then a question about names. Ethbaal, the father of Jezebel, means "Baal is with him" — third person singular. Emmanuel means "God is with us" — first person plural. The difference is grammatical, not just dialect. Hebrew mem means "with." Ethbaal began as a priest of Astarte, murdered the king of Sidon, and seized power, then married Jezebel to Ahab to secure alliances. His name reflects dedication to Baal as patron, analogous to a guardian angel rather than demonic possession. This is why the show keeps telling you to look up the meaning of biblical names. Elijah, or Eliyahu, means "my God is Yahweh." Elisha means "God is my salvation." Joshua means "Yahweh saves." And a chat question — are aliens demons? Yes. Brief joking about illegal aliens and being an outlaw in Terre Haute. Moving on.

Now to the heart of it: Irenaeus on the apostolic preaching, part one. He begins at Genesis, and he reads the first verses trinitarianly. The Father creates through his Word, the Son, and his Wisdom, the Holy Spirit, hovering over the waters. He is not concerned with dating creation. He is concerned with finding the Trinity there. And he uses the language of ousia and hypostasis for the Father-Son relationship — which shows that Trinitarian vocabulary predates Nicaea by a century and a half. This is part of the rule of faith, the apostolic tradition.

From the Genesis prologue he moves to the creation of angelic beings. He speaks of seven heavens, seven spirits, seven works of the Holy Spirit, connecting the angelic host to the Holy Spirit as Wisdom. There is no pre-creation fall of angels here. The creation of the invisible and the visible is one act. God creates both, as the creed says.

Then humanity. Irenaeus emphasizes that man is created as ruler of creation. And the context — following the angelic creation — suggests this rule includes the angelic beings. This connects to St. Andrew of Caesarea's comment on Revelation that the devil fell through envy of humanity, a motif found in many fathers. Man is created as ruler of the rest of creation, including angelic beings, and the devil fell through envy of that.

Adam and Eve, then, are innocent and childlike. Irenaeus reads "naked and not ashamed" as indicating an innocent, childlike mind that understood nothing of lust or shameful desires. They were sinless but not yet mature or perfect. Perfection and sinlessness are not the same thing. The fall made man a sinner and distorted his nature, but Irenaeus does not speak of guilt. The devil is identified as the fallen angel, Satan, the serpent, the originator of sin.

And then the Nephilim. Irenaeus states that angelic beings had relations with women and taught them sorcery, divination, cosmetics, love potions, and idolatry. The hosts argue this describes participation in pagan sexual rituals, not literal angel-human hybrid offspring. This reconciles the fathers: the earlier fathers taught this, while later fathers like Augustine and Chrysostom rejected literal angelic reproduction. A martyred Russian priest, Father Daniel Sysoev, independently reached the same conclusion. The show has been teaching this interpretation of Genesis 6 since 2020, in episode seven.

The modern "Sethite hypothesis" — that Seth's line intermarried with Cain's — comes from modern non-Christian scholarship that dates Genesis 6 after the exile and reads it as a polemic against intermarriage. The church fathers who took a Sethite view, Augustine and Chrysostom, did not hold this materialist reading. They held that Seth's line had a holdover theosis, supercharged with divine power, which is why they are called "sons of God." St. Ephraim the Syrian, in the Cave of Treasures, takes the Sethite view but says the daughters of Cain were possessed by demons, arriving at the same point differently. The hosts argue their view is more patristic than the materialist view of their critics.

A digression on the Cave of Treasures: Seth's family guarded Adam's relics in a cave where worship occurred. At the flood, the body of Adam was washed away and his skull carried to Jerusalem, becoming Golgotha, so that Christ's blood drips on Adam's skull. This is not Irenaeus but part of early church lore from the fourth century.

Then Christ as the second Adam, and Mary as the second Eve. Irenaeus presents Christ taking flesh like Adam's, and Mary whose virginal obedience undoes Eve's virginal disobedience. Mary is presented as an advocate for the human race. This is second-century Mariology, two generations from St. John, before Constantine, during persecution. It is not a medieval invention.

The cross as the tree inverted. Irenaeus connects the cross as a tree to the tree of the knowledge of good and evil. Adam's disobedience at the tree brought the knowledge of evil. Christ's obedience at the tree of the cross brings back the knowledge of good, the knowledge of God. This is the path of obedience to the commandments.

Recapitulation, then, and theosis. Irenaeus argues that Christ passes through the human story, sanctifying and transforming it. This is the basis of theosis. Christ fulfills the promise to Abraham of seed like the stars of heaven, and the promise to David of a Messianic seed.

And the material reality of Christ's birth, death, and resurrection. Irenaeus insists on this against docetism. If Christ was not truly born, he could not truly die. If he could not truly die, he could not be raised, and death is not defeated. This is the same argument St. Paul makes in 1 Corinthians 15. It is high Christology in the second century, not invented at Nicaea.

Irenaeus ends part one by saying all nations are called in Christ to abandon idolatry and sexual immorality and to follow Christ by living as he lived, obedient as he was obedient, which saves from death. Not merely agreeing or saying a prayer.

After a break, part two cycles back through Genesis from a different perspective. But first, a call-in from Owen and Brandon about the narrow gate and the wide gate. The hosts clarify that Matthew 7 does not give a numerical ratio of the saved. The two-ways motif is Old Testament wisdom literature — Proverbs, the Didache. Christ's point is that the way of life is difficult and requires effort, not that only a tiny minority are saved. They also explain that parables and analogies compare in one respect only, citing Aquinas. This connects to a previous Pandemonium question episode where they discussed Matthew 7.

Then Irenaeus part two. He quotes Genesis 1:1 in transliterated Hebrew, passed through Greek and Armenian. He reads barah, "created," as Aramaic bar, "son," producing "a son in the beginning God established the heavens and the earth." This places the Son in the beginning and alludes to John 1. It is not grammatically kosher Hebrew, but it shows how Irenaeus reads Genesis 1 through John 1. The hosts had discussed this verse before, in the Council of God episode, where they noted a better translation is "at the beginning of his creation."

Irenaeus finds Christ throughout the Torah: at the Oak of Mamre with Abraham, at Bethel with Jacob, in the Exodus speaking to Moses, and defeating Amalek by stretching out his arms on the cross. He summarizes that Father and Son are both God and both Lord, but the Father is invisible and inaccessible, so the Son gives access to the Father. This is an ontological statement, not merely a remedy for sin.

On Christ's death, Irenaeus reads Isaiah 53 as teaching that Christ's death was voluntary and according to the Father's will — not a punishment Christ took to satisfy God's wrath, not a substitution for sinners. Irenaeus explicitly denies that God was angry and needed to kill His Son. There is no substitution: Christ does not suffer so we don't have to. He reveals the way of life. Those who killed Christ take judgment on themselves. Those who follow Christ in obedience have judgment taken away. Irenaeus quotes Old Testament passion texts without citing the New Testament, following the apostolic model.

The law is fulfilled in love. Irenaeus teaches that the Torah is not abolished but encompassed by love: if you love God and neighbor, you will keep the commandments. The new covenant writes the law on the heart through the Holy Spirit, enabling us to love and obey. Israel is reconstituted in Christ, so being in Christ means being in Israel. Irenaeus quotes Deuteronomy — love God, love neighbor — as Christ's teaching. Loving God and keeping His commandments are inseparable throughout Scripture.

Irenaeus concludes that the apostolic preaching is both the preaching of the truth and the way of life received from Christ through the apostles. These are not separate.

And this is where the hosts land. On the Apostolic Preaching presents the whole Gospel, including the Tower of Babel and the giant lore. Second-century fathers like Irenaeus, Justin Martyr, and Ignatius already exhibit the full Orthodox faith. This refutes evolutionary views of doctrine. It shows that modern Protestant complexities — perspicuity, the justification and sanctification distinction — are not in the text. They are not derived from simple Bible reading. The Orthodox faith is simple: baptism, Eucharist, love, repentance, trust in Christ. We should present Christianity simply, as Irenaeus and Scripture do. The preaching of the truth and the way of life are one thing.
