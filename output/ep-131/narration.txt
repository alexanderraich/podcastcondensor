In this episode of the Lord of Spirits podcast, Fathers Andrew Stephen Damick and Stephen DeYoung take us on a long journey through the history of death and burial. They start not with written history, but with the very oldest graves we know about. They look at the burials of Neanderthals and early Homo sapiens, and they make a bold claim right away. These were not separate species. Neanderthals, Denisovans, and Homo sapiens all interbred, and they are all human. And their graves show it. At Shanidar Cave in Iraq, at La Chapelle-aux-Saints in France, and at Kebara Cave in Palestine, we find deliberate burials. Bodies are placed in specific positions, sometimes with grave goods. At Qafzeh Cave, early humans buried their dead with shells and ochre. At Panga ya Saidi in Kenya, a toddler was laid to rest on a pillow of plant material, covered with a blanket of earth. The hosts point out that these practices are remarkably similar across vast distances of time and space. This suggests a shared culture, a common human story, and it points back to what they call an original monotheism. Burial is not just a practical way to dispose of a body. It is a deeply ritual act.

The conversation moves forward through history. At Çatalhöyük in Turkey, people buried their dead under the floors of their homes, and sometimes plastered and painted the skulls. In Sumeria, commoners were buried under floors, while elites were placed in brick vaults, sometimes with human sacrifices and grave goods. In ancient Egypt, we see the full flowering of the death cult, with mummification, pyramids, and elaborate tombs. The Greeks had their own customs, with a viewing of the body, a procession, and sacrifices. The Romans initially cremated their dead and buried them outside the city walls, and they kept ancestor masks for processions. But over time, under Christian influence, they shifted back to inhumation, to burying the whole body in the ground.

This brings the hosts to the heart of the episode, which is Christian burial. They emphasize that the Orthodox Church forbids cremation. It rejects the human throughline of burial, and it rejects the hope of the resurrection. The body matters. It is not just a shell to be discarded. The hosts explain that caring for the dead is one of the greatest acts of love. They point to Tobit, who risked his life to bury the dead, and to Joseph of Arimathea, who buried the body of Jesus in his own tomb. This is why Orthodox parishes have a burial ministry. It is a way of loving the departed and of witnessing to the resurrection.

The hosts also address deep theological questions about the afterlife. They explain that before the harrowing of Hades, all humanity went to Hades, the underworld. But after Christ died and descended into Hades, he brought the righteous out and led them to paradise. Now, only the wicked go to Hades. The righteous go to paradise. And here is a key point. The resurrection of the righteous is trans-temporal. It crosses time. The effects of Christ’s resurrection are not bound by linear time. So the righteous are already experiencing resurrection in a heavenly form, even though the general resurrection is still in the future. This is why Matthew’s gospel says that after Jesus rose, holy people left their tombs and entered the city. That event is a glimpse of this trans-temporal reality.

The hosts also touch on the nature of time for the departed. They discuss the story of Moses, whose body was disputed over by the archangel Michael, as mentioned in the letter of Jude. This suggests a special burial for Moses, which connects to his appearance at the Transfiguration. They also clarify that when Jesus says, “Let the dead bury their own dead,” he is not forbidding burial. He is rebuking excuses for not following him.

Finally, they bring it all back to the funeral service itself. The Orthodox vigil includes the reading of Psalms. The body is vested, and for clergy, that means vesting them in their priestly garments. The body is buried facing east, awaiting the rising of the sun and the rising of Christ. Memorial services are held, and kolyva, boiled wheat, is offered as a symbol of the resurrection. And the Church sings “Memory Eternal,” asking God to remember the departed. This is a contrast to pagan ideas, where human memory keeps the dead alive. For Christians, it is God who remembers, and his memory is eternal. The hosts conclude that love is more powerful than death. This is the core teaching of the episode. Burial is an act of love, rooted in the hope of the resurrection, and revealed fully in Christ.
