In this episode, Father Andrew and Father Stephen take on the Torah, the first five books of the Bible. They want to clear up a big misunderstanding about what Jesus meant when He said He came not to abolish the Torah but to fulfill it. A lot of people think that means the Torah is done away with, but the hosts argue the exact opposite. The Torah is the foundation of everything. The rest of the Old Testament and the whole New Testament are built on it. The Gospels and the letters of St. Paul are all grounded in the Torah.

So what does it mean that Jesus fulfilled the Torah? It does not mean the commandments are relaxed. Jesus said not a single yod or tittle will pass away until all is accomplished. The hosts stress that every commandment remains in effect, and Christians are called to do and teach them. The religion of the Old Testament and the religion of the New Testament are the same religion. Christianity is not a new religion. It is the fulfillment of the faith of Israel.

One of the key ideas in this episode is what they call death by holiness. God’s holiness is dangerous to sinful humans. Think of the expulsion from paradise in Genesis chapter three. Sinful humanity cannot just walk into God’s presence and survive. So the Torah provides structures, like the tabernacle, to allow God to dwell among His people safely. These are external structures that prepared humanity for the internal indwelling of the Holy Spirit in the New Testament.

The hosts also talk about the difference between moral purity and ceremonial purity. Moral purity is about being clean from sin. Ceremonial purity is about ritual cleanliness, like not touching a dead body. Both are needed to approach God, but they are not the same thing. Sin is portrayed in the Torah as a disease, a metaphysical taint. It requires quarantine, purification, and healing. That is what the sacrificial system is all about. It is a sin management system. And this understanding carries over into the sacraments.

Now, what about St. Paul? People often think he opposed the Torah. The hosts say that is a historical misinterpretation. Paul was a Pharisee who became an apostle to the Gentiles. He did not reject the Torah. He affirmed it. He sought to reconcile Jewish and Gentile Christians, not to start a new religion. In Romans chapter two, Paul says that doers of the Torah are justified, and Gentiles who keep it by nature are a law to themselves.

So how do the commandments apply to Gentile Christians? That was the question at the Council of Jerusalem in Acts chapter fifteen. St. James, the leader of the Jerusalem church, issued the ruling. Gentile Christians are not required to be circumcised. But they are to abstain from idolatry, sexual immorality, and consuming blood. This ruling is based on the holiness code in Leviticus chapter eighteen, which gives commandments for both Israelites and sojourners. So Gentile Christians are bound to the commandments directed to sojourners, not to all the commandments given to Israel.

The hosts also touch on the danger of rejecting the Old Testament. They mention Marcion, a second-century heretic who rejected the Old Testament and its God. And they mention Martin Luther, whose anti-Semitic writings contributed to the marginalization of the Old Testament and later atrocities. They even bring up Friedrich Nietzsche, who saw a break between Jesus and St. Paul, but in the opposite direction, thinking Paul was judaizing Jesus’ teachings.

There is also a discussion of what is called the high-handed sin in Numbers chapter fifteen. This is a sin committed with a high hand, meaning it is unrepentant and defiant. That kind of sin cannot be atoned for by sacrifices. It results in being cut off from the people. Repentance is necessary for forgiveness.

Finally, the hosts express hope for a future mass conversion of Jewish people to Orthodox Christianity. The Torah is not abolished. It is fulfilled in Christ. And the whole story of Scripture, from Genesis to Revelation, is one continuous story of God dwelling with His people.
