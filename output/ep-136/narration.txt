In this episode, Fathers Andrew and Stephen take on a question that sits at the very heart of the Christian faith: what must I do to be saved? And their answer might surprise you. They argue that the common understanding of salvation—that it’s about escaping hell and going to heaven when you die—is never actually what the word “saved” means in the Bible. Instead, salvation is about theosis, becoming like God through faithful participation in the way of life He sets before humanity.

They trace this theme all the way back to the beginning. Adam was given three commandments, not just one. He was told to fill the earth, to subdue it, and not to eat from the tree of knowledge. These weren’t arbitrary rules. They were a way of life, a path leading to theosis. Adam was on a path to salvation before the fall, and salvation for him meant becoming like God through faithful obedience. St. Basil the Great is quoted here, reminding us that man is commanded to become God. That was the original purpose, and it remains the core of salvation throughout Scripture.

The hosts then walk through the stories of Noah, Abraham, and Moses. Noah was saved from the corruption of his generation through the flood, which prefigures baptism. And after being saved, he was told to continue the original mandate to fill and subdue the earth. Abraham’s faithfulness, not mere belief, was credited as righteousness. St. Paul makes this clear. And Moses, who spoke with God face to face, was himself being transformed into the likeness of God. In every case, salvation is received through faithfulness, not through intellectual agreement or legalistic works.

They also unpack the Torah, the Law given through Moses. It functions on three levels. The lowest concerns the land and life in it. The middle deals with managing sin through commandments and sacrifices. But the highest and most important level is about imaging God, and that is summarized by justice, mercy, and faithfulness. Christ Himself points to these as the weightier matters of the law. The Torah does not and cannot solve the problem of sin. It manages sin by bringing knowledge of it, providing means of atonement, and teaching about its nature. But it points beyond itself to something greater.

That something greater is Christ. He is the second Adam, the perfect image of God. He succeeds where Adam failed, and His incarnation is the basis for theosis. His death and resurrection remove the obstacles of sin, death, and demonic powers. The curse on the serpent in Genesis implies the defeat of those powers, and that defeat is part of salvation. Christ recapitulates Israel’s history and accomplishes what no one else could.

The hosts also address listener questions. They talk about worship and veneration, and they push back hard on the rapture and dispensationalism. They examine Isaiah fifty-three, where it says it was the will of the Lord to crush Him. They argue this does not mean penal substitutionary atonement, the idea that the Father punished the Son in our place. Rather, it means Christ’s suffering was part of God’s plan, not that the Father was pouring out wrath on Him. They also critique the “River of Fire” theology popularized by Alexander Kalomiros, which suggests that heaven and hell are the same experience of God’s love, just experienced differently. The hosts say this distorts scriptural language and presents God as passive, contradicting the active judgment we see in the Gospels. They also reject apocatastasis, the belief that all will eventually be saved.

Finally, they turn to Ephesians chapter two. We are saved by grace through faithfulness, not by works, but we are created in Christ Jesus for good works. Those works are part of our original purpose. They are the means by which we become like Christ and bring life to the world. So salvation is not just about what we are saved from—sin, death, and demons. It is also about what we are saved for. The focus should be on that positive purpose, on becoming like Christ and transforming the world around us. That is the call. That is the way of life. And that is what it means to be saved.
