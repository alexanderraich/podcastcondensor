In this episode of the Lord of Spirits, Fathers Andrew Stephen Damick and Stephen De Young take a deep dive into two of the Bible’s most mysterious and terrifying creatures: Leviathan and Behemoth. These are not just ancient sea monsters or mythical beasts. They are spiritual beings that embody chaos, destruction, and tyranny. The fathers begin by explaining that the Hebrew name Leviathan means a twisting, fugitive sea serpent. This image connects directly to the ancient Canaanite god of the sea, Yam, and his dragon, Lotan. In the myths of the ancient Near East, the storm god Baal defeats Yam, and the Babylonian god Marduk defeats the chaos dragon Tiamat. But the Bible flips this story on its head. It is not a battle between equal forces of order and chaos. God alone is sovereign, and He defeats Leviathan as an act of creation and salvation.

The hosts trace Leviathan through the Old Testament. In Psalm seventy-four, God is praised for breaking the heads of Leviathan and feeding him to the people of the wilderness, even to the Ethiopians. This is not just a victory over a monster. It is a demonstration that God controls the sea, the storms, and all the forces that seem to threaten His order. In Isaiah twenty-seven, the Lord punishes Leviathan, the fugitive serpent, and slays the dragon that is in the sea. This points forward to a final, eschatological victory. Then there is the book of Job. In chapter forty-one, Leviathan is described with crocodile-like features, but also as a creature that breathes fire. That combination tells us this is no ordinary animal. It is a supernatural being, untamable by man, yet completely under God’s control.

Alongside Leviathan, the fathers introduce Behemoth. In Job chapter forty, Behemoth is a land beast of immense strength, associated with the underworld and with virility. The hosts connect this figure to the Ugaritic Bull El, the chief god of the Canaanite pantheon. Behemoth represents tyrannical power on the earth, the oppressive force of empires and false religions. The fathers explain that these ancient gods were not just imaginary. They were hypostases, meaning they were embodied in physical phenomena. Yam was instantiated in the sea itself, and Leviathan was the spiritual power behind it. This helps us understand how spiritual forces manifest in the world.

The conversation then moves to the New Testament, specifically Revelation chapter thirteen. The beast from the sea is clearly identified as Leviathan. It combines features from Daniel’s four beasts, showing that it represents the demonic forces behind all empires. The beast from the earth, with horns like a lamb but speaking like a dragon, is Behemoth. This is the false prophet, the power of false religion that leads people away from God. The fathers also discuss the historical context of Revelation. They argue that Nero is the prototypical antichrist figure, and the number six hundred sixty-six refers to him through gematria, the ancient system of assigning numerical value to letters. Nero persecuted Christians brutally, and he becomes the model for all future persecuting emperors and tyrants.

Finally, the fathers apply all of this to our own lives. They urge Christians not to respond to empire with worldly power, fear, or political anxiety. Instead, we are called to repentance, transformation, and faithful witness. God is sovereign over chaos. He not only defeats Leviathan and Behemoth, but He uses them for His purposes. In Revelation nineteen, the great supper of God is described, where the beasts are thrown into the lake of fire. This fulfills the ancient imagery of Leviathan and Behemoth being eaten. The powers of chaos and tyranny are not ultimate. They are food for the righteous. The message is clear: do not be afraid of the monsters of this world. Trust in the God who controls the sea, the earth, and all the powers within them.
