Father Andrew and Father Stephen open this episode with a long and important introduction. They want to talk about the Passion narratives in all four Gospels, but first, they have to address a big problem: harmonization. That’s the practice of taking all four accounts and blending them into one single story. The fathers argue that this creates a sort of fifth gospel, one that doesn’t actually exist in the Bible. And that fifth gospel is not authoritative. In fact, they say it can lead to heresy, because it obscures the distinct theological point each evangelist is trying to make.

They point out that the differences between the Gospels are not contradictions. They are the natural result of eyewitness testimony. Different people notice different details. St. John Chrysostom made this point long ago, saying that when witnesses agree on everything perfectly, you should be suspicious. Variation is a sign of authenticity. The fathers also critique both liberal and fundamentalist approaches to Scripture as being modernist. Both sides are obsessed with proving historical facts, when the real point is theological meaning.

They mention Tatian, an early Christian writer who made the Diatessaron, a harmony of the Gospels, back in the second century. And they bring up the so-called Secret Gospel of Mark, which turned out to be a modern forgery. The point is, harmonizing is an old temptation, but it’s the wrong way to read.

Then they get into the accounts themselves. They start with Mark, the earliest Gospel, written after St. Peter’s martyrdom. They read through the Passion, and they emphasize that Jesus’ cry from the cross, “My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?” is not a statement of abandonment. It is the opening line of Psalm twenty-two. And that psalm is a psalm of deliverance. It ends with God answering and saving. So Jesus is praying the psalm, and the whole psalm is the context.

In Matthew’s account, there is a lot of Old Testament typology. The fathers see the Day of Atonement all over it. Jesus is both the scapegoat and the sacrificial goat. The scarlet robe and the reed point to that. And when the temple curtain tears, that is not about opening access to all people. It is a sign of judgment against the temple and the high priesthood. God’s presence has departed. Matthew also records the resurrection of the saints, which is the harrowing of Hades. Christ descends into the place of the dead and leads out the righteous.

They also talk about Barabbas. His name means “son of the father.” And he represents the false messianic expectation of violent insurrection. The crowd chooses the son of the father who fights with swords over the Son of the Father who comes in peace. Pilate, meanwhile, is not a man of justice. He is cowardly and superstitious. He is motivated by his wife’s dream, not by any sense of right and wrong.

Luke’s account gives us the penitent thief, St. Dysmas. The fathers note that his salvation is an exceptional case, not the norm. He is venerated as a saint, but you cannot plan on a deathbed confession like that.

Then they turn to John’s Gospel, which is full of new creation imagery. The crucifixion is the beginning of the new creation. Mary is there, and Jesus calls her “woman,” echoing Genesis. But then he gives her to the beloved disciple, and she becomes the mother of all believers. She is the new Eve. And when the soldier, traditionally St. Longinus, pierces Christ’s side, blood and water flow out. That echoes the creation of Eve from Adam’s side in Genesis chapter two. The blood and water are the sacraments of the Eucharist and Baptism. The Church is born from the side of Christ, just as Eve was born from Adam.

John also cites Zechariah, about looking on the one they have pierced. That identifies Jesus as God Himself, and it points to a fountain of cleansing for repentance. And Jesus’ reference to the Son of Man coming with the clouds is a direct allusion to Daniel chapter seven. He is claiming divine authority and enthronement.

The fathers close by reminding us that the crucifixion is the single best-attested fact from the ancient world. Even Bart Ehrman, the agnostic scholar they call a friend of the show, affirms that. So the question is not whether it happened. The question is what it means. And each Gospel tells us something different and essential about that meaning.
