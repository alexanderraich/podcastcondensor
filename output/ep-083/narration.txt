In this episode, Father Andrew and Father Stephen take us deep into the Feast of Theophany, the celebration of Christ’s baptism. They begin with a name that might surprise you: Baal. The word simply means “lord” or “master,” but it came to refer to a specific Canaanite storm god, the rebellious son of El. The prophets of the Old Testament, especially in Ezekiel twenty-eight and Isaiah fourteen, identify this Baal with the devil himself. And the worship of Baal was a constant problem for Israel, particularly during the time of the Omride dynasty, when the Phoenician princess Jezebel promoted it in the northern kingdom. Her very name, they point out, is a Hebrew pun meaning “Where is Baal?”—a mocking question.

The hosts then connect this ancient struggle to Adam. The devil’s envy of Adam’s destiny led to the fall in the Garden of Eden. But there is a tradition, found in the Life of Adam and Eve, that Adam repented after his sin. He stood in the Jordan River for forty days, and the water stopped flowing as a sign of creation’s mourning. This sets the stage for understanding Christ’s baptism. When Jesus enters the Jordan, he is not just receiving a ritual washing. He is entering the very place where Adam repented, and he is winning the first victory over the devil for all of humanity. Christ, as the new Adam, undoes Adam’s failure.

Father Andrew and Father Stephen make a bold claim about the Church calendar. Theophany, they argue, was originally the primary feast of the Incarnation, not Christmas. It is the second oldest Christian feast, celebrated since at least the early second century. The baptism of Christ is the moment when God is manifested in the flesh, and the Trinity is revealed. This is why the feast is called Theophany, which means “appearance of God.”

They also draw a fascinating parallel between Elijah and Saint John the Forerunner. Elijah’s showdown with the prophets of Baal on Mount Carmel is a type of Christ’s baptism. Fire came down from heaven then, and Christ himself speaks of casting fire on the earth and having a baptism to be baptized with, as we hear in Luke’s Gospel. The fire of judgment and purification is tied to the waters of the Jordan.

The episode then turns to the meaning of the Incarnation for our own identity. Christ’s identity is his entire existence. He is fully God and fully man, and his baptism reveals who he is. Through baptism, we put on Christ and find our true identity in him. We become theophanic persons, people who manifest God to the world. This is the destiny that the devil envied in Adam, and it is restored in Christ.

The hosts also touch on the garments of skin from Genesis, which they interpret as our mortal flesh. These garments provide the capacity for repentance. They are not a punishment so much as a place where we can turn back to God. In Christ, we are offered new garments, the garments of immortality.

So the story of Theophany is not just about a moment in the past. It is about the cosmic battle between the devil and God, between Baal and the true Lord. It is about Adam’s failure and his repentance in the Jordan. And it is about Christ’s victory, a victory that we share through baptism. The water of the Jordan is the place where chaos is conquered, where the devil is defeated, and where we are given a new identity in Christ. This is the heart of the feast, and it is a message that echoes from the prophets to the Gospel and into our own lives today.
