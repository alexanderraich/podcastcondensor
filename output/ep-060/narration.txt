In this episode, Father Stephen De Young takes us back to the ancient world, to the Neo-Babylonian Empire of the mid-first millennium BC. He reads and comments on a remarkable Akkadian text known as the Babylonian Theodicy. It’s a dialogue between a sufferer and his friend, and it sounds a lot like the Book of Job. The sufferer is a pious man. He has done everything right. He has offered the sacrifices, he has said the prayers, he has honored the gods. And yet, his life is falling apart. He laments his misfortunes, and he cannot understand why the gods have abandoned him.

His friend, on the other hand, gives him the standard religious advice of the day. He tells him to keep up the traditional worship, to keep appeasing the gods, and not to expect any immediate results. The friend says the ways of the gods are remote and incomprehensible. You just have to keep doing your part. This is what Father Stephen calls a quid pro quo religion. You give the gods their offerings, and in exchange, they are supposed to give you their favor. But the sufferer sees the problem. He did everything right, and it didn’t work. The system is broken. He finds it completely ineffective.

The friend has another argument, too. He says that humanity itself is broken. The gods gave people twisted words when they created them. So, of course, people praise the wicked and revile the good. That’s just the way the world is. It’s a bleak picture. The Babylonian gods are capricious and distant. They are powerful, but they are not good in any way that we would recognize. They don’t operate according to a moral order that makes sense to us.

Father Stephen contrasts this entire worldview with the Book of Job. In the Babylonian text, the gods are ultimately unknowable and arbitrary. You serve them because you have to, not because they are just. But in the Book of Job, God is different. He is sovereign, and He is just. His ways are mysterious, yes, but they are not arbitrary. Job’s suffering is not a punishment for his sins. It is a test of his faith. And in the end, God reveals Himself to Job. He doesn’t give Job a neat explanation, but He shows up. He speaks. He restores Job’s fortunes. The story ends with a relationship, not just a transaction.

The episode really digs into the problem of theodicy, the question of why bad things happen to good people. The Babylonian Theodicy is one of the oldest attempts to wrestle with that question, and it ends in confusion and despair. The friend’s advice is basically to give up on understanding and just keep going through the motions. But the biblical revelation offers something else. It offers a God who is personally involved, who allows suffering for a purpose, and who ultimately brings justice out of it.

Father Stephen points out that the Babylonian text has a lacuna, a gap in the manuscript where some content is missing. But even with that gap, the message is clear. The pagan religion of Babylon was a dead end. It couldn’t answer the sufferer’s cries. It could only tell him to keep sacrificing and hope for the best. The Book of Job, by contrast, shows us a God who hears the cry of the afflicted and who is working all things together for good, even when we cannot see it. The contrast between the two is stark. One is a religion of fear and manipulation. The other is a revelation of a loving and just God. Father Stephen and Father Andrew walk through this ancient text to show us just how different the God of Israel is from the gods of the nations. And they remind us that our faith is not about getting God to do what we want. It’s about trusting Him, even in the darkest times.
