In this Christmas special, Fathers Andrew Stephen Damick and Stephen DeYoung tackle a question that sits at the very heart of the feast: what does the incarnation of Jesus Christ have to do with time? They begin by challenging a common assumption, arguing that time and space are not objective realities that exist on their own. Instead, they are categories of human experience, much like color, which only exists in relation to a perceiver. This is a crucial starting point, because it helps explain why God, who is not finite, is not bound by these categories at all.

The conversation then turns to what it truly means for God to be eternal. The fathers reject two popular ideas. They push back against the Platonic notion that eternity is a kind of static, unchanging stillness, and they also dismiss the popular idea that eternity is just an endless amount of time. Instead, they assert that temporal categories simply do not apply to God. There is no past or future for Him. He does not exist at a point in time, and He is not a being among other beings. As St. Maximus the Confessor taught, God does not exist in the same way creatures do. This means that questions about God’s foreknowledge or predestination, which assume He is looking ahead from a point in time, are ultimately incoherent. The fathers even critique the evangelical philosopher William Lane Craig for his views on this, noting that his belief that God exists within time creates more problems than it solves.

So, how does an infinite God interact with finite humans? The answer, they say, is the incarnation itself. This is the solution to the problem. By taking on human nature, the eternal Son of God enters into human experience, bridging the gap between the infinite and the finite. Through this union, Christ’s humanity, being united to His divinity, becomes eternal and omnipresent. This is why He can appear throughout history, and it is why the saints can hear our prayers. They do not hear them through physical sound waves. Rather, their humanity, being united to Christ, participates in His divine omnipresence. The fathers point to St. Nicholas, who is known for appearing in multiple places at once, as a perfect example of this reality.

This leads to a discussion of theosis, the process by which humans are called to participate in the divine nature. The fathers quote St. Basil the Great, who expressed the idea that humanity is commanded to become God, which is only possible through the incarnation. They reference St. Paul’s letter to the Ephesians, where he speaks of Christ’s descent and ascent, and they explain that this is the very purpose of the incarnation. They also bring up St. John, who saw himself in glory in the book of Revelation, illustrating that the saints are already seated with Christ in heavenly places. The fathers are careful to clarify that this does not mean a cessation of Christ’s divinity, which they call kenosis, but rather His self-emptying in humility and suffering. They also reject the Calvinist doctrine of the Extra Calvinisticum, which confines Christ’s human nature to a specific place while His divine nature is everywhere. Instead, they insist that His humanity is fully united to His divinity.

Finally, they reflect on the implications for Christian life and the hope of the resurrection. The physicality of Christ’s resurrection body refutes the early heresy of Docetism, which claimed He only appeared to be human. The incarnation is not a temporary arrangement. It is eternal. And because of it, believers are called to share in that eternity, transcending the limitations of time and death. The episode closes with a reading from the book of Revelation, emphasizing the glory of Christ and the worship of the heavenly hosts, reminding us that the one who was slain is worthy of all praise, yesterday, today, and forever.
