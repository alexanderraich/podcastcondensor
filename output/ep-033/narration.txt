This episode wraps up a four-part series on Old Testament Christology, and the hosts, Father Andrew and Father Stephen, focus on the times God appeared in human form before the Incarnation. They look closely at three major visions, starting with the prophet Daniel. In the tenth chapter of Daniel, we meet a "heavenly man" described with a face like lightning, eyes like flaming torches, and a body like beryl. This figure is so overwhelming that Daniel loses all strength. The hosts point out that this description is nearly identical to the vision of the Son of Man in the first chapter of Revelation, which strongly suggests this heavenly man is the pre-incarnate Christ, the second person of the Trinity, appearing in human form long before Bethlehem.

The episode then moves to Ezekiel’s famous vision in the first chapter. Ezekiel sees a great cloud with fire and brightness, and within it, four living creatures. These cherubim each have four faces: a man, a lion, an ox, and an eagle. Father Andrew and Father Stephen connect these four faces to the four cardinal signs of the zodiac, showing that the angelic beings and the constellations are tied together, pointing to the cosmic scope of God’s throne. Above these creatures is a firmament, and on top of that, a throne. Seated on the throne is a figure with the likeness of a man, but He is surrounded by fire and brilliant light. This is the Merkavah, the chariot-throne of God, and the man on the throne is again identified as Christ. The vision is not just a display of power; it is a revelation of the One who will take on human flesh.

The third major passage is from Exodus chapter twenty-four. Here, Moses, Aaron, and the elders of Israel go up the mountain, and they see the God of Israel. Under His feet is something like a pavement of sapphire, clear as the sky. And remarkably, they eat and drink in His presence. This covenant ratification ceremony involves blood sprinkled on the people and a sacred meal shared with God. The hosts draw a direct line from this meal to the Eucharist. Just as the elders were sealed by blood and then feasted with God, so Christians are sealed by the blood of Christ and then fed at His table. The Latin term sacramentum, which means a sacred oath, is brought up here, emphasizing that the Eucharist is not just a symbol but an oath-taking, a covenant bond.

The hosts also revisit the story of Abraham in Genesis eighteen, where Yahweh appears as a man with two angels. Abraham feeds Him, and He eats. This is a profound act of divine condescension. But the crucial point is what happens next. God discusses the judgment of Sodom with Abraham. The hosts explain that the Incarnation inverts this pattern. In Genesis, God comes down to judge. In the New Testament, Christ comes down not to condemn the world but to save it. The judgment is still real, but the primary motive of every divine appearance is soteriological—it is always for the sake of human salvation. God does not reveal Himself for spectacle. He reveals Himself to rescue us.

The episode also touches on the battle described in Daniel, where the heavenly man is opposed by the Prince of Persia, a fallen angelic ruler, and is helped by Michael, the archangel. This shows that the pre-incarnate Christ is actively engaged in spiritual warfare on behalf of His people. The hosts emphasize that all of these appearances, whether to Daniel, Ezekiel, Abraham, or the elders of Israel, are not random theophanies. They are all pointing to the same reality: God desires to be with us, and He will go to any length, even taking on human flesh and dying on a cross, to accomplish our salvation. The heavenly man of the Old Testament is the same Lord who rises from the dead and appears to His disciples, and He is the same Lord who meets us in the Eucharist today.
