Father Andrew Stephen Damick and Father Stephen De Young open this episode by looking at the devil and Satan, and they quickly make a crucial point. These are not simply two names for the same being. The word devil comes from the Greek *diabolos*, meaning one who throws apart or a slanderer. Satan, on the other hand, is a Hebrew title meaning adversary or accuser. In the book of Job, for instance, Satan appears among the sons of God, not as a fallen prince of darkness, but as a prosecuting attorney, testing and accusing Job. So the question arises: are these two distinct figures, or two aspects of the same spiritual reality? The hosts note that Saint Andrew of Caesarea, an early commentator on Revelation, suggested they might be both. In Revelation twelve, the dragon is called both the ancient serpent and the devil and Satan, which seems to blend these traditions together.

Now, a very popular idea, largely thanks to John Milton’s *Paradise Lost*, is that the devil was a high angel who rebelled against God before creation and was cast out of heaven in a primordial war. Father Andrew and Father Stephen push back on this. They argue that the devil fell after the creation of the world, and the reason for his fall was envy. He looked at humanity, made in the image of God, and he resented us. This is why the serpent in Genesis three is identified with the devil. The devil, out of envy, corrupted humanity, and in doing so, he was cursed and cast down. He was given power over death, but he is not a rival god. He is a creature, and a defeated one at that.

The episode then introduces some less familiar figures from the Old Testament and Second Temple Jewish literature. There is Azazel, who appears in Leviticus sixteen, in the Day of Atonement ritual. Two goats are brought before the Lord. One is sacrificed, and the other is sent away into the wilderness, bearing the sins of the people. That second goat is often called the scapegoat, but the hosts insist that Azazel is not a goat. He is a demonic spirit, a leader of fallen angels who corrupted humanity. The parallel language in the text and the unanimous interpretation of Jewish literature from that period make this clear. And this matters for theology. The scapegoat is not sacrificed. It is sent away alive. So the idea that this ritual points to a penal substitutionary atonement, where sin is placed on a sacrificed animal, simply does not hold up.

Then there is Mastema, a figure from the Book of Jubilees. He is the leader of the disembodied spirits of the giants, and God allows him to tempt and torment the wicked. He is a tool of divine judgment, not an independent force of evil. And there is Belial, a name that means worthlessness or lawlessness. In Second Temple literature, Belial is the opponent of the Messiah, the embodiment of rebellion against God. The New Testament picks up on this when it asks what harmony can exist between Christ and Belial.

The hosts also mention Sammael, an archangel in Jewish tradition, often identified as the angel of death and the adversary. His name means poison of God. These figures are not meant to form a neat, systematic demonology. The point is discernment. Christians are called to recognize the presence of evil spirits in the world and in their own hearts. And that brings us to the practical application. Spiritual warfare, as taught by Saint Paul in Ephesians six, is not about exposing evil or chasing demons. It is about putting on the whole armor of God. It is about truth, righteousness, faith, and prayer. It is about personal holiness. The devil and his angels are real, but they are already defeated. Our job is not to be experts on demons. Our job is to be faithful to Christ.
