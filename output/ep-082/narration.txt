Father Andrew Stephen Damick and Father Stephen De Young open this episode by tackling a question that often comes up: is the Orthodox Christian practice of having patron saints just a dressed-up version of paganism? They want to answer that charge directly, and they do so by showing that the Bible actually presents a very robust spiritual reality. In the Scriptures, angelic beings are assigned to elements of nature, to peoples, and to individual persons. And human saints, through the process of theosis, come to fill the vacancies left by fallen angels.

The conversation begins in the Old Testament, with the divine council. This is the biblical and Second Temple Jewish understanding that there is a council of divine beings, sometimes called gods or holy ones, who serve as administrators under God. They govern creation and the nations. Father Stephen points to Deuteronomy chapter four, verse nineteen, where God allots the sun, moon, and stars to the nations. This is not just about astronomy; it is about angelic beings assigned to peoples. Then they turn to Psalm eighty-two, where God stands in the divine council and judges the gods who have failed to administer justice. This psalm is not just a relic of the past. It is a prophecy of Christ’s authority over the nations. When Christ says in Matthew twenty-eight that all authority in heaven and on earth has been given to Him, He is fulfilling that psalm and commissioning the apostles to make disciples of all nations.

The hosts make a clear distinction here. This is not polytheism. These beings are created and subordinate to God. They are holy ones, a term used in the Old Testament for the angelic beings surrounding God’s throne, and later applied to human saints. Father Andrew and Father Stephen explain that salvation is not merely about escaping hell. It is about becoming part of the divine council, sharing in God’s stewardship of creation, and being co-enthroned with Christ. Jesus Himself says in Luke twenty, verse thirty-six, that those who are sons of the resurrection are equal to the angels. That is a stunning claim, and it is the foundation for understanding why human saints can take on these roles.

They bring in Philo of Alexandria, the first-century Jewish philosopher, who wrote about the divine administration and the subordinate role of heavenly bodies as gods under the one supreme God. This was not a fringe idea. It was a common understanding in the ancient world. The early church carried this forward. Saint John Chrysostom is quoted on how people become like demons through sin, not through violent death. The point is that alignment of will matters. This is what the Greek term koinonia means, participation or fellowship. It describes the synergistic relationship between humans and spiritual beings, whether God, angels, or demons, through the alignment of wills and activities.

The hosts then apply this to the practice of patron saints. They use the example of Saint Demetrius, the patron saint of Thessaloniki. He replaced Aphrodite as the spirit of the city, not because of some magical transfer, but because the people of the city realigned themselves with Christ. The spirit of a place is not a fixed metaphysical entity. It is phenomenological, a relational and experiential reality. It is how a being relates to and influences a group, a place, or a person. When a community turns to Christ, the spiritual character of that place changes.

They also address the question of heresy. A heretic is not simply someone who is wrong. A heretic is someone who, after being corrected by the Church, refuses to repent and persists in teaching error. Arius is the classic example. He refused correction and was condemned at the Council of Nicaea. Saint Augustine, on the other hand, made theological errors, but he retracted them and remained in the Church. The difference is repentance.

The episode closes with a critique of liberal theology. This is the post-Enlightenment approach that sees history as progressing toward a pinnacle, often the present culture, and interprets that development as the work of the Holy Spirit. Father Stephen and Father Andrew argue that this view is inherently chauvinist, racist, and colonial, because it dismisses other cultures and traditions as backward. It is a spirit of the age, a zeitgeist, that stands in opposition to Holy Tradition. Holy Tradition is not just a set of old customs. It is the life of the Holy Spirit in the Church, the historical instances where humans synergized with the Holy Spirit, and those instances serve as authoritative guides.

The practice of patron saints, then, is not a leftover from paganism. It is rooted in the biblical understanding of holy ones assigned to guide and intercede. And it points to the ultimate hope that glorified humans will share in the angelic roles, filling the vacancies left by the fallen, and joining the divine council in Christ.
