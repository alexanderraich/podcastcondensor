In this episode, Father Andrew and Father Stephen take a deep look at Cyrus the Great and the Persian Empire. They start by contrasting Persia with the empires that came before it, like the Assyrians and the Neo-Babylonians. The Persian Empire was different. It was vast, but it was also incredibly well organized. It had a sophisticated bureaucracy and established trade routes that earlier empires just couldn't match. And perhaps most importantly, it practiced a level of religious tolerance that was almost unheard of in the ancient world.

The hosts walk through Cyrus’s rise to power. They talk about his conquests, including the famous story of how he had to get around the Median Wall, a massive defensive structure built by the Babylonians. Eventually, Cyrus captured Babylon itself in 539 BC. This was the moment that ended the Jewish exile. The Babylonians, under Nebuchadnezzar, had destroyed Jerusalem and taken the people captive. But Cyrus came in and changed everything. He issued a decree allowing the Jews to return home and rebuild their temple. This is recorded in the first chapter of Ezra, and it’s also the note that the book of Second Chronicles ends on. It was the fulfillment of Jeremiah’s prophecy.

The conversation then turns to Persian religion, specifically Zoroastrianism. They discuss its founder, Zoroaster, who reformed the older Iranian paganism. He established the worship of a supreme god called Ahura Mazda, who created the universe out of light and embodied justice and goodness. The hosts are careful to clear up a common misconception here. People often think Zoroastrianism is a dualistic religion, with a good god and an evil god locked in an eternal struggle. But that’s not quite right. The destructive spirit, Angra Mainyu, is not a separate, co-equal deity. He’s more like a shadow of Ahura Mazda. The religion also had some unusual practices, like sky burials and the use of a hallucinogenic drink made from a plant called haoma. And they mention the Magi, the priests of this religion, who interpreted dreams and performed rituals.

Father Andrew and Father Stephen also talk about Cyrus’s personal piety, or rather, his lack of it. They suggest his famous religious tolerance probably didn’t come from some deep theological commitment. It was more likely just a practical policy. He simply wasn’t that devout himself, so he didn’t care much what gods other people worshipped. It was a smart administrative move that kept his empire stable.

But the heart of the episode is a theological reflection on a passage from the prophet Isaiah. In chapter forty-five, God calls Cyrus his anointed one. The word used there is messiah. This is a stunning thing. Cyrus was a pagan king, a foreigner who didn’t know God. And yet, God calls him his shepherd and his anointed. God says he will use Cyrus to rebuild Jerusalem and the temple, even though Cyrus doesn’t even know him. The hosts emphasize that this shows God can work through anyone, even a pagan ruler, to accomplish his purposes. God is sovereign over all history, and he can anoint whoever he chooses for his own plans.

The episode closes with some lessons for the listener. If God could use Cyrus, a man who knew nothing of the true God, then we should be humble about our own importance. We should also be aware that God might be preparing us for a calling we don’t yet understand. And we should be ready to serve his purposes, no matter where we find ourselves in the world. The story of Cyrus is a powerful reminder that God’s hand is at work in all of history, and he can use anyone to bring about his will.
