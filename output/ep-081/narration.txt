In this episode, Fathers Andrew Stephen Damick and Stephen De Young take a deep look at technology, but not in the way you might expect. They aren't just talking about smartphones and computers. They trace the whole idea of technology back to the ancient Greek concept of *techne*, which simply means know-how. It’s the knowledge of how to manipulate things in the world to get a desired result. And once you understand that, you realize technology is much bigger than gadgets. It includes ideas, concepts, and even entire worldviews. These are all tools we use to shape our lives and our surroundings.

The fathers make a crucial point right away. Technology itself is not evil. It’s a form of knowledge, and knowledge can be used for good or for ill. The real problem is that humanity lacks the spiritual maturity to use it wisely. They point to the Tree of the Knowledge of Good and Evil in Genesis. That tree wasn’t forbidden because knowledge was bad. It was forbidden because humanity wasn’t ready for it. The same principle applies to our collective use of technology. We need a certain level of spiritual wisdom, a kind of collective maturity, before we can handle certain kinds of power.

In the ancient world, techne extended into the spiritual realm. People used rituals, sacrifices, and even magic as instrumental ways to manipulate spiritual beings. This is very different from a relational approach to prayer, where you humbly ask God and respect His freedom. The fathers contrast this with the myths of Prometheus and Sisyphus. Prometheus stole fire from the gods and gave it to humanity, and he was punished for it. Sisyphus used his cunning to deceive the gods, even chaining up Death itself, and his punishment was to roll a boulder uphill forever. These stories show the danger of trying to use spiritual knowledge as a tool to get what you want.

The same pattern appears in the Bible. The fathers look at the line of Cain in Genesis, specifically Tubal-cain, who is described as the forger of instruments of bronze and iron. This invention of metallurgy is associated with violence and rebellion. It’s a picture of technology arising in a family marked by separation from God. And this isn’t unique to the Bible. The fathers also mention the Apkallu, seven sages in Babylonian mythology who brought wisdom and technology to humanity. They were fish-men, and they were eventually imprisoned for their gift. So across cultures, the origin of technology is tied to a kind of forbidden knowledge.

The conversation then turns to how we use technology on people. When you apply techne to human beings, you treat them as objects to be manipulated. This leads to dehumanization and a mechanistic view of the cosmos. Everything becomes a machine, and all events are caused by prior events. There’s no room for free will or purpose. This is the heart of modern scientific materialism. The fathers even use Barbie as an example. She’s a piece of technology that creates an artificial image of womanhood, shaping how people see themselves and others.

They also argue that ideas don’t have agency. Racism, for example, doesn’t cause anything by itself. People use ideas as instruments to justify their actions. The real problem is the human heart. And when it comes to modern technology, especially the internet and social media, the fathers are quite critical. They say these tools have primarily served to isolate and alienate people. Earlier technologies often brought communities together, but these new ones pull us apart.

So what’s the answer? True freedom, they say, is not individuality. It’s the ability to serve God and flourish as human beings, and that can only happen in community. The Church is the primary place where this freedom can be realized. We are called to reject the ideologies that enslave us and to live together in love. The episode closes with a doxology from Revelation, chapter five, where the angels worship Christ, the one who is worthy. It’s a reminder that our ultimate purpose is not to master the world through our own cleverness, but to join in that heavenly worship, together, as one body.
