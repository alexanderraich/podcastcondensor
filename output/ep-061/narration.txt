In this episode of the Lord of Spirits, Fathers Andrew Stephen Damick and Stephen De Young take up the Orthodox Christian mystery of Holy Unction, the anointing of the sick. They begin with a deep look at what it means to be human, and they push back hard against the idea, which they trace back to Plato, that the soul is a separate, immaterial thing trapped inside a body. That view, they say, is not the biblical one. Instead, they explain that the soul is the life of the body. When God formed Adam from the dust and breathed into him the breath of life, Adam became a living soul. The Hebrew word is nefesh, and the Greek word is psyche, and both point to the same reality: the soul is what animates the body, not a ghostly orb floating inside it.

This leads them to a striking claim. Your body, they say, is more related to your identity than your soul is. Human souls are more alike than human bodies are. So the particular shape of who you are is tied up in your embodiment. That is why the Christian hope is not escape from the body, but the bodily resurrection. And that is also why healing matters so much. Illness and sin are portrayed in the Bible as parallel forces, and both need to be addressed.

The fathers then turn to the ancient world of pestilence. They talk about Resheph, an ancient Near Eastern deity of plague, often pictured as an archer. In the Old Testament, Resheph appears as a force under God’s control. In Deuteronomy thirty-two, God says He will spend His arrows on His people, and the text mentions Resheph and Ketev, another demonic force of pestilence. Psalm seventy-eight says God gave their flocks to Resheph, and Habakkuk describes Deber and Resheph following God as He goes forth. Even the first horseman of Revelation, who carries a bow, is connected to this same imagery of pestilence. So these forces are real, but they are not independent. God uses them for judgment, but He is not the author of evil.

The hosts are careful to correct some common misunderstandings. Sickness is not always a punishment for sin, and faith alone does not guarantee healing in this life. They stress that using medicine and doctors is fully compatible with prayer and the sacraments. Healing is holistic, meaning it addresses the whole person, body and soul together. And they point out that in the ministry of Christ, and in the Epistle of James, healing and the forgiveness of sins are closely linked. James chapter five tells us to anoint the sick with oil, and it connects that anointing with forgiveness.

Holy Unction, then, is a sacrament aimed at the healing of the whole person. It is not a magic charm, and it does not promise that we will never die. The ultimate healing is the bodily resurrection, and Unction prepares us for that. The fathers also touch on a heresy called Apollinarianism, which denied that Christ had a human soul, to show how important it is to understand the soul correctly. And they mention the passing of the biblical scholar Michael Heiser at the beginning of the episode, whose work on the supernatural in the Bible has been influential.

They close with a personal reflection on miscarriage and the hope of salvation. Even in the deepest grief, the Church’s teaching on the soul and the body gives us hope that all will be restored in the resurrection. The mystery of Holy Unction, then, is not just about getting better in this life. It is about being made whole for the life to come.
