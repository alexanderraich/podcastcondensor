In this episode, Fathers Andrew and Stephen take a long look at the history of Latin theology, and how it came to be so different from the Orthodox faith. They start with a bold claim: the very idea of "the West" is a false construct. It lumps together different cultures and time periods in a way that doesn't really hold up, and it’s not helpful for understanding how theology actually developed.

The real story, they argue, is about translation. Latin theology began when Greek ideas were translated into Latin. But over the centuries, the meaning of those words began to slip. The original Greek was lost to most people in the West, and so later generations were working with translations of translations. This gradual slippage in meaning, combined with the influence of Platonic metaphysics, led to a theology that became more and more transactional and mechanical.

They trace this back to the early Latin fathers. Tertullian, who coined the term "Trinitas," was an early voice, though he later fell into Montanism. Then there’s Saint Augustine, the Bishop of Hippo. His writings on original sin and grace were hugely influential, but Fathers Andrew and Stephen point out that both Roman Catholics and Protestants later misread him, projecting their own ideas onto his work.

The medieval period brought figures like Peter Lombard, whose "Sentences" became the standard textbook. He introduced a distinction between the sufficiency and the efficiency of Christ’s work. Then came Thomas Aquinas, who synthesized Christian theology with Aristotelian philosophy, and that synthesis became the norm in Roman Catholicism. But the hosts argue that this trajectory didn’t stop with the Reformation. Martin Luther and John Calvin didn’t break from Latin theology; they continued it. They kept its transactional view of salvation and its forensic understanding of guilt. Luther’s doctrine of justification by faith alone and imputed righteousness, and Calvin’s penal substitutionary atonement, are both built on that same Latin foundation.

The episode also touches on how the Greek terms for God’s essence and His energies, "ousia" and "energeia," were translated into Latin as "substantia" and "actus." But those Latin words carried different connotations, and that shift in meaning had huge consequences. The Orthodox distinction between God’s essence and His energies, His active presence in the world, was lost in the West.

The hosts also discuss how modern evangelicalism has become more of a political movement than a religious one, held together by political allegiances rather than shared theology. And they mention Charles Finney, the nineteenth-century revivalist who emphasized emotional conversion techniques, and Karl Rahner, the twentieth-century Jesuit whose transcendental Thomism influenced Vatican II.

Throughout the episode, they use scripture to illustrate their points. They discuss John chapter one, verse one, where the Greek term "Logos" is translated as "Word," and how later English usage can distort the original meaning. They also mention a caller’s question about the Greek word for "serpent" in Genesis chapter three, and a textual variant that reads "drakon" instead of "ophis." At the very end, they quote Revelation chapter five, verses eleven through thirteen, describing the worship of the Lamb by angels and elders.

The core message is that the modern crises in Western Christianity are not accidents. They are the logical outcomes of this long historical development. The Orthodox Church, they argue, has preserved the original Christian faith without those radical changes, and it stands as a stable alternative to a theology that has been slowly drifting for centuries.
