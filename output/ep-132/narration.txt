Father Andrew Stephen Damick and Father Stephen De Young open this episode by clearing away some common misunderstandings about the Old Testament sacrificial system. They want to talk about sin offerings, and they want to talk about what they actually meant. A lot of people assume that the animal was a substitute, that the sins of the person were placed on the animal, and that the animal died in the sinner’s place. The fathers say that none of these ideas are actually in the text. They are reading later theological ideas back into the Torah, and that is a mistake.

They start with Leviticus chapter four, which lays out the procedures for sin offerings. The chapter is very detailed. It describes what to do if the anointed priest sins, if the whole congregation sins, if a leader sins, or if a common person sins. In each case, there are specific animals and specific rituals. But nowhere in this chapter are sins placed on the animal. The blood is used to purify the sanctuary, and the fat is burned on the altar. The animal is not a stand-in for the sinner. The killing of the animal is not even the main point. In the biblical view, sacrifice is really about a meal of reconciliation. The priestly actions with the blood and the fat are the sacrificial act, and that act is a gift to God and a shared meal.

The only place in the Old Testament where sins are explicitly placed on an animal is in Leviticus chapter sixteen, the Day of Atonement. There are two goats on that day. One goat is for the Lord, and it is offered as a sin offering. The other goat is for Azazel. The people confess their sins over the goat for Azazel, and that goat is sent out into the wilderness. It is not sacrificed. It is not killed. It carries the sins away. So the one animal that carries sins is not a sacrifice at all. That should tell us something important.

Another common assumption is that the sacrificial system was substitutionary, that the animal died in place of the sinner. But the fathers point out that most sins did not carry the death penalty. For example, in Exodus chapter twenty-two, theft is punished by restitution, not by death. The sinner still has to make things right. The animal does not take the punishment. The system was never transactional or mechanical. It was always tied to repentance. The prophets make this very clear. In Isaiah chapter one, God rejects the sacrifices of unrepentant Israel. In Amos chapter five, God hates their feasts because they are not living justly. In Jeremiah chapter seven, God reminds them that He did not command sacrifices at the Exodus, He commanded obedience. Sacrifices are not a substitute for following God’s ways.

The fathers also address the word “unintentional” in Leviticus. It does not mean accidental. It means sins committed without defiant intent. This is contrasted with what the text calls high-handed sins, sins committed with an uplifted hand. Numbers chapter fifteen makes this distinction. Unintentional sins can be atoned for, but high-handed sins result in being cut off from the people. This is not because God is unwilling to forgive. It is because high-handed sin indicates unrepentance. The person is defiant. They bless themselves in their heart, as Deuteronomy twenty-nine describes. The sacrificial system cannot cover that kind of sin because the person is not seeking forgiveness.

This connects directly to the New Testament. The fathers argue that Christ’s death is not a penal substitution. He did not take the punishment we deserved. He did not suffer spiritual death, because that would mean He was separated from the Father, which would mean He was damned. That is not what happened. Christ experienced physical death, the separation of the soul from the body. But His physical death does not mean we do not have to die. Instead, His death and resurrection overcome both physical and spiritual death. Saint Gregory Palamas is quoted here, and he says that Christ’s physical death takes away both physical and spiritual death.

The Greek word used in the New Testament for Christ’s death is “hyper,” which means in behalf of or for the benefit of. It does not mean instead of. So Christ died for us, for our benefit, not in our place in a transactional sense. And repentance remains essential. The New Testament consistently calls for repentance. First John chapter one says that if we confess our sins, He is faithful and just to forgive us. Confession is a condition for forgiveness. Hebrews chapter ten warns that if we sin deliberately after receiving the knowledge of the truth, there no longer remains a sacrifice for sins. This is the same distinction we see in the Old Testament between repentant and unrepentant sin.

The fathers also talk about the story of Zacchaeus in Luke chapter nineteen. Zacchaeus repents by giving half his goods to the poor and restoring fourfold what he stole. Only after that does Jesus declare that salvation has come to his house. Repentance is not just feeling sorry. It involves concrete action, restitution, and a change of life. That is what makes forgiveness real.

They close with pastoral encouragement. True assurance of salvation does not come from a one-time decision. It comes from a continuous life of repentance. God desires all to be saved. No one who sincerely repents will be condemned. The fathers recommend a book called “Welcoming Gifts” by Father Jeremy Davis, which explores the true meaning of sacrifice. And they remind the listener that the sacrificial system was never about appeasing an angry God. It was always about purification, reconciliation, and the restoration of right relationships. And that is exactly what Christ accomplishes for us.
