In this episode of the Lord of Spirits, Fathers Andrew and Stephen take on the big topic of Doomsday—the final judgment and the end of all things. They start by looking at where the phrase "end of days" comes from. It’s interesting, because that exact phrase doesn’t actually appear in the Bible. But the idea is built into the very first chapter of Genesis, with its progression of days leading to a final day. The hosts make a strong point here: the notion that history has a beginning and an end is a biblical creation. The ancient world around Israel mostly thought of time as a cycle, endlessly repeating. The Bible, though, gives us a linear story with a purpose and a conclusion.

So what is judgment, really? They define the biblical word for justice, mishpat, as the state of everything being in its proper place and working the way it should. Judgment, then, isn’t just about punishment. It’s about restoring that order. To make this concrete, they turn to agricultural images from the Gospels. There’s the parable of the weeds, where the wheat and the tares grow together until the harvest, and then they’re separated. That’s the big, macrocosmic judgment at the end of the age. But there’s also the image of the vine and the branches, where the vinedresser prunes the branches so they’ll bear more fruit. That’s the microcosmic judgment—the ongoing correction and discipline in our lives now, meant to bring us to repentance and growth.

The conversation then moves to the Day of the Lord in the Old Testament. This isn’t just a future event. The prophets used it to describe historical judgments on nations like Babylon and Egypt. When Isaiah describes the Day of the Lord coming upon Babylon, he uses apocalyptic imagery, but it’s about a real, historical fall. These events are participations in the final judgment, little previews of the ultimate setting right of all things.

Then they get to the heart of the New Testament teaching: everyone will be judged according to their works. They cite passage after passage—Matthew, Romans, Second Corinthians, First Peter, Colossians, Revelation. And they address the common objection that this contradicts salvation by faith. The hosts are clear that salvation is a gift of God’s grace. But the judgment is based on the works that flow from faith. And here’s the key: those works aren’t really our own. They’re God’s works performed through us. This is what they call theurgy, literally "God’s works." So it’s not about earning salvation. It’s about participating in what God is doing.

They connect this to the principle of iconography. When you honor an image, the honor passes to the prototype. So when you feed the hungry or visit the prisoner, you’re doing it to Christ himself, because every person is made in the image of God. This principle, they argue, is foundational to Christian ethics. And they warn that rejecting it—iconoclasm—undermines not just our treatment of others, but the very doctrine of the Trinity.

Finally, they explore why the bodily resurrection is so necessary. Justice can’t be fully realized if we’re just disembodied souls. We have to be raised in our bodies to receive vindication or condemnation for what we did in the body. This belief goes back to Job, who declared that in his flesh he would see God. It’s in Isaiah, where the dead shall live and their bodies shall rise. And it’s in Daniel, where many who sleep in the dust awake, some to everlasting life and some to shame. The resurrection of Christ is his vindication as the righteous one, and it’s the basis for our justification. The hosts conclude with a simple exhortation: live faithfully, do the works of God, so that at the last judgment you might hear, "Well done, good and faithful servant."
