In this episode, Father Andrew and Father Stephen take on the subject of prophecy and prophets. They start by placing prophecy within the wider ancient Near Eastern practice of divination, which included things like astrology and reading animal entrails. But they make a clear distinction. Prophecy is a form of divination, yet it is completely different in kind. It is not about interpreting signs or techniques. It is a direct encounter with the divine. The prophet does not read the stars or inspect a liver. The prophet stands in the presence of God and receives a message.

The hosts look at ancient texts like the Mari documents and Neo-Assyrian records to show that prophets were often outsiders. They were not part of the established power structure. Their authority came directly from the deity, which allowed them to challenge kings and priests. This pattern carries into the Old Testament. They discuss the Hebrew terms for prophet, like navi, which means one who calls out, and the seer, which emphasizes the visionary aspect of receiving revelation. They also bring up the Akkadian term mahhu, which is related to going insane or entering a frenzy, reflecting the ecstatic nature of the experience.

The conversation moves through key figures. Abraham is called a prophet. Moses is the great prophet. And then there is Balaam, a fascinating case. He was a non-Israelite hired to curse Israel, but instead he blessed them and delivered a messianic prophecy about a star coming out of Jacob, which they connect to Christ. Later, Balaam becomes an archetype of false teachers. The hosts also examine Jeremiah, who emphasized that true prophecy requires standing in the counsel of the Lord. Jeremiah contrasts this with false prophets who run without being sent. They speak from their own imagination, not from the divine council.

The hosts are clear that prophecy is not primarily about predicting the future. It is about delivering a message from God, often calling people to repentance. True prophets must stand in that council and receive a direct word. False prophets are often self-deluded. They believe they speak for God when they have not received a genuine revelation. The primary role of a prophet is to call the people of God back to faithfulness, not to tear down institutions.

The discussion then moves to the New Testament. Prophecy did not cease. It continues. They point to Agabus, who predicted a famine and later performed a symbolic act by binding Paul’s belt to prophesy his imprisonment. This is in the style of the Old Testament prophets. They also interpret Paul’s conversion on the road to Damascus as a prophetic call. Paul himself describes this encounter in Galatians, echoing Jeremiah’s language. His meeting with Christ was a prophetic commissioning. The gift of prophecy is given by the Spirit for building up the Church. Paul distinguishes it from speaking in tongues, which he says is directed toward unbelievers and requires interpretation.

Father Andrew and Father Stephen also connect prophecy to monasticism. The monastic tradition is a continuation of the prophetic communities. Monks often serve as prophets who call the Church to repentance. They warn about the dangers of false prophets in the Church today. They also reference the story of King Saul, who experienced prophetic frenzy when he encountered the Spirit of God. This illustrates how disruptive such encounters can be, especially for the unrepentant.

The episode closes with practical advice. The hosts encourage seeking prophecy through repentance and faithfulness, not by chasing special revelations. They reference Luke’s Gospel, where Abraham says that if people do not listen to Moses and the prophets, they will not be convinced even if someone rises from the dead. This emphasizes the sufficiency of Scripture. The true prophetic call is to return to God, to listen to His word, and to live in faithfulness. That is the heart of the prophetic message.
