In this Thanksgiving episode of the Lord of Spirits, Fathers Andrew Stephen Damick and Stephen De Young continue their series on the Holy Mysteries of the Orthodox Church by taking up the Holy Eucharist. They start with a direct question: is the Eucharist just a symbol? They firmly reject that idea, and they dig into the biblical and historical roots of the Eucharist as a true sacrifice.

The conversation centers on the Christian priesthood. This is not a continuation of the old Levitical priesthood. Instead, it is a participation in the priesthood of Christ Himself. Christ is the one true priest, and human priests share in His priesthood. His sacrifice is His own voluntary self-offering to the Father. The fathers connect this to the Old Testament sacrificial system, especially the sin offering, the peace offering, and the thank offering. The Eucharist gathers up all of these sacrifices into one. They point to Psalm one hundred and ten, verse four, which establishes Christ as a priest forever according to the order of Melchizedek. Melchizedek was a king and priest of Jerusalem, and his priesthood was not hereditary. Christ’s priesthood follows that same pattern.

They also bring in the Maccabean martyrs, who offered themselves as sacrifices for their people. These martyrs foreshadow Christ’s own voluntary self-offering. The fathers are careful to say that the Eucharist is not a repetition of Christ’s crucifixion. It is a participation in that one, once-for-all event, which is both historical and eternal. They turn to Saint Paul’s first letter to the Corinthians, chapter ten, where he explicitly links the Eucharist to sacrifice. He compares it to the sacrifices of Israel and even to pagan sacrifices offered to demons. The fathers also use Numbers chapter ten, verse ten, to show that the Greek word for memorial is used for sacrifices. So a memorial can indeed be a sacrifice.

Then they address the relationship between the Eucharist and the Jewish Passover. They make it clear that the Last Supper was not a Seder. The Seder is a ritual meal that developed later, after the destruction of the Temple. The Last Supper was a pilgrimage meal, the kind described in Deuteronomy chapter fourteen. The Eucharist is the new Passover, and Christ is the Passover lamb. The use of leavened bread in the Orthodox Church is significant because it points to the resurrection. Pascha, the Greek word for Passover, is what Orthodox Christians call Easter.

The second part of the episode takes up listener voicemails. One question is about infant communion. The fathers explain that the Orthodox practice of giving communion to infants is rooted in the Passover, where the whole family participated. Infants can love God with their heart and soul, even without intellectual understanding. The Eucharist is nourishment for everyone, regardless of age. Another voicemail asks about the agape meal. This was a sacrificial meal that accompanied the Eucharist in the early Church. It was open to all, while the Eucharist itself was restricted to the faithful. This distinction highlights the sacrificial nature of the Eucharist.

The third part of the episode focuses on the body and blood of Christ. The fathers discuss the Old Testament prohibition on drinking blood. This was a limit on predation, a reminder to humanity not to become predatory. In the Eucharist, we drink Christ’s blood, but it is not an act of predation. It is a voluntary self-offering that purifies us from sin. The blood of Christ cleanses and transforms us. It does not appease wrath. They connect this to the piercing of Christ’s side in John chapter nineteen. Blood and water flow out, and they connect this to the creation of Eve from Adam’s side in Genesis chapter two. From Christ’s side, the Church is born.

Christ is truly and really present in the Eucharist, regardless of the disposition of the recipient or the priest. This is why receiving unworthily can be harmful. The Orthodox Church does not define the how of this presence. They avoid the Western debates about transubstantiation and consubstantiation. Instead, they focus on what the Eucharist does. It is a meal, and it is a sacrifice. In the ancient world, sacrifices were meals. The Eucharist is a sacrificial meal that brings us into communion with God and with one another.

The word Eucharist means thanksgiving. The sacrament is a thank offering to God. It includes elements of the Old Testament grain and drink offerings. The episode closes with a practical exhortation. We are called to live a Eucharistic life, offering our whole lives to God. The Eucharist is not just something we receive. It is something we become.
