In this episode of the Lord of Spirits, Fathers Andrew Stephen Damick and Stephen De Young begin a new series on the last things by tackling the figure of the Antichrist. They start by clearing away a lot of popular modern ideas. The common picture of a single, future political villain is not the whole story. In fact, the idea of an anti-Messiah is much older than Christianity. It comes straight out of Second Temple Judaism, where the coming of the Messiah was already tied to the appearance of a great adversary.

The heart of the discussion is Saint Paul’s second letter to the Thessalonians, chapter two. There, Paul speaks of the man of lawlessness and the mystery of lawlessness that is already at work. The hosts point out that the Greek word for lawlessness, anomia, means a rejection of God’s way of life, the Torah. So this figure is not just someone who breaks rules. He is the ultimate embodiment of rebellion against God’s order. And he is not merely one individual. He is an archetype, a negative type of Christ. Just as Adam and Christ are archetypal men in Romans chapter five, the man of lawlessness stands as the archetype of evil, affecting all of humanity.

The hosts also dig into the word apostasia, which is often translated as rebellion or falling away. In the Greek, it has military connotations. It means a defection, a retreat from a post. This rebellion comes first, before the man of lawlessness is revealed. And then there is the famous restrainer, the katechon. This is the thing or person holding back the mystery of lawlessness. The Fathers argue that this restrainer is not a political power or a secret society. It is the liturgical worship of the Church, especially the Eucharist. The liturgy is like a garrison holding the line against evil. When Christian worship ceases, the end will come. The world is sustained by the worship of the Church, and the end of the world will coincide with the end of the liturgy.

They connect this to the writings of Saint John. In his first letter, John says that many antichrists have already come. This means the spirit of antichrist is not a future surprise. It is already active in the world. And in the book of Revelation, the beast from the sea is a composite of the beasts from Daniel’s vision in Daniel chapter seven. These beasts represent world empires, and the beast of Revelation is a blasphemous ruler who embodies a whole world system. The number of the beast, six hundred sixty-six, is decoded using gematria, the practice of assigning numerical values to letters. It points to Nero Caesar. Nero was an early archetype of the Antichrist because of his persecution of Christians and the legend that he would return. The hosts also mention Julian the Apostate, the emperor who tried to bring back paganism after Constantine. He became a model for the Antichrist in the writings of the Church Fathers.

The episode also touches on the figure of Belial from Second Temple Jewish literature. Belial is a demonic figure associated with lawlessness, and the man of lawlessness is linked to that tradition. There is even a connection to the Nephilim, the giant offspring of the sons of God and human women, as an archetype of demonic human rulers.

The hosts are clear that many historical figures, like Hitler and Stalin, were indeed antichrists in the sense that they embodied the spirit of antichrist. But they were not the final one. The final Antichrist will be a usurper. The prefix anti in Greek primarily means instead of, not against. So the Antichrist is someone who seeks to replace Christ, to stand in His place. That is why he is a negative type of Christ. He mimics and distorts everything Christ is.

The episode ends with practical advice. Christians should not live in fear or extremism. Instead, they should remain faithful, engage the culture, and stand firm in worship and love. The liturgy is the great defense against the mystery of lawlessness. As long as the Church is worshipping, the world is being held together. So the call is simple. Stay in the liturgy, stay in love, and do not be shaken.
