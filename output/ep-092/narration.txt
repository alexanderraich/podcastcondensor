In this episode, Father Andrew and Father Stephen take up the Book of Jubilees, a Second Temple Jewish text that retells the stories of Genesis and the early part of Exodus. It is a book that many people have heard of, but few have actually read. The hosts make the case that this is a text worth knowing, not just as an ancient curiosity, but as a window into how Jewish and early Christian communities understood their own scriptures.

The Book of Jubilees is often classified as apocryphal, but it holds a unique place among those writings. Unlike most other Second Temple Jewish texts, which were preserved only by Christians, Jubilees was used by both Jewish and Christian communities. The complete text survives in Ge’ez, the liturgical language of the Ethiopian Orthodox Church, which shows how deeply it was valued in certain Christian traditions.

One of the central themes of the episode is the book’s apocalyptic character. Jubilees is not simply a retelling of Genesis. It is framed as a revelation given to Moses on Mount Sinai. Moses receives a vision of history from God himself, a kind of heaven’s eye view of the world. That makes it apocalyptic literature in the truest sense, because it reveals heavenly realities and gives the listener a perspective that goes beyond ordinary human history.

The hosts spend a good deal of time on the Enochic calendar, which is a 364-day solar calendar with four seasons of three months each, plus four intercalary days. This calendar was considered mathematically perfect, and it was central to certain Jewish communities, including the Essenes at Qumran. The Book of Jubilees insists that this calendar is built into the very structure of creation. The commandments of the Torah are not arbitrary rules. They are woven into the fabric of the world itself. That is a key claim of the book, and the hosts emphasize it strongly.

Another important tradition found in Jubilees is the idea that the Law was given through angels. This is not just an obscure detail. It shows up in the New Testament. In Acts chapter seven, Stephen refers to the law given by angels. Paul makes a similar point in Galatians chapter three, and the author of Hebrews mentions it as well. The hosts point out that this tradition, which seems strange to modern readers, was common in the world of the early church.

The episode also explores some of the specific stories in Jubilees. There is the creation of the angels, which is described in more detail than in Genesis. There is the figure of Mastema, a devil who leads evil spirits and makes a deal with God to afflict the wicked. Mastema is associated with the giants and with the angel of death. He is a troubling figure, but he plays an important role in the book’s understanding of evil and divine justice.

Noah also appears in a new light. In Jubilees, he offers sacrifices, prays against demons, and begins the celebration of Pentecost. He is depicted as a preacher of righteousness, a man who stands against the corruption of his age. Abraham’s story is expanded as well. We see his rejection of idolatry, his encounter with Mastema, and his blessing of Jacob. The Angel of the Presence, a divine figure who speaks for God throughout the book, is identified by the hosts with the Second Person of the Trinity. This angel often uses the word “we” when describing God’s actions, which points to a Trinitarian understanding of the divine nature.

The hosts also draw attention to the way Jubilees influences the New Testament in subtle ways. For example, Paul’s reference to four hundred and thirty years in Galatians matches the timeline in Jubilees, not the Masoretic text of Exodus. Peter’s language about new heavens and a new earth echoes Jubilees. And the phrase “kingdom and priests” in Revelation matches Jubilees exactly, with the word “and” instead of “of.” These connections show that the early Christians were reading and using Jubilees as part of their scriptural tradition.

Finally, the episode reflects on the meaning of ritual participation. The liturgical cycles and feasts described in Jubilees are not just commemorations of past events. They allow believers to enter into eternal realities. The calendar shapes a person’s life and community. The hosts compare this to the way modern consumer calendars shape our own lives, for better or worse. The Book of Jubilees reminds us that time itself is holy, and that the feasts of the Church are a participation in the life of heaven.
