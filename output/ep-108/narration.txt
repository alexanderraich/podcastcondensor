In this episode of the Lord of Spirits, Fathers Andrew Stephen Damick and Stephen DeYoung take up the topic of ritual purity and uncleanness in the Old Testament, and how those ancient laws continue to matter in Orthodox Christian life today. They start with a crucial distinction. There is moral uncleanness, which comes from sin like murder, sexual immorality, or idolatry. That kind of uncleanness makes a person unfit to approach God and requires repentance. But there is also ceremonial, or ritual, uncleanness. This comes from unavoidable bodily functions, touching unclean things, or coming into contact with death. And it is not sinful at all.

The fathers stress that the clean and unclean distinction is not about good versus evil. It is about holy versus common. Things set apart for God are holy, and ordinary things are common. Ritual uncleanness is simply a normal part of life in a fallen world. It requires purification before approaching God, but it is not a moral failing. They point to Leviticus chapter ten, verse ten, where God tells the priests to distinguish between holy and common, and between clean and unclean. That parallel shows that the whole system is about set-apartness, not morality.

They also clarify what the sin offering was for. After a period of uncleanness, a person would offer a sacrifice when reentering the community. But that offering was for sins committed during the time of separation, not for the uncleanness itself. The uncleanness was not sinful. Approaching God while unclean, however, was a sin. Leviticus chapter seven makes that clear, saying that anyone who touches the flesh of the sacrifice while unclean will be cut off from the people.

The conversation then moves to how these laws persist in the New Testament and in the Church. The fathers mention the practice of churching mothers and infants after childbirth, which is rooted in Leviticus chapter twelve and the presentation of Christ in the temple. They also discuss the preparation of clergy and the ongoing need for purification before receiving the Eucharist. They warn against two common errors. One is dismissing these practices as obsolete. The other is over-spiritualizing every aspect of life, turning everything into a metaphor and losing the concrete reality of holy and common.

They bring up the example of Cain. In Second Temple Judaism, Cain was the archetypal morally unclean person. His murder of Abel and the curse that followed made him permanently unclean, alienated from creation. The historian Josephus and the philosopher Philo of Alexandria both wrote about the permanent ritual impurity of murderers, saying they could not perform sacred functions. Even King David, though his sin was forgiven and he repented deeply, was not allowed to build the temple because he had shed blood. His impurity was permanent in this life, even though his soul was saved.

This leads to a deeper point. Certain extreme sins, like murder, can result in permanent ritual impurity in this life, barring a person from sacred functions, even after repentance and forgiveness. The Greek term for this state is agos, meaning accursed. It is a permanent condition, not a temporary one.

The fathers also emphasize that baptism uniquely washes away moral uncleanness. Unlike the ritual washings of the Old Testament, which only addressed ceremonial impurity, baptism cleanses the soul from sin. That is why it is done only once. The distinction between holy and common remains in the New Covenant. Not everything is holy, and common things are not inherently bad. They cite Second Corinthians chapter six, where Paul calls Christians to separate from unclean things, showing that the distinction persists.

They also touch on the Theotokos, the Virgin Mary. Her giving birth without corruption is celebrated in the feast of the Purification, and her example is followed in the churching of mothers. The Western name for that feast is Candlemas. The fathers note that the birth of Christ did not make the Virgin unclean, but the Church still follows the pattern of the law in bringing mother and child for prayers of thanksgiving and reentry.

The episode closes with a reflection on the purpose of all this purification. It is not about shame or rejection. It is about enabling intimacy with God. The whole system of clean and unclean, holy and common, exists so that people can draw near to the Lord. The fathers encourage balancing the holy and the common in Christian life, neither treating everything as sacred nor treating nothing as sacred. The goal is always communion with God, and purification is the path that leads there.
