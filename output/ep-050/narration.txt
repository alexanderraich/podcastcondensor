This is the fiftieth episode of the Lord of Spirits podcast, and it is a special one. Fathers Andrew Stephen Damick and Stephen DeYoung are celebrating their second anniversary with a live question and answer session, taking calls from listeners on all sorts of topics. The whole thing kicks off with a wonderfully humorous question about catching leprechauns, which quickly leads them into a discussion of fairy folklore and how these old stories connect to deeper spiritual realities.

From there, the questions get more serious. One caller asks whether angels or saints could ever fall again. Father Andrew and Father Stephen explain that once a being is perfected in Christ, they are preserved by grace and cannot fall. They bring in St. John of Damascus to support this, and they clarify that Adam was in a state of innocence, not perfection. He was mutable, which is why he could fall in the first place. This leads to a discussion of the natural will, which impels us toward the good, and the gnomic will, which is that second movement of the will that emerges after the fall, characterized by indecision and the need to choose between alternatives.

Another caller brings up the branch theory of the church, the idea that different denominations are just different branches of the same tree. The fathers reject this outright. They argue that schism is always a tragedy that needs to be healed, not something to accept as normal. Doctrine matters, and it matters because it affects what we do. They bring in Father George Florovsky’s distinction between dogma, doctrine, and theology. Dogma is the non-negotiable core, doctrine is the teaching of that dogma, and theology is the ongoing reflection and application. They point to the book of Acts, chapter fifteen, where the Apostolic Council’s decrees are called dogmas, showing that dogma includes practical instructions, not just abstract beliefs.

The conversation then turns to identity and relics. The fathers argue that identity is not just about essence, but about relationships and activities. They use the example of a relic of the True Cross. Even if a relic were phony, it could still function as the True Cross for those who venerate it in faith, because the relationship and the action matter. They also field a question about the title Son of Man, referencing Matthew chapter sixteen and its Second Temple Jewish background. And they discuss the Most High God, noting that while many religions use that title, it does not mean they are all worshipping the same being. The title belongs to the true God alone.

The relationship between idolatry and sexual immorality comes up as well. They cite St. Dionysius the Areopagite to emphasize that the feedback loop of idolatry starts with human instigation, not with God assigning fallen angels to deceive us. They also touch on the canon of Scripture, church architecture, and the nature of evangelism. On that last point, they stress that evangelism must be contextual. It is not about just being right, but about focusing on who Jesus is, what He accomplished, and what He expects. They bring up St. Herman of Alaska as an example of someone who evangelized by living among and serving a community.

The fathers also address the issue of abortion, arguing that to truly address it, we need systemic cultural changes that value children and families, not just legislation or charity. They advocate for a beauty-first approach, changing hearts and culture through beauty and truth, not just protest. They reference a book on the ethics of beauty by Timothy Patrias, and they mention C.S. Lewis and the idea of the noble pagan, as well as J.R.R. Tolkien’s thoughts on the canons of narrative art. They even touch on Zachariah Sitchin and ancient astronaut theories in relation to UFOs and mythology.

Throughout the episode, the fathers emphasize the dangers of materialism and relativism. They quote St. Nicholas Cabasilas, who said that miracles are simply gifts from God at which men marvel. They remind listeners that we are judged by our works, not by our correct opinions. Doctrine emerges from practice and serves to protect and define it, not the other way around. The episode closes with a reading from Revelation chapter five, about the voice of many angels around the throne, a fitting end to a conversation that constantly points back to the spiritual realities that surround us.
