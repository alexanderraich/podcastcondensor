This is the fifth anniversary episode of the Lord of Spirits, and Fathers Andrew and Stephen are marking the occasion by taking a deep dive into the ancient flood stories. They survey the great flood narratives from Sumer, Akkad, Babylon, Greece, and even Norse mythology, and they lay them side by side with the biblical account in Genesis. You hear about the Sumerian hero Ziusudra, a priest-king who survives the waters. You hear about Atrahasis, the wise man warned by the god Ea to build a boat. And of course, there is Gilgamesh, who seeks immortality and hears the flood story from Utnapishtim. In these pagan tales, the gods are capricious. They send the flood out of annoyance or petty anger, and they seem almost surprised by the destruction they have caused. The heroes survive with their animals, but the divine will behind the catastrophe is arbitrary and cruel.

The hosts then turn to a different kind of flood story, the tale of Atlantis. It begins with Plato, who wrote about a lost island civilization in his dialogues, claiming it was based on Egyptian records. But the story did not stay with Plato. In the nineteenth century, an American congressman named Ignatius Donnelly wrote a book popularizing Atlantis as a real, ancient civilization and the source of all human culture. From there, the idea was picked up by the occultist Madame Blavatsky, who wove Atlantis into her Theosophical cosmology and linked it to the Aryan race and psychic powers. Later, the psychic Edgar Cayce claimed to have visions of Atlantis and even predicted its rise in the 1960s. The hosts trace how these esoteric ideas eventually fed into the Thule Society and Nazi ideology. It is a strange and dark path, and they make clear that this is not innocent mythmaking but something that led to real horror.

Against all of this, they set the Genesis flood. And here is the key point. The biblical story is not a plagiarism of the Mesopotamian myths. It is a deliberate theological reworking. In the pagan stories, the gods want to destroy humanity because humanity is noisy or troublesome. But in Genesis, God is grieved by human wickedness, and even in judgment, He is working toward salvation. The God of the Bible loves humanity and desires its flourishing. That is a radical contrast to the gods of the ancient world.

Fathers Andrew and Stephen also offer a particular way of reading the flood itself. They argue that the flood is best understood as an uncreation and a recreation. In Genesis, God created the world by separating the waters on the second and third days. The flood reverses that. The waters come crashing back together, and the world returns to its original chaotic state. Then, after the waters recede, God recreates. Noah emerges as a new father of humanity, a type of Christ, the righteous one through whom God saves the world, pointing forward to Jesus, the true second Adam.

They also take time to critique the documentary hypothesis, the old scholarly theory that the Pentateuch was stitched together from multiple sources over centuries. They explain that this theory is built on circular reasoning. It assumes that Israelite religion evolved from polytheism, and then it reads that assumption back into the text. For an Orthodox Christian, they say, this approach is simply not useful.

And finally, they address the modern debates about whether the flood was global or local. They say these debates miss the point entirely. The scripture is not a scientific textbook. It is a theological and moral revelation. Father Stephen emphasizes that biblical faith is not mere mental assent. It is faithfulness, demonstrated in how you live your life. Debates over historical details are irrelevant to Christian living. Jesus Himself referenced the days of Noah in Matthew twenty-four, warning us to be ready for His return. The lesson is not about geology. It is about being prepared, about living faithfully, so that when the water comes, you are found in the ark.
