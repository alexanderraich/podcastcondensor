In this episode, Father Andrew and Father Stephen take up the Shepherd of Hermas, an early Christian text from the second century. They begin by placing it in history, noting it was written in the 140s AD by a man named Hermas, who was the brother of Pope Pius the First, the bishop of Rome. This dating comes from the Muratorian Canon, an early list of scriptural books from around the year 170. That same list makes clear that the Shepherd was never considered part of the New Testament, yet it was widely read and deeply influential in the early church.

The hosts spend time correcting a common modern misunderstanding about this book. Many scholars assume that early Christian thought was simple and only later became complex, a view they call the developmental hypothesis. Under that assumption, the Shepherd of Hermas is often read as teaching that Jesus was merely a man adopted by God. Father Andrew and Father Stephen argue this is a misreading. They point to the work of a contemporary Orthodox scholar, Father Bogdan, who has shown that the text actually supports a more orthodox understanding. In particular, the fifth parable, which speaks of a son and a vineyard, is not about Jesus at all. The son in that parable represents Christians who are adopted as sons of God through the Holy Spirit, which fits the Orthodox theme of theosis, the process of becoming like God.

The episode also addresses the question of authority. In Orthodox Christianity, Scripture holds primary authority, but it is not the only authority. The Shepherd of Hermas, along with the writings of the church fathers, holds a secondary but real authority for guiding interpretation and practice. The hosts point out that this is very different from the Protestant idea of Sola Scriptura, which they argue does not match how the early church actually operated. The Shepherd was recommended for catechesis by Saint Athanasius in the fourth century, cited frequently by Saint John Cassian in the fifth, and included in a list of books for private reading by Saint Nicholas the Confessor in the ninth. Even the great fourth-century manuscript known as Codex Sinaiticus includes the Shepherd at its end, though that does not mean it was considered canonical.

A major theme in the Shepherd is the seriousness of sin after baptism. The text teaches that there is one opportunity for repentance during a person’s lifetime, not that forgiveness is impossible. This is a call to take Christian living seriously, not a denial of God’s mercy. Another powerful image is the church as a tower being built from stones, with each stone representing a believer and Christ as the foundation. This picture shows the church as both collective and dynamic, always being built up by the faithful.

The hosts also defend the reality of visions and prophecy after the time of the apostles. They reject cessationism, the idea that such gifts stopped with the apostles, and instead hold to continuationism, the belief that these gifts continue throughout church history. They point to the prophet Agabus in the book of Acts, who predicted a famine, and to the words of the prophet Joel, quoted by Saint Peter at Pentecost, promising that visions and dreams would come in the new covenant era. This supports the legitimacy of Hermas receiving visions.

There is also a brief discussion of the mention of a Hermas in Saint Paul’s letter to the Romans, in the sixteenth chapter. Some early writers, like Origen, thought this was the same person, though the hosts do not insist on it. They also touch on the first parable of the Shepherd, which echoes Saint Peter’s words that Christians are strangers and exiles in this world.

The episode closes with a reflection on how to approach such texts. The hosts encourage humility and openness, reading the Shepherd of Hermas not as a forgotten relic but as a living witness to the faith of the early church. It is a book that challenges modern assumptions and rewards careful attention, reminding listeners that the Holy Spirit was at work in the church long before the canon was settled.
