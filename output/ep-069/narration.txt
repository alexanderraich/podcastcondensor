Father Andrew and Father Stephen open this episode by tackling one of the most misunderstood passages in all of Scripture: the thousand years in Revelation chapter twenty. They start with a simple but crucial idea—what the Bible means by an “age.” The Greek word is *aeon*, and it’s not just a measure of time. An age is a whole world order, a distinct state of things. Scripture speaks of many ages, from creation onward, and each one has its own character and its own turning point.

The turning points between ages are what the prophets call the Day of the Lord. That’s the moment when God steps into history to judge and to deliver, to close one era and open another. The hosts trace this through the Old Testament, from the exile to the return, and then they land on the big one: the coming of the Messiah. When Christ came, He didn’t just start a new chapter in the same old story. He inaugurated the Messianic Age. And here’s the key claim—that age is not something we’re still waiting for. It’s now. It’s the present period of the Church.

Father Andrew and Father Stephen are careful to explain what this means. Christ reigns from heaven, right now, in the midst of His enemies. They point to Psalm one hundred ten, where the Messiah rules until His foes are made His footstool. That’s not a future earthly utopia. That’s the current reality of the kingdom. The binding of Satan in Revelation twenty is not a future event. It’s what happened at the cross and the resurrection. The first resurrection, they argue, is not a bodily rising before a literal millennium. It’s the spiritual resurrection of the souls of the righteous, who reign with Christ in this age. The martyrs are already with Him.

This leads them to critique two ancient and modern errors. First, there’s chiliasm—the old belief in a future thousand-year earthly reign of Christ, often pictured with material abundance. The Church condemned this as an eschatological error, though the hosts are quick to note it’s not a heresy about Christ or the Trinity. Second, there’s dispensationalism, which inserts a separate “church age” between the tribulation and the millennium. The New Testament, they insist, knows nothing of that. The present age is the Messianic Age. There’s no gap.

They also take on a common claim about St. Irenaeus. Many people say he was a chiliast. Father Andrew and Father Stephen push back. The passages often cited from his writings actually affirm the bodily resurrection and the present reality of the kingdom. They don’t describe a future earthly reign. Irenaeus was not a chiliast. The association of chiliasm with heretical movements like Montanism helped get it condemned, but that doesn’t mean every early father believed it.

The hosts also connect this to the New Covenant. Jeremiah thirty-one promised a time when the law would be written on hearts, not on stone. That’s not the abolition of the Torah. It’s its internalization. The Holy Spirit empowers believers to fulfill the righteous requirements of the law. That’s what the Messianic Age looks like. Joel’s prophecy about the Spirit poured out on all flesh was fulfilled at Pentecost, and that’s the Day of the Lord. Isaiah’s peaceable kingdom, where the wolf dwells with the lamb, is not a future zoo. It’s a picture of creation being reordered in this age.

Father Andrew and Father Stephen draw on St. Augustine, who developed the idea of the *seculum*—the worldly age—in contrast to the City of God. And they mention Joachim of Fiore, the twelfth-century mystic who dreamed of a future age of the Spirit. His ideas fed later millenarian movements, but they’re a distortion of the biblical witness.

The episode closes with an exhortation. Because the Messianic Age is now, we are called to live joyfully and hopefully in it. We participate in Christ’s victory over the powers of evil. Satan is bound. The saints reign with Christ. The final judgment is coming, but we don’t wait for a golden age on earth. We live in the age that has already begun.
