In this episode, Father Andrew and Father Stephen kick off a brand-new series on the Apocrypha, and they’re starting with a deep dive into the Testaments of the Twelve Patriarchs. Right off the bat, they clear up a common misunderstanding about the word “apocrypha.” It doesn’t mean heretical or forbidden. It simply refers to books that were read privately, not aloud in public worship. So it’s a middle category—between the scriptures you hear in church and the writings that are considered dangerous.

The Testaments themselves are a collection of Jewish texts from the Second Temple period, written before Christ. Each one presents the last words of one of the twelve sons of Jacob, and the whole genre is modeled on Jacob’s blessing of his sons in Genesis forty-nine. These are prophetic farewell speeches, and they’re packed with moral instruction and visions of the future.

Father Andrew and Father Stephen walk through each of the twelve testaments, and certain themes keep coming up. The Testament of Reuben, for example, is all about his sin with Bilhah, which is mentioned in Genesis thirty-five. Reuben repents and warns his descendants against lust. He also lays out what he calls the seven parts of the human spirit—soul, sight, hearing, smell, taste, speech, and sex. And the point is that these are all good things, but they have to be directed properly. If they’re not, they can lead you into ruin.

Levi’s testament is different. He’s presented as the archetypal priest, and his vision is apocalyptic. This is where the idea of two Messiahs really comes into focus. The Testaments talk about a priestly Messiah from Levi and a kingly Messiah from Judah, and the priestly one is actually elevated above the kingly one. That’s a distinctly Jewish expectation, not a Christian invention.

Judah’s testament, on the other hand, portrays him as a warrior king, but it also warns against drunkenness and lust. And Joseph’s testament emphasizes chastity and forgiveness, and it includes a dream vision of the Messiah as the Lamb of God. That image—the Lamb of God—is something people often think of as purely Christian, but here it is in a pre-Christian Jewish text.

The hosts make a strong point about this. They argue that the Testaments are genuinely pre-Christian, and the messianic prophecies in them are not later Christian interpolations. These were existing Jewish expectations. And that leads to a bigger claim: Christianity is not a new religion. It’s the continuation of the religion of ancient Israel, with the coming of the Messiah inaugurating the Messianic age. So many ideas that people assume are Christian innovations—like the virgin birth from Isaiah seven fourteen, or the fullness of the Gentiles from Romans eleven—actually have Jewish precedents. The Testament of Naphtali, for instance, envisions the northern tribes being taken up into heaven, which St. Paul later connects to the idea that Gentiles will find salvation through Israel.

The hosts also take some listener calls. They talk about sabbatical years and about the Israelites plundering the Egyptians, and they tie those discussions back to the broader theme of the episode. And they close with a practical application. You can’t just pick and choose the parts of the Christian tradition you like. You have to submit to the whole thing, even the parts you find difficult or unpleasant. That’s how you get transformed.

They also mention the Book of Enoch, which is quoted in the Testaments and was widely believed in that period to be genuinely written by Enoch. And they touch on the Nephilim, the giants from Genesis, who show up in Reuben’s testament in connection with lust and power. And Belial, the name for the devil in Second Temple literature, appears throughout as the force behind evil.

So the big takeaway is this: the Testaments of the Twelve Patriarchs are a window into the world of Second Temple Judaism, and they show just how much of what we think of as Christian was already there, waiting. The Church didn’t burn these books. It just set them aside for private reading. And if you read them, you’ll see that the faith of the apostles was deeply rooted in the faith of the patriarchs.
