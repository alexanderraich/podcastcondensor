Father Andrew and Father Stephen open this episode with a question that has haunted Christian theology for centuries: what does it actually mean to be chosen? They argue that the way we’ve come to understand election, especially in the Calvinist tradition, has twisted the biblical text into something it never intended to say. The key, they say, is grammar. The Hebrew word *bachir* and the Greek word *eklektos* are adjectives, not participles. They describe a quality or a role, not the result of an action done to a person. So when Scripture calls someone chosen, it isn’t saying they were picked out of a crowd. It’s saying they hold a particular office or purpose.

This distinction matters because it changes how we read the whole story of Israel. The fathers make a bold claim: Israel was not chosen from among the nations. Israel was created. If you look at the table of nations in Genesis chapter ten, you find seventy nations listed there, and Israel is not one of them. Israel is something new, something God formed through Abraham and through the Exodus. So the idea that God looked at a lineup of nations and arbitrarily picked one is simply not what the text says. Israel was brought into being for a purpose, to be a kingdom of priests to the rest of the world.

The fathers then turn to a phrase that often trips people up: the elect angels. Paul mentions them in First Timothy chapter five. Now, angels don’t have salvation in the way humans do. There’s no redemption story for angels. So if the word elect meant chosen for salvation, it would make no sense to apply it to them. Instead, it refers to their role, their office, their place in the heavenly hierarchy. This is a powerful proof that election is about function, not favor.

From there, they dive into Romans, especially the famous golden chain in chapter eight. You know the verses: foreknew, predestined, called, justified, glorified. Calvinists read this as a logical sequence of decrees, a kind of blueprint for who gets saved and who doesn’t. But the fathers point out that all those verbs are in the past tense. Paul isn’t laying out a timeless order of divine decisions. He’s describing what God has already done in salvation history. The chain is about God’s faithfulness to His promises, not about individual selection.

They also tackle the hard saying in Romans nine, where Paul quotes the prophet Malachi: Jacob I loved, but Esau I hated. The fathers insist this is about nations, not individuals. Jacob and Esau are the ancestors of Israel and Edom. And the hatred here is about the Edomites’ betrayal of Israel, not about the eternal destiny of two brothers. Likewise, when Paul brings up Pharaoh, he’s not talking about Pharaoh’s personal salvation. He’s talking about how God used Pharaoh as an object lesson, to display His power and make His name known throughout the earth.

The fathers also spend time on the Calvinist debates between infralapsarianism and supralapsarianism. These are attempts to figure out the logical order of God’s decrees, whether He decided to allow the fall before He decided who to elect. The fathers argue that these systems are ultimately attempts to avoid making God the author of evil, but they end up creating more problems than they solve. The biblical picture is simpler and more mysterious. God works within history, calling people to faithfulness, not pre-programming their destinies.

So what does election actually mean? The fathers conclude that it means qualification. To be chosen is to be equipped for a role. We see this in military units, in elders, in the Messiah Himself. And in the New Testament, Christians are told to make their calling and election sure. Peter writes in Second Peter chapter one that we do this through effort, through growing in virtue and faithfulness. Election is not a one-time event. It’s a process, a calling to holiness and service. And that’s a much more hopeful and demanding message than the idea that God has already decided everything for us.
