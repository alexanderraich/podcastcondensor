In this episode, Fathers Andrew Stephen Damick and Stephen De Young take a deep look at what idolatry really is, and how it differs from Christian iconography. They start with a simple but powerful idea. Idolatry is not just the making of images. It is a technique. It is a way of trying to access, interact with, and ultimately control the spiritual realm. The pagan practice was to localize a deity, to bring a spirit that is not physically present into a specific place. By creating a body for that spirit, you create a version of that god tied to that location. The idol acts as a visual definition, limiting the deity to specific qualities. And that limitation is necessary for humans to gain leverage over it. It is a way of getting a handle on the divine.

This is the exact opposite of the Incarnation. In the Incarnation, God takes on human flesh to elevate humanity to union with Him. In idolatry, humans try to reduce the divine down to the human, or even the subhuman. It is an inversion of everything Christianity teaches about God becoming man.

The hosts trace this practice through history. Early idols were often theriomorphic, combining human and animal features. Over time, the practice developed from natural sites to crafted images. Even a philosopher like Plato, while critiquing the humanization of the gods, still operated within paganism. He replaced the gods with abstract Forms, which functioned in a similar way. They were concepts that could be shaped and delimited, and therefore manipulated.

The Old Testament is full of examples of Israelite idolatry. And this was not just worship of other gods. It was often idolatrous worship of Yahweh Himself. The Golden Calf is the archetypal example. Aaron made it while Moses was on Mount Sinai, and it was likely modeled on the Apis bull of Egypt. It was an attempt to worship the true God through an idol. The Northern Kingdom’s calves at Bethel and Dan, set up by Jeroboam, were the same thing. He did it to keep his people from traveling to Jerusalem, creating a new religious system. Even Solomon, for all his wisdom, built shrines to the gods of his foreign wives, leading to idolatry in the temple courts themselves.

There is also the incident at Baal-Peor, a localized form of Baal worshiped at Peor. This was associated with orgiastic fertility rites. It led Israel into idolatry and sexual immorality, and it resulted in a plague. Throughout Scripture, idolatry is linked with sexual immorality. This kind of pagan worship involved transgression of social boundaries to achieve ecstatic experiences.

The Jewish critique of idolatry is that it is self-contradictory. If a god has power, why can’t it stand up or dress itself? The idol is more limited than the human who made it. And those who worship idols become like them. They try to control the divine, but they end up being controlled by it. They become subhuman.

The hosts then turn to Christian idolatry. While literal paganism faded, idolatry persists. St. Paul identifies covetousness, or greed, as idolatry. It is an attempt to gain a handle on the forces of the world through material wealth. This is what Mammon is. It is wealth personified as a false god, representing the idolatrous pursuit of material security.

Concepts can also become idols. When a concept is mentally shaped and delimited, it can be manipulated. This applies to concepts like justice, love, or even God Himself, when He is reduced to human definitions. And the church itself can be treated as an idol. People can worship a concept of the church, using it as a handle for control, rather than recognizing it as the living body of Christ.

The hosts also clarify the distinction between worship and veneration. When the angel in Revelation refuses worship from John, it is a distinction between worship of God and veneration of creatures. It is not a prohibition of all bowing.

The episode concludes with a call to live in the present, trusting God rather than seeking control through idols. The prohibition against graven images in Exodus is about idolatry directed toward Yahweh, not about iconography. Iconography is a means of veneration, not a technique of control. And that is a topic for the next episode.
