In this episode, Fathers Andrew Stephen Damick and Stephen De Young take up the fourth in their series on the sacraments, turning their attention to the Holy Mystery of Ordination. They begin where the Bible begins, with Adam. Adam, placed in the garden of Eden, is presented as a prototype of the priesthood. His placement in that sanctuary is, in a real sense, a kind of ordination. From there, they move to Aaron and his sons, whose ordination is described in detail in the eighth chapter of Leviticus. Moses officiates at that ritual, even though he is not himself a priest. The chapter includes washing, vesting, anointing, and sacrifices, all of which set Aaron apart for his sacred task.

The fathers then explore the symbolism of the high priest’s vestments. The ephod carries twelve stones representing the tribes of Israel. The breastplate holds the Urim and Thummim, objects used for discerning God’s will, though their exact nature remains unknown. The high priest also wears a diadem, a gold plate inscribed with the name of Yahweh, marking him as the representative of God before the people. Aaron is presented as a new Adam, and the tabernacle and temple imagery evoke paradise itself. The high priest, like the cherubim and seraphim, serves as a throne guardian. His primary job is to guard the sanctuary and protect the people from the danger of approaching God unworthily. This includes fencing the table at the Eucharist, a role that connects directly to Saint Paul’s warning in First Corinthians, chapter eleven, about approaching the Lord’s Supper in an unworthy manner.

The conversation then turns to the budding of Aaron’s rod, found in Numbers seventeen. That dead piece of wood blossoms with new life, marking Aaron as God’s chosen priest. The fathers draw a striking parallel to the cross, itself a dead piece of wood that blossoms with new life, marking Christ as the great high priest and the true new Adam. In Numbers eighteen, God explains Aaron’s duties further. The high priest bears the sins of the people, leading them in repentance and representing them before God. This bearing of sins is not about penal substitution. The fathers read Isaiah fifty-three in this high priestly light, and they see Jesus’ healing miracles in Matthew eight as the fulfillment of that passage. Christ bears our sins in the sense of carrying them into the presence of God and interceding for us, not by absorbing divine punishment.

The discussion then moves to Saint Dionysius the Areopagite and his writings on the celestial and ecclesiastical hierarchies. The fathers contrast Eastern and Western readings of this saint. The West has often read Dionysius through a Neoplatonic lens, seeing hierarchies as ontological, as chains of being that mediate grace in a graded, almost physical way. The East, by contrast, reads them functionally. The orders of angels and the orders of clergy are not about levels of being but about roles and tasks within the community. Grace is understood as divine energy, not as a substance that gets passed down a chain.

This leads to a crucial point about ordination itself. Ordination is not about imparting an ontological change to a person. It does not change their essence or make them a different kind of being. Rather, ordination sets a person apart for a particular task. It is a gift from God, given to specific individuals for the service of the community. It is not a reward for personal merit or achievement. The grace of God works through the ordained person functionally, not ontologically. The priest is not holier by nature, but he is dedicated to a specific role within the body of Christ.

The fathers also address the priesthood of all believers. Israel is called a kingdom of priests and a holy nation in Exodus nineteen. This collective priesthood is a communal calling to represent God to the world and the world to God. It is distinct from the ordained priesthood, which exists to serve that collective calling. Repentance, they note, is not only individual but also collective. Communities can sin, and communities must repent corporately. Priests lead in this repentance, bearing the sins of the people as they guide them back to God.

Finally, they touch on the ordination service itself, particularly the Greek term parakatatheke, meaning deposit or charge. The bishop gives the newly ordained priest the consecrated bread to guard. This act symbolizes the priest’s role as a guardian of sacred things, a protector of the sanctuary, and a shepherd of the flock. Ordination, in the end, is not about power or status. It is about service, about being set apart so that God can work through a particular person for the good of the whole community.
