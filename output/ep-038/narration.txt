This episode of the Lord of Spirits is a question and answer session, and Fathers Andrew and Stephen tackle a wide range of listener questions. They begin by exploring the true meaning of the word "liturgy." It’s a common misconception that it means "work of the people," but historically, the Greek word *leitourgia* meant "public works," like a civic project for the city-state. In the Septuagint, it was used for the service of the Levites within the covenant, and Christians later adopted it for their public worship. The phrase often translated as "It is time for the Lord to act" is actually a mistranslation; the correct rendering is "It is time to begin the service of the Lord." So the liturgy is both God’s work for us and our service to Him, with God acting through the clergy and the people.

The conversation then turns to the nature of the soul. In the Hebrew Bible, the word *nephesh* simply means life. The soul is not an immortal, independent entity, but the life of the body. After death, that life continues contingently, preserved by God, and will return to the body at the resurrection. This is different from the Greek philosophical ideas of Plato, which have influenced Western thought. Demonic possession, then, involves a demonized life, not a disembodied soul.

They also address the Kingdom of God. It’s not a nation-state or a spiritualized territory, but the extension of the king’s rule—those under God’s influence and obedience. It’s a verbal idea, meaning kingship or dominion, rather than a reified realm. Christ is already enthroned and reigns, and the kingdom is established through ordering creation, culminating in the final judgment when all creation is set right.

On the Feast of Tabernacles, they explain that it commemorates Israel’s dependence on God during the wilderness wanderings. Living in booths was meant to unsettle oneself, especially at harvest time when settlement was most tempting. The feast also included offerings for the seventy nations, reflecting Israel’s priestly role. In Christianity, its themes are fulfilled in the Transfiguration and Pentecost.

The Greek term *pharmakeia*, translated as sorcery in Galatians, refers to the use of psychoactive substances within magical practices. In the ancient world, such substances were used in ritual contexts to induce altered states. Not all *pharmakeia* involves drugs, but some does, and it’s connected to folk magic and local practitioners, distinct from official temple worship.

They discuss priestly agency in the sacraments. In Orthodox theology, the priest is an instrument through which God acts. The Latin translation of the Greek *energeia* as *effectus* led to a Western focus on the sacrament itself producing grace, known as *ex opere operato*. The East, however, emphasizes God’s direct action through divine energies. This is why a layperson can baptize in an emergency—God is the true minister.

The evil eye is a real phenomenon involving envy or bitterness directed at someone, often invoking demonic power to harm them. It’s attested in ancient Ugaritic curses. Orthodox Christians should not use amulets like blue beads for protection, but instead rely on prayer, the sign of the cross, and holy water.

They also speak about re-enchanting science and mathematics. These disciplines can be seen as means of discovering divine beauty and order, rather than tools for power or consumerism. The ancient Pythagoreans and medieval thinkers saw mathematical and scientific truths as revealing spiritual realities, contrasting with modern instrumentalism.

On the second judgment, they clarify that the final judgment is where Christ sets all creation in its proper order permanently. The particular judgment at death is a continuation of one’s state. We pray for the departed because the final judgment has not yet occurred. The judgment is not a verdict but a putting-right of all things.

Catechesis should be formational, not merely informational. It’s about teaching how to pray, fast, give alms, and live an Orthodox Christian life, similar to an apprenticeship. The length and content should be tailored to the repentance and transformation needed, not just the completion of a syllabus.

They also discuss the sea god Yam. In the Hebrew Bible, *yam* often refers both to the physical body of water and to the sea deity, as ancient peoples did not separate the material from the spiritual. Calming the sea is also subduing Yam. The biblical authors did not deny the existence of such spirits but demoted them from gods to created beings under Yahweh’s authority.

On small-r revelation, they emphasize that Orthodox Christianity does not focus on seeking personal, discursive revelations, like God telling you whom to marry. Revelation is primarily the self-revelation of Christ, not a series of propositions. Discernment is about knowing Christ and making good choices, not divining a single perfect will. The idea that God micromanages every decision is a form of Calvinism that causes unnecessary anxiety.

The title *Panagia*, meaning All-Holy, for the Theotokos means she is set apart for a special purpose, not necessarily sinless in a juridical sense. Holiness is about being consecrated to God. The title calls us to likewise devote ourselves entirely to Christ.

Finally, they touch on the creature Ziz, a giant griffin-like bird in Jewish tradition, mentioned in Psalm fifty and Psalm eighty. It is the Hebrew version of the Mesopotamian Anzu, a magnificent but mischievous creature. Unlike Leviathan and Behemoth, Ziz is not portrayed as a chaos monster but as a symbol of divine power, reclaimed by God from foreign gods.
