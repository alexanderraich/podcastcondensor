In this episode, Father Andrew and Father Stephen take us on a tour of the ancient mystery cults, and they use them as a mirror to understand Christianity. They start by defining what a mystery cult actually was. Unlike the public, communal pagan worship of the day, these cults were private and individualistic. They were small groups focused on giving a single person a powerful, transformative experience. The goal was often to simulate a near-death experience, to bring someone to the brink and pull them back, so they would see their whole life in a new light. They did this through rituals, darkness, and sometimes substances.

The fathers then walk through some of the most famous cults. There were the Eleusinian Mysteries, which reenacted the story of Demeter and her daughter Persephone, who was abducted into the underworld. The initiates would drink a spiced wine called kykeon, which likely contained psychoactive ingredients, and they would go through a dramatic ritual led by a high priest called a hierophant. They also discuss the Dionysian Mysteries, which were wilder and more dangerous. These involved intoxication, frenzy, and even homophagy, which is the ritual act of eating a live animal. And then there was the strange cult of Antinous, the teenage lover of the Roman Emperor Hadrian. When Antinous died, Hadrian deified him and built a whole city in his honor, creating a mystery cult around his memory.

Now, a common claim is that Christianity is just another mystery cult, that it borrowed its ideas from these older religions. Father Andrew and Father Stephen push back hard on this. They admit there are superficial similarities, but the core differences are profound. For one thing, Christianity is communal, not individualistic. It is open to everyone, not a secret society for a select few. And most importantly, it is not centered on inducing a subjective experience. In historic Christianity, spiritual feelings are epiphenomenal. That means they are a byproduct, a secondary effect that might accompany the ritual, but they are not the point. The sacraments are valid whether you feel something or not. The mystery in the New Testament, the mysterion, is not secret knowledge for an elite. It is God’s plan of salvation, revealed in Christ for the whole world. Saint Paul talks about this in Ephesians, and he describes baptism in Romans as a real participation in Christ’s death and resurrection, not a symbolic trip to the underworld.

The hosts then turn a critical eye on modern American evangelicalism. They argue that it actually resembles the ancient mystery cults more than it resembles historic Christianity. It focuses on the individual’s personal experience, and it uses techniques, like altar calls and emotional manipulation, to induce altered states. They trace this back to Charles Finney, a nineteenth-century revivalist who believed conversion could be manufactured through the right methods. This is a stark contrast to the Orthodox liturgy, which is a repeated, ordered ritual that shapes how we perceive the world, but does not depend on our feelings.

The episode closes with a reflection on our calling as Christians. They even bring up the strange story of Jephthah’s daughter from the book of Judges, suggesting it might be an alternate origin for some of these pagan festivals. But the main point is that we are called to engage with the world, not to hide from it. They quote Saint Paul’s exhortation to think on what is true, noble, and pure. And they highlight the particular role of women in nurturing and bringing forth goodness in the world. The mystery cults were about escaping reality through a manufactured experience. Christianity, in contrast, is about embracing reality, participating in the life of the community, and letting God’s grace work in us, whether we feel it or not.
