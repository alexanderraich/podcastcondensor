In this episode, Father Andrew and Father Stephen take on the subject of angels, and they start by clearing away a lot of modern confusion. A big part of their discussion is the idea that angels are not a separate species of being. They don't reproduce, so each one is completely unique. The nine ranks of angels—Seraphim, Cherubim, Thrones, Dominions, Virtues, Powers, Principalities, Archangels, and Angels—are not biological categories. They are roles, or jobs, that God has assigned. This hierarchy reflects how each angel participates in God’s glory and the specific tasks they are given.

The hosts point to Dionysius the Areopagite, an early Christian theologian who wrote a book called *The Celestial Hierarchy*. That work systematized these nine ranks and had a huge influence on how the Church understands the angelic world. But the key point is that these ranks are about function, not nature. And this connects directly to us. When Jesus says in the Gospel of Luke that those who are sons of the resurrection are equal to the angels, it means glorified humans are being brought into this same heavenly structure. The Virgin Mary, the Theotokos, is the prime example of this. She is a human being, but she is exalted above the angels. So the celestial hierarchy is not closed off to us.

A lot of the episode is spent correcting the modern tendency to flatten the spiritual world. Many people today act as if angels are no longer active after Christ’s ascension, but the Book of Acts shows them still delivering messages and acting as intermediaries. The hosts also talk about the guardian angel tradition. Each baptized Christian is believed to have a guardian angel assigned to guide and protect them. But they are careful to say this is not a rigid box. The tradition also includes prayers for angels of peace and guidance, so it is not a simple one-to-one system.

The angels have their own free will and agency. They are not sock puppets or drones. Their obedience is a voluntary alignment with God’s will, just as human obedience is freely given. And this is where the fall of the angels comes in. Angels fall by aligning their will with human wills in rebellion against God. This often happens through idolatry and communion with demons. It happens in communion with humanity, and that explains how angels can be mutable. They are not locked into their choices by nature; they can change, and some did change for the worse.

Father Andrew and Father Stephen also get into the idea that God administers creation through angels and humans. He does not do this out of necessity, but out of love. He shares His work with us to create community. This is why the angels and the glorified saints intercede for us. It is not because God needs information. He already knows everything. It is an expression of love and communion, just like asking a friend to pray for you. Saint Paul urges intercessions for all people in his first letter to Timothy, and he says there is one mediator, Christ. The hosts make clear that this supports the practice of intercession, not opposes it.

The spiritual warfare they describe is not a physical sword fight. It is a battle for the mind and the heart. Angels and demons operate in what they call the noetic world, the world of thoughts and minds. They influence human consciousness, guiding us toward God or toward destruction. This is why the battle is so intense. It is a war of thoughts and ideas. The Holy Spirit’s indwelling is not primarily about feeding us information. It is about God’s presence in us, like in the temple. Claiming direct divine messages can actually be blasphemous, because it confuses our own thoughts with God’s voice.

They also bring up Psalm eighty-two, which talks about the judgment of angels who failed their tasks over the nations. This is connected to the Great Commission in Matthew twenty-eight, where Christ declares all authority and commands the disciples to make disciples of all nations. The idea is that the fallen angels over the nations are being replaced, and the Church is taking part in that work.

The episode closes with a reminder that we are not alone. The spiritual world is crowded with angels and saints. Engaging with them is not a distraction from our relationship with God. It is part of spiritual health. Even the word for monk, *monachos*, means alone, but in practice, monks are not alone because they are deeply engaged with this spiritual world. The hosts quote Revelation chapter five, which describes many angels around the throne praising the Lamb. That is the reality we are invited into. The battle for our minds is real, but so is the help that surrounds us.
