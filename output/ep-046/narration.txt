This episode begins a three-part series on the fall of man, and the hosts, Father Andrew and Father Stephen, start by tackling the first big element: death. They want to reframe how we think about it entirely. The conversation opens with the creation of the invisible world, the angels, and then moves to the fall of the devil and the expulsion from Paradise. But the central, surprising claim is that death is not a punishment from God. Instead, it’s a compassionate gift.

Think about it this way. If Adam and Eve had eaten from the tree of life after their disobedience, they would have become immortal in a fallen, sinful state. That would be a nightmare, a permanent prison of corruption. So God, in His mercy, cuts them off from that tree. He allows them to die physically, because physical death is actually the only thing that can put a stop to the downward spiral of sin. It creates a hard limit, a boundary. And within that boundary, there is room for repentance. There is room for change. If we lived forever in our fallen state, we would never change. We would just become more and more entrenched in our passions and our sins. Mortality, therefore, is a tool. It’s a gift that makes repentance possible.

The hosts then dig into the famous "garments of skin" that God makes for Adam and Eve in Genesis chapter three, verse twenty-one. They argue that these garments aren't just simple clothes. They symbolize a profound change in human nature itself. After the fall, humanity becomes mortal, animal-like, subject to the biological cycles of life and death, and vulnerable to the passions. We took on a new, heavier mode of existence. This is tied to the distinction between spiritual and physical death. The real death, the true tragedy, is spiritual death, which is the separation of the soul from God. That happened the moment they disobeyed. Physical death is just the outward consequence, the visible image of that inner spiritual catastrophe. Father Andrew and Father Stephen cite St. John of Damascus here to make this point clear. The body dies because the soul has already died to God.

Another major thread in this episode is the timing of the devil’s fall. A common idea is that Satan fell before creation, in some primordial rebellion. But the hosts argue, using St. Andrew of Caesarea’s commentary on Revelation, that the devil’s fall actually happens in Genesis chapter three. He was present in Paradise as a guardian cherub, as we see in the description of the King of Tyre in Ezekiel twenty-eight. He fell because of pride and envy. He envied humanity’s destiny, the incredible calling to be united with God. So, when he tempts Adam and Eve and they fall, he is cast down. This connects directly to Revelation chapter twelve, verses seven through nine, where the dragon is thrown out of heaven. That’s not a pre-creation event; it’s the cosmic consequence of what happened in the Garden.

This leads to the ultimate point of the episode, which is about our destiny. Humanity was made for theosis, for union with God. The fall interrupted that journey, but it didn’t destroy the destination. In Christ, that path is restored. And here’s the beautiful paradox: our mortality, our mutability, the very things that seem like curses, are the conditions that allow us to grow. They allow us to change, to repent, to turn back toward the light. The hosts urge listeners to see their own lives, their own struggles with the passions, not as obstacles to God, but as the very material He uses to draw us closer to Him. The goal is not just to be good people, but to be united with Christ, to become partakers of the divine nature. And it is precisely our weakness, our mortality, that makes that journey possible.
