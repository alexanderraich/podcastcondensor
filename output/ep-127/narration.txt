In this episode, Fathers Andrew Stephen Damick and Stephen De Young take on a very popular internet meme. You have probably seen it yourself. It claims that Christmas, and really all of Christianity, is just a copycat of the ancient Roman mystery cult of Mithras. The fathers show that this idea is not just wrong, but that it falls apart the moment you look at the actual history.

They start by going back to the very beginning. The god Mithra is ancient, far older than Rome. He appears in Hittite treaties and in the Rig Veda. In those early Indo-Persian religions, he was a covenant mediator, a god who oversaw agreements and promises. But the Roman Mithras was something different. Roman Mithraism emerged in the mid-to-late first century AD, around the time of the emperor Nero. That is important. It means the cult postdates Christianity by at least thirty years. Christianity was already established and spreading before Roman Mithraism even existed.

The fathers then describe what Roman Mithraism actually looked like. It was popular among Roman soldiers. They worshiped in caves, or in artificial caves called Mithraea, which were often underground. The central image in these caves was the tauroctony, a sculpture of Mithras slaying a bull. This image was packed with astrological symbolism, representing cosmic creation and renewal. The initiates also shared a sacred meal, and there were grades of initiation. Mithras himself is often shown wearing a Phrygian cap, that soft conical cap we associate with Persia. He was born from a rock, and that rock, called Petra Genetrix, was itself worshiped as the mother rock.

Now, the fathers take on the specific claims of the mythicists. Did Mithras have a virgin birth? No. He was born from a rock. Did he have twelve disciples? No. He had two attendants. Was he born on December twenty-fifth? There is no evidence for that at all. The claim is based on nothing. Did he die and rise again? There is no evidence for that either. That is a complete fabrication of modern mythicists. The fathers point out that these false parallels are not just sloppy. They are often based on misinformation that has been repeated so many times people just assume it is true.

They also bring in a fascinating detail from the Bible itself. The name Mithra appears in the scriptures. In the book of Ezra, chapter one, verse eight, there is a Persian official named Mithridates. The name also shows up in the Greek text of First Esdras, in chapter two. This shows that the name was common in the Persian court, but it has nothing to do with a savior god.

The fathers also discuss the second-century pagan philosopher Celsus, who wrote against Christianity. We know his work only because Origen responded to it. Celsus was a real critic, but even he never claimed Christianity came from Mithras. That idea simply did not exist in the ancient world.

Father Andrew and Father Stephen then step back to reflect on the bigger picture. They talk about the importance of critical thinking. When you hear a claim that sounds dramatic, you should check the sources. The parallels between Christianity and Mithraism are superficial at best. They are often just distortions. Christianity is not derived from Mithraism. It is unique.

They close with a warning about ecumenical movements that downplay doctrinal differences. When you blur the lines between religions, you lose the truth. You end up with a vague spirituality that does not actually save anyone. The fathers encourage us to be discerning, to know our history, and to hold fast to the faith that was once delivered to the saints. The story of Mithras is interesting, but it is not the story of our salvation.
