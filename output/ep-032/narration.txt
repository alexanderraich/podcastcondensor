In this episode, Father Andrew and Father Stephen take up one of the biggest questions in the Gospels: who is the Son of Man? It is the title Jesus uses for himself more than any other, appearing over eighty times across all four Gospels. But the phrase did not start with Jesus. It has a long history in the Old Testament and in the Jewish world between the testaments, and understanding that history changes how we hear Jesus’ own words.

The hosts begin with the Hebrew and Greek behind the phrase. In the Old Testament, “son of man” often just means a mortal human being. It emphasizes weakness and frailty. The prophet Ezekiel is called “son of man” ninety-three times, and the hosts point out that this was not a compliment. It was a reminder that he was a fragile man speaking to a people who were spiritually dead, the “undead” in exile. The same usage appears in Job, where it simply means a human being in all his limitation. So the title is not always about Jesus. Sometimes it is just about being human.

But then the conversation turns to Daniel chapter seven. There, the prophet sees something extraordinary. One like a son of man comes on the clouds of heaven and approaches the Ancient of Days. The Ancient of Days is depicted as an elderly figure with white hair, seated on a fiery throne. And this son of man figure receives dominion, glory, and an everlasting kingdom. The hosts stress that this is not just a human being. Coming on the clouds is something Yahweh does in the Psalms. Psalm one hundred four says the Lord rides on the clouds. So Daniel is describing a second divine figure, one who shares the identity and authority of God himself.

This vision became the foundation for a widespread Second Temple Jewish belief often called the two powers in heaven. Many Jews at the time believed there were two divine figures in heaven, both somehow identified as Yahweh. Some thought the second figure was a divinized human, like Enoch or Moses. Others thought he was a created divine being, like the angel Metatron. Still others believed he was an eternal aspect of God, a hypostasis of Yahweh himself. The hosts explain that this was not a fringe idea. It was a lively and important tradition.

The Book of First Enoch develops this figure further. There, the Son of Man is pre-existent, chosen before creation, and he will come to execute judgment and save the righteous. The New Testament even quotes First Enoch. The letter of Jude cites Enoch’s prophecy about the Lord coming with his holy ones. So this text was taken seriously by the earliest Christians.

Then the hosts turn to the Gospels. At his trial before the high priest, Jesus is asked directly if he is the Messiah. He answers by identifying himself with the Son of Man from Daniel, the one who will be seated at the right hand of power and come with the clouds of heaven. That is the moment the high priest tears his robes and charges Jesus with blasphemy. The priests understood exactly what Jesus was claiming. He was saying he was the divine figure from Daniel, the second power in heaven.

The episode also covers what happened later in rabbinic Judaism. After the second century, the rabbis labeled belief in two powers as heresy. They expelled Christians from the synagogues and suppressed the texts that supported this view. The Talmud contains stories about figures like Elisha ben Avuya, also called Aher, who was condemned for believing in two powers. Even Rabbi Akiva is shown being corrected when he interpreted the two thrones in Daniel as one for God and one for David.

Finally, the hosts reflect on what this means for Christmas. The infant Jesus is not just a gentle baby in a manger. He is the divine warrior from Daniel, the one who comes to defeat the demonic powers and be enthroned as Lord of all. The book of Revelation even describes the risen Christ with white hair like wool, echoing the Ancient of Days. So the Son of Man is not merely a human title. It is a claim to divine authority, a claim that changes everything about how we read the Gospels and how we understand the child born in Bethlehem.
