In this episode, Fathers Andrew and Stephen take up the sacrament of holy matrimony within the Orthodox Christian tradition. They begin at the very beginning, with the creation of humanity in Genesis. The hosts emphasize that humanity is created in the image of God as male and female. This was a radical claim in the ancient world, where the idea that both sexes bear the image of God equally was virtually unheard of. They then turn to the story of Adam’s aloneness. The Hebrew word often translated as “rib” actually means “side.” So Eve was not made from a rib, but from Adam’s side, showing that she is a partner who corresponds to him, a helper fit for him. The foundational principle here is that marriage is the union of two becoming one flesh, a divine action rooted in the very beginning.

The conversation then moves through the patriarchal narratives. Abraham and Sarah are held up as examples of faithfulness, and their marriage is even referenced in the Orthodox wedding service. Isaac and Rebekah are presented as the ideal couple in Genesis. Their marriage was arranged, yet Isaac loved Rebekah, showing that love is not primarily a feeling but a concrete commitment. Jacob’s story is more complicated. He married Leah and Rachel and had children with their maidservants, and his life illustrates the deep problems of polygamy. The hosts are clear that polygamy is condemned in Scripture, pointing to Leviticus and the negative examples of such marriages. It is not endorsed by the Bible.

The discussion then turns to the nature of love itself. In the ancient world, love was often seen as a kind of weakness. But Christian love is something else entirely. It is not a feeling but a concrete action of self-sacrifice and service, directed even toward enemies. This is the foundation of marital love. The hosts also critique modern romantic love, which they describe as consumeristic, focused on what one can get from the other rather than what one can give.

Moving to the New Testament, they examine Christ’s teaching on marriage in Matthew nineteen. Jesus quotes Genesis to affirm the indissolubility of marriage. They also look at St. Paul’s instructions in Ephesians five, where he calls marriage a profound mystery, referring to Christ and the Church. This is the heart of the matter. Marriage is a sacrament because it participates in the mystery of Christ and his bride, the Church. The prophet Malachi declared that God makes husband and wife one with a portion of the Spirit. So marriage is not merely a human contract; it is a divine action, a means of theosis, of becoming like God.

The hosts also address the historical practice of weddings. They debunk the idea that Christians simply adopted Roman civil ceremonies. Roman weddings were deeply pagan, involving sacrifices to household gods, a fake kidnapping, and a procession. Christians could not participate in such things. Instead, they transformed these practices, creating a distinctly Christian rite. The Orthodox wedding service references the patriarchal marriages and includes the reading from Ephesians, emphasizing that God joins the couple together.

Finally, they explore the theological depth of marriage. The Bible consistently uses the imagery of Christ as the bridegroom and the Church as his bride, from the Old Testament prophets to the book of Revelation, where the consummation of all things is depicted as the wedding feast of the Lamb. Marriage is not an end in itself. It is a path to salvation, a way of bringing people into the household of God. The Church itself is a family, where all believers are brothers and sisters. In the end, marriage is about participating in the life of God, and through the Holy Spirit, the union of husband and wife becomes a living icon of Christ’s love for his Church.
