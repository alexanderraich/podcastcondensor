In this episode, Father Andrew and Father Stephen take us deep into the Bible’s understanding of sacred space, and they start where it all begins: the Garden of Eden. The word “paradise” actually comes from a Persian term for a walled garden, and Eden is described as just that—a garden on a mountain, with four rivers flowing out from it. This is the pattern for everything that follows. In the ancient Near East, people believed the gods lived on mountains, and Scripture tells us plainly that Eden was on the holy mountain of God. So paradise is not just a physical location; it is wherever God is present, the experience of communion with Him.

From there, the hosts trace the story of the tabernacle. Moses was shown a heavenly tent on Mount Sinai, and the tabernacle was built as a copy and shadow of that heavenly reality. The imagery is fascinating. In the ancient world, the gods were envisioned as living in a large tent, with partitioned sections for each deity, much like a patriarchal clan. This is the backdrop for Jesus’ words in John’s Gospel about His Father’s house having many mansions—the Greek word actually means partitions or rooms, referring to this divine council tent. The tabernacle itself was designed as a microcosm of the universe. The seven-branched lampstand represented the seven planets, and the cherubim woven into the curtains represented the heavenly council.

But then came Solomon. He built a permanent temple in Jerusalem, and while it was grand, it was a departure from God’s original design. In pagan thought, the temple building was seen as the body of the god, and Solomon’s temple incorporated pagan elements, like the chariot of the sun god. God’s response was to distance Himself from that temple. The prophets, like Jeremiah, warned that the temple was not a talisman and that God could leave it. The hosts emphasize that God never really wanted a permanent temple; He preferred the tabernacle because it kept Israel from settling down and becoming corrupt. The temple often led to syncretism with pagan practices.

The episode then traces how God’s presence moved—from the tabernacle, to the temple, then to the prophets, and ultimately to Christ and the Church. In the New Testament, the tabernacle remains the pattern for heavenly worship. The Book of Revelation describes the heavenly sanctuary as the tent of witness, not a temple. And this is crucial: Orthodox Christian churches are designed after the tabernacle, not the temple. Baptism corresponds to the laver, confession to the altar of burnt offering, and the Eucharist to the bread of the presence. The church building itself is a microcosm of creation.

Father Andrew and Father Stephen also address Ezekiel’s vision of a future temple. They explain that this is not a literal building but a typological prophecy of Christ’s body, as understood by early Christians and Second Temple Judaism. The true temple is Christ Himself.

Finally, they make a powerful point about the Christian life. Christianity is primarily a way of life, not just a set of beliefs. Faith is shown by works, as the Apostle James wrote. The early church was known for its practices, such as venerating the relics of the saints. Pagans like the emperor Julian the Apostate and the philosopher Eunapius of Sardis mocked Christians for venerating the bones of criminals—the martyrs. But the hosts explain that this veneration is a testimony to the resurrection of the dead. The saints are alive in Christ, and their bodies are holy, temples of the Holy Spirit.

Believers are called to live as aliens and strangers, not settling into the world’s cultures. The goal is theosis—becoming like God through participation in His divine life. Paradise is wherever God is present, and we are invited to dwell there with Him, following the pattern He has given us.
