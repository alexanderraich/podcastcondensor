Father Andrew Stephen Damick and Father Stephen DeYoung open this episode with a question that has haunted philosophers for centuries, the so-called Euthyphro dilemma. Is something good because God commands it, or does God command it because it is already good? The fathers argue that both answers are traps. If good exists as a standard above God, then God is not supreme. He is bound by something outside Himself, which leads to an endless chain of standards above standards. But if good is simply whatever God decides, then saying God is good becomes nothing more than saying God is God. The universe itself would be morally neutral, and goodness would be an arbitrary divine whim.

They then walk through the major ethical systems that try to answer this question. Divine command theory collapses into that tautology. Natural law tradition has biblical roots, but it fails to account for the fact that Christian moral principles were not discovered through human reason alone. They had to be revealed. Ancient civilizations, for all their wisdom, were morally blind in ways that required direct revelation to correct. Consequentialism, which judges actions by their outcomes, runs into a serious problem. God brings good out of evil, as we see in the story of Joseph. But that does not make the evil good. Evil remains evil even when God redeems it. So consequences cannot be the measure of morality. Deontological ethics, the rule-based system associated with Immanuel Kant, fails in concrete situations. The fathers point out that Christian tradition has always allowed exceptions for higher goods, such as lying to save a life. Kant’s abstract rules simply cannot handle the messy reality of human existence.

The fathers also critique the modern obsession with motives. Pre-modern Christian ethics judged actions, not inner intentions. Involuntary sins were still sins. But modern focus on motives leads to neurotic self-analysis and denies the goodness of many actions. They bring up the parable of the two sons from Matthew twenty-one. One son says he will work but does not, the other refuses but then goes. The one who actually does the right thing, even with the wrong attitude, is still doing the right thing.

They then turn to the biblical understanding of evil itself. In Genesis, sin is described as crouching at the door, a malevolent force seeking to master a person. This is not a mere absence of good or a private moral failing. Evil is an enslaving power that brings death. Good, by contrast, is life and flourishing. The Hebrew word tov, used in Genesis, means good, beautiful, whole, and intact. The Greek kalos carries the same sense of beauty and goodness. When Christ says in John ten that He came to give life abundantly, He is talking about flourishing, not mere biological existence.

This leads to the heart of the Orthodox answer. The fathers introduce the distinction between God’s essence and His energies. God’s essence is utterly unknowable and incomprehensible. But His energies, His eternal activities in creation, are knowable. When we say God is good, we are not describing His essence. We are describing His energies. This solves the Euthyphro dilemma in a third way. Goodness is not a standard above God, nor is it an arbitrary command. Goodness is what God does. It is His eternal activity.

Human goodness, then, is not about following rules or calculating outcomes. It is about participating in these divine energies. This is the path of theosis, the process of becoming like God. Following the commandments is not about earning favor or avoiding punishment. It is the way we enter into God’s own life and are transformed by it. Real freedom is not the ability to choose between good and evil. As Saint Maximus the Confessor teaches, in the age to come we will be free because we will be unable to sin. True freedom is the freedom to flourish as a human being, and that only comes through participation in God’s life.

The fathers contrast this with the views of thinkers like Thomas Hobbes, who claimed all human actions are either self-centered or other-centered, with only altruism being good. They find this unrealistic and absurd. They also critique Sam Harris, the contemporary atheist who proposes a utilitarian ethics based on maximizing well-being. Without God and a final reckoning, there is no rational basis for altruism. The wicked often prosper in this life, as the example of Henry Kissinger shows. Ethics requires God and judgment to make any sense.

They also address the problem of God’s nature. Some argue that God cannot do evil because His nature prevents it. But the fathers point out that God’s nature is infinite. An infinite nature cannot restrict Him. If it does, then it is finite. John Calvin’s view of total depravity is also critiqued, as it makes all human actions evil and places a standard of justice above God. The fathers contrast this with the Christian view of human nature, which Saint Paul preached as universal and dignified. This was revolutionary in the ancient world, where Aristotle held that some people were natural slaves and women were inferior. Saint John Chrysostom’s homilies challenged these pagan views, advocating for compassion toward the poor and disabled.

The episode concludes with practical implications. Christian communities should be places of true human flourishing, not utopian political projects. Goodness is not a set of rules or outcomes. It is a way of being, a participation in the eternal life of God. And that life is abundant, beautiful, and whole.
