This episode is about dreams — not the psychology of dreams, not dream dictionaries, but what dreams actually are in the biblical and ancient Near Eastern world, and what they mean for a Christian trying to live faithfully. The hosts open with a line from the book of Enoch, from the forty-eighth chapter, where wisdom is described as being given to the elect and holy, and they use that as a doorway into a subject that turns out to be far older and far stranger than most modern people suspect.

Before they get to the substance, there is a moment of remembrance. Peter Cullen, the voice of Optimus Prime, had died, and the hosts take a moment to say memory eternal for him. This leads to a question some Orthodox listeners wonder about: can you pray for someone who was not Orthodox? The answer they give is yes, and they cite a pastoral instruction of St. John Maximovich, who made clear there is no rule forbidding the commemoration of non-Orthodox departed. The conversation drifts through Kevin Conroy and Transformers, but the point about commemoration is worth holding onto.

The topic itself, oddly enough, was suggested by a Google AI that had been scanning Reddit. The hosts joke that this is probably the most AI will ever be involved in the show, and then they get to work.

Dreams are as old as humanity. Dogs dream. Every culture that has left any written record has left records of dreams and what people thought about them. The difficulty is that we cannot access what prehistoric people thought about dreams, because without written texts we have no window into their understanding. What we do have is the earliest written material, and that material is remarkably consistent.

Before going further, the hosts correct a misconception that runs deep in modern religious culture. People today tend to imagine the spiritual world as a separate dimension, an astral plane, some other realm you travel to. That is not the biblical picture. The word astral comes from the word for stars, and the astral plane is not the spiritual world of Scripture. The spiritual world is an overlay on the material world. Think of an overhead projector with transparencies stacked on top of one another, or the way CGI layers are composited into a film. The spiritual world is right here, interpenetrating the material world at every point. What perceives it is the nous, the faculty of spiritual perception in the human person, and the nous is purified through theosis, through the lifelong process of being transformed into the likeness of God. The classic example is Elisha's servant, who was terrified by the army surrounding them, until Elisha prayed and the servant's eyes were opened to see the angelic host already present on the hills. Nothing changed except his perception. The material and spiritual worlds are constantly interacting. Giving a cup of cold water to a child has spiritual effect. This is not metaphor.

With that established, the hosts turn to dreams as a means of entrance into the spiritual world. In the ancient Near East, dreams were understood as a passive way of encountering God, spirits, demons, and departed human beings. You do not go into a dream the way St. John goes into his visions in Revelation, asking questions and receiving answers. You receive a dream. It comes to you. This passivity is the key distinction between a dream and a vision. A vision is active; a dream is passive and requires interpretation. And interpretation itself is not a scientific decoding of symbols. It is a prophetic or visionary act.

In the ancient world, most people were not lucid dreamers. The minority who were became religious functionaries — prophets, seers, interpreters. The earliest Semitic terms for prophet meant seer. And prophethood itself had two tiers. The primary tier was direct visionary encounter with God, the kind Moses had face to face. The secondary tier was dreams. This distinction matters for everything that follows.

Dream interpretation is a form of divination. The technical term is oneromancy, and it is attested from the earliest written texts. At Mari, in Mesopotamia, around three thousand BC, we find dream interpretation texts. Egyptian and Mesopotamian dream divination are nearly identical in their methods and assumptions, which points to a common prehistoric origin — and by prehistory the hosts mean before writing, not before cavemen. There is also a distinction between spontaneous dreams and induced dreams. Induced dreams, brought on by drugs or hallucinogens, were used practically, to seek knowledge of the future from the gods.

The Epic of Gilgamesh gives a window into how dreams actually functioned in that world. Gilgamesh dreams of finding a friend, and his mother, the goddess Ninsun, interprets the dream for him. Even Gilgamesh, who is two-thirds divine, cannot interpret his own dreams. Later, before the fight with Humbaba, there are induced dreams, and Enkidu reinterprets them positively. This is important: omens and dreams are not completely fatalistic. Interpreters can differ, and the interpretation can change the outcome. Then Enkidu has his own dreams — first of the council of the gods decreeing his death, then of the horrors of Sheol. These are not divination. They are passive entry into the spiritual world, and they foretell what is coming.

The Assyrian Dream Book, called Ishkar Zaqiqu, takes all of this and formalizes it. Zaqiqu means both spirit or ghost and the god of dreams. The book is a manual, something like a diagnostic manual, listing dream diagnoses and ritual cures. Dream of eating human flesh? Wealth is coming. The book recognizes three categories of dreams: messages from spirits, predictive dreams, and purely internal dreams. This last category is crucial. Ancient people acknowledged that many dreams were just internal, not mystical. They were not naive. The book also lists the various functionaries involved in dream interpretation: the sha'ilu, or dream interpreter; the astrologer; the mahhu, an ecstatic; the rakimu and rakintu, prophet and prophetess; the baru, a diviner or haruspex; and the ashipu, an exorcist. Mesopotamian exorcism differs from Christian exorcism — it involves inviting good spirits to drive out bad ones. By the first millennium BC, dream interpretation was a formalized societal institution with royal staff.

At this point the hosts take questions. The first caller asks how to respond when young children report dreams of demons or patron saints. The advice is practical: focus on what the child does with the dream, not on the phenomenon itself. Teach children about protection through baptism, prayer, repentance, and the patronage of the saints. Do not dismiss dreams as unreal — that is not a healthy way to deal with fear. One of the hosts shares an anecdote about his great-nephew, who saw a ghost, grabbed an icon, charged it, and the ghost disappeared. The goal is to raise dragon-slayers, not people who do not believe in dragons.

The second caller asks about profound mystical dreams and about extraterrestrial and UFO phenomena. The answer extends the principle: the source matters less than the response. Use whatever comes for repentance and faithfulness to Christ's commandments. Even demonic activity is permitted by God for our repentance. Whether a dream comes from God, an angel, a saint, or a demon, receive it as from the Lord and let it drive you to repentance. Unusual dreams should be discussed with a spiritual father or confessor, not acted on as private revelation. Do not denounce your parish because of a dream. Human beings have always done terrible things, and the Christian mission is within that same dark context.

A third question touches on dream discernment and premonitions, but the hosts have already covered this ground. Dreams are judged by their fruit. A dream that makes you think you are spiritually superior is bad. A comforting dream of a departed loved one does not need to be over-analyzed; just thank God. Premonition dreams and deja vu are common, and it is probably useful that we do not remember most of them, so we do not think we know the future.

Then the hosts turn to Scripture, and the picture that emerges is one of broad continuity with the ancient Near East. Numbers chapter twelve, verse six: God speaks to prophets in visions and dreams, but not like Moses, face to face. Deuteronomy chapter thirteen, verses one through five: false prophets and dreamers of dreams are paralleled and must be rejected if they lead astray. Jeremiah chapter twenty-three, verses twenty-five through thirty-two: prophets claim to have dreamed, but false dreams lead people astray; true prophets speak God's word. Jeremiah twenty-seven, verse nine, and twenty-nine, verse eight: do not listen to dreamers who deceive. The connection between dreams and prophecy persists from Moses to the late monarchy.

In the New Testament, Peter's Pentecost sermon in Acts chapter two, quoting Joel chapter two, declares that dreams will be available to all because of the Holy Spirit. Your old men shall dream dreams. This is not the abolition of dreams but their democratization. And the story of King Saul in First Samuel chapter twenty-eight shows what happens when God withdraws. Saul inquired of the Lord, but got no answer by dreams, by Urim, or by prophets. So he sought the witch of Endor. Dreams were a standard way God communicated with people, including kings. When that channel closed, Saul was left in the dark.

But Scripture also recognizes that many dreams are merely internal. Job chapter twenty, verse eight; Psalm seventy-three, verse twenty; Psalm ninety, verse five; Ecclesiastes chapter five, verse three; Isaiah chapter twenty-nine, verses seven and eight; Isaiah chapter fifty-six, verse ten. These describe dreams as fleeting and insignificant, reflecting the dreamer's mental state. The hosts share their own stress dreams. One dreams he is enrolled in a class he did not know about, and the final exam is approaching. The other dreams of overlooking something important, or of harm coming to his children. These dreams have no greater significance than personal anxiety.

Genesis shows a pattern. Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob have direct encounters with God. Jacob also has the dream of the ladder in Genesis chapter twenty-eight. But Abimelech, a Gentile, receives a warning in a dream in Genesis chapter twenty. Joseph has no direct encounters with God at all. All his communication is through dreams and through interpreting dreams — his own dreams of his family bowing down, and his interpretation of the cupbearer's, the baker's, and Pharaoh's dreams. The pattern is that as the patriarchs decline morally, God steps back and communicates through dreams for their sake.

Daniel parallels Joseph. As a young man in a pagan court, he interprets dreams — Nebuchadnezzar's statue in Daniel chapter two, the beasts in Daniel chapter seven, the tree in Daniel chapter four. Dreams are often sent to Gentiles: Abimelech, Pharaoh, Nebuchadnezzar, and Pilate's wife in Matthew chapter twenty-seven, verse nineteen. In the New Testament, Joseph receives a series of dreams from angels — to take Mary as his wife, to flee to Egypt, to return, and to go to Galilee — because no prophet was active at that time.

A caller asks about the difference between dreams and visions, and about the importance of an ancient understanding of the mind. The distinction is that dreams are passive while visions allow interaction. St. John in Revelation asks questions and receives answers. A dream comes to you; a vision is a conversation. On the ancient mind, the hosts affirm that modern mental health concepts are valid and real, but they must not replace the spiritual. Spiritual, mental, and physical health are separable but deeply interrelated. Mental illness should not be reduced to spiritual problems, nor physical illness to spiritual states. The ancient understanding of the nous is important for Orthodox theology, but it does not evict other forms of knowledge.

Then the conversation turns to Shakespeare, and to the invention of the individual. In Hamlet, the question "Who's there?" reflects the individual's isolation. Hamlet talks to himself and ignores others. In A Midsummer Night's Dream, the spiritual world is real but only relevant insofar as it concerns us. This marks a transition. The shift from ancient to modern involves the rise of the individual, which will lead to Freud and Jung. Tolkien disliked Shakespeare for shrinking fairies into tiny, materialized pests. Modern culture treats spirits as trivial. The film Labyrinth shows fairies as bugs to be exterminated, the final materialization. Individualism creates a private world, which enables materialism. The Reformation pushed saints and angels aside, then God, leaving only the self. And now even the self is dissolving.

A caller named Monica asks about three sources of dreams and how to discern them. The answer applies the framework already established: dreams can be internal, demonic, or divine, but the source is often ambiguous. The key is how one responds. All dreams can be received as from God if they lead to repentance and faithfulness. Sharing dreams requires a close relationship and sobriety; a confessor can help discern whether a dream has significance for a community. Start by assuming a dream is for you personally. Another caller, Rudy, asks about shepherding a spouse's dreams, and receives the same advice: consult your parish priest, and assume any spiritual experience is first for you.

Then the hosts turn to the modern views. Freud said dreams are triggered by daytime events, express wishes, including the death drive, and are censored by the conscious mind. Jung said dreams come from a collective unconscious, a shared layer of all human minds connected telepathically, with universal symbols. Jung's Red Book emerged from his deliberate immersion in that collective unconscious. Jung denied the supernatural, seeing telepathy as a scientific reality. Both Freud and Jung keep dreams within the human plane, excluding God, angels, and demons. The New Age pole is no better. Using Llewellyn's Complete Dictionary of Dreams as an example, the hosts describe a system in which dreams are messages from a divine higher self, guiding you toward your future divine self. The symbols are a chaotic mix drawn from Jung, paganism, medieval sources, and pure invention. All modern approaches share the assumption that dreams are purely human, and that assumption sets people up for deception. Demons can use dream dictionaries to mislead.

The trade-off is real. Modern technological progress came at the cost of neglecting spiritual wisdom. Humanity has regressed in its understanding of dreams. The goal is not to return to the ancient world but to reacquire spiritual wisdom to guide material knowledge. Spiritual wisdom is more important than material knowledge.

The practical takeaway is restraint and caution. That is the normal posture toward spiritual experiences. Assume they are for you. Test the spirits. Live an ordinary life of prayer, fasting, and almsgiving. The irony is that the modern age's scientific explanations actually set us up for less knowledge, not more. The framework helps read Scripture better.

And the closing exhortation is this: give up on your dreams. Dreams of the past or the future distract from the beauty and blessings God has placed in the present. This is the day the Lord has made. Live today and see what God is giving you now.
