Father Andrew Stephen Damick and Father Stephen De Young continue their series on the history of Israel, moving from Joshua’s conquest through the divided monarchy and into the exile. They start by tackling a common Protestant idea head-on: that the Torah was given to show people they cannot keep it. The fathers argue this is backwards. God’s commandments are keepable. They point to Deuteronomy chapter thirty, verse eleven, where God says the commandment is not too difficult for you. The Torah was a gift, a guide for life in the land, not a trap to prove human failure. And they point to King David as proof. David kept the Torah perfectly, not because he never sinned, but because he repented when he did, exactly as the Torah prescribes.

Then they turn to the conquest of Canaan. This was not a genocide. It was a judgment against specific giant clans, like the Anakim and the Rephaim, who practiced human sacrifice, cannibalism, and sexual immorality. The goal was to end those abhorrent practices, not to wipe out every human being. Individuals could leave those groups and join Israel. Caleb and Rahab are the prime examples. The conquest was targeted, and it was just.

The hosts then trace the long, sad story of the tribes failing to fully possess the land God gave them. They walk through the period of the judges, the people demanding a king like other nations, and the rise of Saul. Saul was a war chief, not a medieval king, and he failed to execute God’s judgment on the Amalekites, a giant clan descended from Esau who had attacked Israel at Mount Sinai. That failure led to his rejection. Then came David, a man after God’s own heart. He completed the conquest by killing the remaining giants, received the Davidic covenant, and became the ancestor of the Messiah. His son Solomon built the Temple but fell into idolatry and polygamy. He repented at the end of his life, as reflected in Ecclesiastes, and is considered a saint, but his sins tore the kingdom apart. Jeroboam, son of Nebat, set up golden calves at Dan and Bethel, establishing a state religion of idolatry that poisoned the Northern Kingdom for its entire history.

A key theme throughout is the distinction between God giving and humans receiving. God gave the land, as Joshua chapter twenty-one makes clear. Verses forty-three through forty-five state plainly that all the promises were fulfilled. Not one failed. But not all the tribes took possession of what was given. This distinction is crucial for understanding Saint Paul’s arguments about faith and works. God gives the gift, but people must faithfully receive it. The hosts use this to refute dispensationalist claims that some land promises remain unfulfilled. They argue that reading Joshua twenty-one honestly destroys that whole system.

They also emphasize the faithful remnant. Despite the collective disobedience of Israel and Judah, there were always individuals who kept the Torah. This is important for understanding Saint Paul’s collective language about the curse of the law in Galatians chapter three. The curse was the collective exile of the people, not a metaphysical curse on every individual.

Finally, they come to the exile itself. The Assyrians destroyed the Northern Kingdom in 722 BC with extreme brutality. The Babylonians under Nebuchadnezzar conquered Judah and destroyed the Temple in 586 BC. The fathers explain the seventy-year exile as repayment for seventy missed Sabbath years. Israel never observed the Sabbath year or the Jubilee, so God acted to establish the order prescribed by the Torah, even through judgment. Second Chronicles chapter thirty-six, verse twenty-one, says the land enjoyed its Sabbaths. The exile was not random punishment; it was the fulfillment of the curses written in the Torah itself. And the church fathers traditionally taught that any future temple would be built by the Antichrist, not as a positive event. This whole story, from conquest to exile, sets the stage for the coming Messiah, the one who would finally and fully receive the inheritance on behalf of all.
