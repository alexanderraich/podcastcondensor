In this episode, Fathers Andrew and Stephen take us back to a foundational moment in the Gospels. When Saint Peter confesses that Jesus is the Christ, the Son of the living God, how did he and the other disciples come to that knowledge? The answer, they argue, is not that Jesus checked off a list of prophecies. Instead, the disciples recognized Him through direct encounter. They saw that Jesus embodied Israel, the Torah, and the God of Israel Himself.

Let’s take the first of those. Jesus embodies Israel. The history of the people of God in the Old Testament is a story of calling and failure. Israel was called to be faithful, but time and again stumbled. Jesus recapitulates that history. He succeeds where Israel failed. The Gospel of Matthew makes this explicit when he quotes the prophet Hosea, saying, “Out of Egypt I called my son.” That was originally about Israel, but Matthew applies it to Jesus. Jesus relives the experience of the nation. He is the true Israel. And because He is, He is recognized as the Messiah.

Then there is the Torah. The fathers are careful to explain that the Torah is not just law. The Greek word for it is nomos, which means a way of life, a custom. The Jewish tradition calls it halakha, which means walking, or how you live. So when Jesus says He came not to abolish the law but to fulfill it, He means He fills it full. He embodies the Torah as a way of life. He teaches it and lives it perfectly. Saint Paul understood this too. He taught that Christians walk according to the Spirit, which is the true fulfillment of the Torah. Jesus is the living Torah.

And finally, Jesus embodies the God of Israel. This is where the fathers spend much of their time. The God revealed in the Hebrew Scriptures is not a distant, angry deity. He is a God of humility. He identifies with the weak and the powerless. We see this throughout the Old Testament. And Jesus reveals that character perfectly. He shows us what God has always been like. The humility of God is on full display in the Incarnation and the Cross.

This leads to a major point about prophecy. We often treat prophecy as a checklist of specific predictions. But the fathers argue that prophecy is a pattern. It is a shape that Jesus embodies. He fulfills all prophecies in a deeper sense than we usually imagine. For example, the prophet Ezekiel saw a valley of dry bones. That was a vision of Israel’s death and resurrection. Jesus embodies that. He goes through death and rises again. He is the resurrection of Israel. Similarly, the psalmist wrote that the Messiah reigns in the midst of His enemies. That points to the time between the first and second coming. And the prophet Jeremiah lamented over Israel’s faithlessness. Jesus laments over Jerusalem in the same way. He is living out the prophetic pattern.

The fathers also address a modern problem. There is a tendency to de-Judaize Christianity, to strip it of its Jewish roots. But that warps the faith. Christianity is a form of Judaism. It is the fulfillment of the Jewish story. To remove that is to misunderstand who Jesus is.

There is also a beautiful image they use regarding the veil. In the temple, the veil protected people from the direct presence of God. The fathers say Christ’s humanity is like that veil. It reveals God’s presence to us without destroying us. We see God in the face of Jesus, and we live.

So the confession of Saint Peter is not about a list of fulfilled predictions. It is about identity. Jesus is the Christ because He is Israel. He is the Torah. He is the God of Israel in the flesh. And when we see Him, we see the Father.
