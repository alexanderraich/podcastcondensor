This is the second part of the Lord of Spirits’ deep dive into the Fall of Man, and this time, Fathers Andrew and Stephen turn their attention to Cain. They make a bold argument right off the bat: while Adam’s disobedience brought death into the world, it is actually Cain, not Adam, who stands as the archetypal sinner in Scripture. When the Bible and the writings of the Second Temple period talk about sinfulness, they point to Cain. He is the one identified with the origin of sin, the one on the wrong side of judgment.

The episode walks through Cain’s story in detail, from his birth to his rejected sacrifice, and then to that famous warning from God in Genesis chapter four, verse seven. Sin is crouching at his door, and it desires to master him. The hosts dig into the Hebrew word for crouching here, which is *rovates*. It’s a fascinating term, because it’s actually derived from an Akkadian word for a demon that crawls up from the ground. So this isn’t just a poetic image of a wild animal waiting to pounce. It’s a picture of sin as a singular, demonic power, a personal force that seeks to dominate a person. This is a key point for them: sin is not just a list of individual offenses, or "sins." It is a power, a demonic presence that wants to master you.

Of course, Cain doesn’t master it. He murders his brother Abel, and in doing so, he becomes something. The hosts reference the first epistle of John, chapter three, verse twelve, which says Cain was "of the evil one." They are very careful to explain what that means. Cain was not literally the son of the serpent. They thoroughly refute that fringe theory, the so-called serpent seed idea. Cain was conceived naturally, the firstborn of Adam and Eve. He became a son of the devil through his actions. By imaging the devil through murder and wickedness, he underwent what the hosts call "demonosis." That’s their coined term for the opposite of theosis. Theosis is the process of becoming like God, the goal of the Christian life. Demonosis is the process of becoming like a demon, and Cain is its first great example. This echoes what Jesus tells his critics in John chapter eight, verse forty-four, that they are of their father the devil, who was a murderer from the beginning.

Then there’s the curse and the mark. Cain is cursed from the ground, which is a different curse than the one Adam received. Adam’s curse was on the ground itself, making it produce thorns. Cain’s curse is that the ground will no longer yield its strength to him at all. He becomes a wanderer, alienated from creation. The hosts bring in Philo of Alexandria here, who wrote about this deep alienation. And what about the mark of Cain? The hosts are clear that it is not a physical mark, and it certainly has nothing to do with skin color. The Hebrew word is *oat*, which means a pledge, a witness, or an oath. It was God’s pledge that He would avenge Cain, breaking the cycle of violence so that anyone who killed him would suffer sevenfold vengeance. And they note that all of Cain’s descendants perished in the flood, so the line ends there.

Cain then goes and founds a city, which the hosts connect to the ancient Sumerian city of Eridu, traditionally considered the first city. This is the foundation of the "city of man," built on sin and human pride, in contrast to the "city of God." They bring in St. Augustine here, who famously developed this paradigm. But they also bring in St. Irenaeus, who saw the events of Genesis four through nine, the story of Cain and the flood, as the main event of the fall, rather than the expulsion from Paradise. Different fathers emphasize different falls. The hosts discuss three distinct falls: the expulsion, the sin of Cain leading to the flood, and the Tower of Babel. Augustine emphasized the first, while Irenaeus focused on the second.

The hosts also bring in the first-century historian Josephus, who described Cain as a teacher of evil, introducing measures, weights, and boundaries to a humanity that had previously lived simply. And they touch on the ritual impurity of murder, using the Greek word *agos*. Murder, especially in a sacred context, incurs a permanent ritual impurity that cannot be removed, even though forgiveness and repentance are still possible. The episode closes with practical applications. We are called to self-mastery, to spiritual warfare against that crouching sin, and to live as part of a community, not as isolated individuals like Cain, who tried to build his own world apart from God.
