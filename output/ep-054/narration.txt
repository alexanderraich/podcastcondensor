In this episode of the Lord of Spirits, Fathers Andrew Stephen Damick and Stephen De Young begin a new series on the Holy Mysteries of the Orthodox Church, and they start with the sacrament of baptism. They open by looking back at the Old Testament practice of circumcision, and they argue that it was not just some kind of boundary marker or cultural badge. It was a foundational commandment, given to Abraham, that formed the family of God. It was a sign of faithfulness, intimately connected to obedience, and it set people apart for the Lord.

The fathers then tackle the New Testament controversies surrounding circumcision, especially in the book of Acts and in Galatians. They are careful to clarify that baptism does not simply replace circumcision. Rather, it fulfills and extends its meaning. Both are acts of faithfulness and obedience that bring a person into the family of God. They talk about the works of the law, which were those boundary markers of Jewish identity, like circumcision, dietary laws, and the Sabbath. Saint Paul’s point is that salvation is not based on these markers but on faithfulness to Christ, which is open to all nations.

They also spend time on the baptism of John the Baptist. To understand it, they explain the distinction between ritual and moral uncleanness in the Torah. Ritual uncleanness could be contracted through normal life events, like touching a dead body, and it was remedied by ritual washings that allowed a person to approach God. John’s baptism fits into this background of washings, but it was also something new. His ministry in the wilderness was an inversion of the expected religious order. He called people away from Jerusalem and the temple, which had become corrupt, and out to the Jordan River for purification and repentance. It was a direct critique of the religious establishment.

Then they move to Christian baptism itself. They draw on Saint Paul’s letters to the Corinthians and the Colossians, and also on the first epistle of Peter. Baptism is a participation in Christ’s death and resurrection. The believer is buried with Christ in the water and raised to new life. It is the death of the old self and the beginning of a life of faithfulness. They also describe baptism as a declaration of spiritual warfare. It involves renouncing the devil and being enlisted into the army of Christ. It is not a passive ritual but the start of a struggle against evil spiritual powers.

They also address a strange passage in First Corinthians, where Saint Paul mentions baptism for the dead. The fathers explain that this does not mean proxy baptism for the dead. Instead, it refers to the practice of taking a patron saint’s name at baptism. This was rooted in Roman patronage, where a person would take the name of a departed saint who then serves as a guide and intercessor. It is a way of honoring the saint and establishing a relationship with them.

Finally, they critique modern individualism. Many people see baptism as a personal transaction between themselves and God. But the fathers emphasize that baptism incorporates a person into a communal family, the Church. Salvation is pursued together with others. Individualism must die in the water. They connect this to the story of Noah, whose salvation through the flood is a type of baptism, and to the crossing of the Red Sea, which Saint Paul calls a baptism into Moses. They also mention Joshua, whose name is the same as Jesus, and who led the people across the Jordan into the Promised Land. Baptism is a new conquest, a new beginning, and the start of a life lived in the family of God.
