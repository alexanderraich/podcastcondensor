In this Halloween special, Fathers Andrew and Stephen take us on a deep dive into the origins and nature of demons, looking at how the ancient Greeks, Romans, and Jews understood these spirits, and how the early Church took those ideas and transformed them. They start with the Greek concept of the daemon. Now, in the ancient world, a daemon wasn’t necessarily evil. Originally, these were the spirits of deceased heroes, people who had been venerated at shrines and were believed to still influence the living. The poet Hesiod wrote about these beings, and even Socrates spoke of his own guiding spirit, his daimonion. The Romans had a similar idea with the genius, a guardian spirit tied to a person, a place, or even a whole people. The genius of the emperor was a big deal in the imperial cult. The key point here is that in paganism, having a guardian demon was seen as a positive good. It was a spirit that guided and protected you. This is a far cry from the Christian view of demons as purely evil.

Then the conversation shifts to the Second Temple Jewish perspective. This is where things get really interesting. In texts like the Book of Enoch and Jubilees, demons are identified as the disembodied spirits of the Nephilim. You remember the Nephilim from Genesis chapter six, where the sons of God saw the daughters of men and had children with them. These offspring were the giants, the mighty ones of old. The early Church Fathers, like Justin Martyr and Athenagoras, ran with this. They connected these Nephilim spirits to the Greek gods and Titans. They argued that the pagan myths were actually distorted accounts of the Watchers, the angels who descended and mated with women. So when the pagans worshipped Zeus or the other Olympians, they were really dealing with demons, the offspring of those fallen angels. The apologists even pointed to the Greek myth of Tartarus, the deepest abyss where the Titans were imprisoned. The Apostle Peter uses that exact same Greek word, Tartarus, in his second epistle to describe the prison of the fallen angels. It’s a direct connection between the pagan worldview and the Christian revelation.

But the episode doesn’t stop there. The Fathers also address a major shift in interpretation that came later. St. John Chrysostom and St. Augustine, for example, pushed back on the literal idea of angels having sex with women. They argued that angels are spiritual beings and can’t reproduce that way. Instead, they said the Nephilim were the result of spiritual corruption, not literal hybrids. Chrysostom taught that demons are not the souls of the violently dead, but rather the souls of wicked people who live in sin and imitate demonic behavior. Augustine’s arguments also contain pieces of the earlier view, but he rejected the literal reading. So you have this fascinating development where the Church Fathers are wrestling with the text and the tradition, trying to understand the nature of these spiritual beings.

Throughout all of this, the hosts stress the reality of the spiritual world. The Christian response to demonic influence isn’t fear, but reliance on God. The pagan guardian demon is replaced by the Christian guardian angel. We see this in the book of Tobit, where the Archangel Raphael guides and protects Tobias. And Jesus himself speaks of the angels of the little ones who always behold the Father’s face in Matthew chapter eighteen. This is the basis for our belief in guardian angels, which is reflected in our baptismal services and our prayers. So, the episode takes you on a journey from the pagan daemon to the Christian angel, showing how the early Church took the spiritual worldview of antiquity and filled it with new meaning, always pointing to the power of Christ over the forces of darkness.
