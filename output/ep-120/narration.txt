This is a special episode of the Lord of Spirits. With Father Andrew away, Father Stephen De Young takes the helm for a session he calls Storytime. And instead of his usual teaching, he reads two ancient texts from two very different civilizations. The first is the Memphite Theology from Egypt. The second is a Babylonian dialogue between a master and his servant.

Father Stephen begins with the Egyptian text. It’s an Old Kingdom document, but the version we have was inscribed on stone by Pharaoh Shabaka around 710 BC. At first glance, it looks like a myth about the gods. But Father Stephen explains that it’s really a theological and political argument. Its purpose was to unify Upper and Lower Egypt under the god Ptah. The text takes all the local deities and their stories and weaves them into one system, with Ptah at the center as the creator and ruler of everything.

According to the Memphite Theology, Ptah created all things through the thoughts of his heart and the words of his tongue. All the other gods are really just manifestations of his creative power. Even the great Ennead, the group of nine deities, came into being through his spoken word. Father Stephen points out that this is a very sophisticated way of doing theology. It doesn’t simply discard the old stories. It retells them with new emphasis. It reinterprets them to teach a deeper truth and to support a political reality. And he makes a striking observation. This is exactly the same method we see in the Old Testament. The biblical authors took familiar ancient Near Eastern stories, like the flood narrative, and retold them, often inverting their meaning, to teach the truth about the one God. So the Memphite Theology isn’t just a pagan curiosity. It’s a window into how ancient people actually thought about the divine.

Father Stephen also walks through some of the details of the text. There’s Geb, the earth god, who acts as a judge. He divides the land between Horus and Seth. Horus is given Lower Egypt, and Seth is given Upper Egypt. But then they are reconciled, and Horus becomes the ruler of the whole land. This is a direct reflection of the political unification of Egypt under Memphis. The city of Memphis becomes the seat of Ptah’s cult, and the theological story justifies that political reality. Father Stephen even uses the word hypostasis to describe how Ptah appears in different forms, like Ta-Tenen, as different manifestations of the same divine being.

Then Father Stephen turns to the second text. It’s a Babylonian dialogue, and it’s a dark comedy. A master and his servant are talking. The master proposes one thing, and the servant agrees. Then the master changes his mind and proposes the opposite, and the servant agrees again. This goes on and on. Should we have a feast? Yes, master. Actually, no, let’s not. Very wise, master. Should we go to court? Yes, master. No, let’s stay home. Absolutely, master. In the end, the master asks what is good in life. And the servant’s answer is bleak. Throw yourself into the river. Or perhaps, hang yourself. Nothing matters. It’s all absurd.

Father Stephen uses this text to make a modern point. The servant is the ultimate yes man. He offers constant affirmation, no matter what his master says. And the result is nihilism. When you surround yourself with people who always agree with you, when you never hear a word of criticism or correction, you lose the ability to tell good from bad, right from wrong. Everything becomes meaningless. The Babylonian dialogue is a satire, but it’s also a warning. Constant affirmation doesn’t lead to peace. It leads to despair. Father Stephen leaves the listener with that reflection, a sobering thought from an ancient text that still speaks to our own time.
