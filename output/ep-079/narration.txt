Fathers Andrew Stephen Damick and Stephen De Young open this episode by asking a deceptively simple question: what does stuff mean? They start with the very beginning, with Genesis, where God brings order out of chaos. But they’re careful to explain that this chaos isn’t a pile of raw material. It’s closer to non-being, to nothing at all. And the order God brings isn’t some external blueprint imposed on the world from the outside. That order is identified with God’s own Wisdom, with the Logos, who is Christ. This is rooted in Proverbs, chapter eight, where Wisdom is personified as the pattern of creation. And then John’s Gospel picks that up in the very first verse, telling us that the Word, the Logos, was with God and was God. Saint Paul says the same thing in Colossians, that in Christ all things hold together. So the pattern of the universe isn’t a set of rules or a cosmic template. It’s a person.

Now, this gets really interesting when they talk about humanity. We are made in the image of God, which means we are called to participate in this ordering work. We don’t just live in the world; we are meant to humanize it. We continue God’s work of filling and ordering creation. This is tied to theosis, the process of becoming like God by grace. And that’s not a solitary project. The ultimate meaning of life is found in becoming like Christ through active participation in community, in the Church. You can’t do this alone.

They contrast this with Plato, who thought of eternal forms as separate, abstract realities. The Christian understanding is different. The order is internal to God. It’s not a distant ideal. It’s the very life of the Trinity, and we are invited into it.

Then the conversation turns to how meaning works in our daily lives. They reject the idea that meaning is purely discovered, as if it’s just sitting there waiting for us to find it. And they also reject the idea that we just invent meaning out of thin air. Instead, meaning arises from relationships. It’s a communal, participatory process. Your identity, your very personhood, isn’t something you possess on your own. The Greek word for person is prosopon, which literally means face. A face is only fully known in relationship, in community. So meaning is both given and created, discovered and made, through our active and passive engagement with others and with God.

Finally, they apply all of this to how we read the Bible. The Church Fathers didn’t try to find one single, objective meaning in every text. They applied Scripture to their own communities, to their own specific problems and questions. Saint John Chrysostom’s homilies are perfect examples of this. He wasn’t writing a commentary for all time; he was speaking to his congregation in that moment. Moses did the same thing in Deuteronomy, chapters twelve through fourteen. He took the law given at Sinai and applied it to a new generation about to enter the Promised Land. The application is an instance of the pattern, not the pattern itself.

So what does this mean for us? It means we shouldn’t try to interpret everything on our own. We should trust the authority of bishops and spiritual fathers to apply the faith to our lives. They are the ones standing in that tradition of Moses and the Fathers. And then, we are called to creative, faithful engagement with the world. We don’t just passively receive meaning, and we don’t just make it up. We participate in God’s ordering work, trusting in His providence, and we let that shape how we read, how we live, and how we love.
